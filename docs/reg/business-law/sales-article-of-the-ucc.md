# Sales Article of the UCC

## Overview

**Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC)**, the Sales Article, governs contracts for the sale of **goods**: tangible, movable personal property. It does not cover services, real estate, or intangibles such as stocks, which remain under common law or other statutes. Article 2 was written to make trade easier, so it fills gaps and relaxes several strict common law rules, especially for **merchants**. This chapter follows a sale from formation through performance: forming the contract, the defenses that can block enforcement, delivery and risk of loss, warranties, remedies for breach, and the rule that protects buyers from merchants. The general contract rules it modifies are in [Contracts](./contracts.md).

When a contract mixes goods and services, courts apply the **predominant purpose test**. If the main purpose is to buy goods, Article 2 governs the whole contract; if it is to buy services, common law governs.

> **Example:** Panda Industries hires a contractor to supply and install a custom HVAC system. If the unit is the main thing Panda is paying for and installation is incidental, the UCC applies. If the job is mostly skilled labor with a modest amount of equipment, common law applies.

### Merchants

A **merchant** is a party who deals in goods of the kind sold or who holds itself out as having special knowledge or skill about them. Several Article 2 rules apply only to merchants:

| Rule | Merchant | Non-Merchant |
|---|---|---|
| **Good faith** | Honesty in fact **and** reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing | Honesty in fact |
| **Firm offer** | A signed written offer can be irrevocable without consideration | Does not apply |
| **Additional terms in an acceptance** | Become part of the contract between merchants, unless an exception applies | Are only proposals |
| **Written confirmation** | Can satisfy the Statute of Frauds against a merchant who receives it and does not object | Does not apply |
| **Implied warranty of merchantability** | Given by a merchant seller | Not given |

Bear Co., a dealer in office furniture, is a merchant when it sells desks. When it sells its old delivery van, it is likely not a merchant for that sale, because it does not deal in vehicles.

## Forming the Contract

The UCC lets a contract form whenever the parties show they intended one, even if some terms are missing.

### Open Terms

A contract for the sale of goods does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy. The UCC supplies missing terms:

- An open **price** becomes a reasonable price at the time of delivery.
- Open **delivery**, **place**, and **payment** terms are filled by UCC gap-fillers (for example, payment is due at the time and place the buyer receives the goods).
- **Quantity** is the one term that generally must be stated. **Output** and **requirements** contracts are the exception: the quantity is whatever the seller produces or the buyer needs, in good faith.

### Merchant's Firm Offer

At common law, an offer can be revoked at any time before acceptance unless the offeree paid to keep it open. Under the UCC, an offer is **irrevocable without consideration** if it is made by a **merchant**, in a **signed writing**, that **assures** the offer will be held open. It stays open for the time stated, or a reasonable time if none is stated, but never more than **3 months**.

> **Example:** Polar Co., a merchant, sends Bear Co. a signed letter promising to hold its offer open for 6 months. The offer is firm for only **3 months**. After that, Polar Co. may revoke it at any time before Bear Co. accepts.

### Acceptance and the Battle of the Forms

The UCC rejects the common law mirror image rule. Under **UCC §2-207**, a definite and timely expression of acceptance is an acceptance **even if it adds or changes terms**, unless it is expressly conditioned on the offeror's agreement to those terms. What happens to the additional terms depends on who the parties are:

- **Between merchants**, additional terms automatically become part of the contract **unless** (1) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (2) the new terms **materially alter** the contract, or (3) the offeror objects within a reasonable time.
- **If either party is not a merchant**, additional terms are only **proposals** and become part of the contract only if the offeror agrees.

> **Example:** Kodiak Inc. offers to sell 500 units of inventory to Salmon Entertainment at \$20 each. Salmon replies, "We accept; delivery to our loading dock by 8 a.m." Both are merchants, and the delivery-time term does not materially alter the deal, so it becomes part of the contract unless Kodiak objects within a reasonable time. A new term requiring arbitration of all disputes is more likely to be a material alteration that does not become part of the contract without Kodiak's agreement.

### Acceptance by Shipment

Unless the offer requires a particular method, a seller may accept an offer to buy goods for prompt shipment by **promising to ship** or by **promptly shipping**. Shipping **nonconforming goods** is both an acceptance and a breach, unless the seller notifies the buyer that the shipment is only an **accommodation**. In that case the shipment is a counteroffer, which the buyer may accept or reject.

### Modifications

A good-faith agreement to **modify** a contract for the sale of goods needs **no new consideration**, unlike at common law.

> **Example:** Grizzly Inc. agrees to buy 1,000 widgets from Panda Industries at \$10 each. A shortage of raw materials raises Panda's costs, and the parties agree to raise the price to \$12. The modification is enforceable without new consideration because it was made in good faith. (If the modified contract is for \$500 or more, it must also satisfy the Statute of Frauds.)

### Auctions

An auction is **with reserve** unless announced otherwise, meaning the auctioneer may withdraw the goods at any time before the hammer falls. In an auction **without reserve**, the goods cannot be withdrawn once bidding starts. Each bid is an offer, the fall of the hammer is the acceptance, and a bidder may retract a bid before the hammer falls.

## Defenses to Enforcement

### Statute of Frauds

A contract for the sale of goods for **\$500 or more** is enforceable only if there is a **writing** sufficient to show that a contract was made, **signed** by the party against whom enforcement is sought. The writing must state the **quantity**, and the contract is enforceable only up to that quantity. Other terms may be missing.

Four exceptions, remembered as **SWAP**, make an oral contract enforceable:

| Exception | Rule |
|---|---|
| **S**pecially manufactured goods | Goods made for the buyer that are not suitable for sale to others, once the seller has substantially begun making them or committed to buy them |
| **W**ritten confirmation between merchants | A confirmation sent within a reasonable time that is sufficient against the sender binds a merchant recipient who has reason to know its contents, unless the recipient objects in writing within **10 days** |
| **A**dmission | The party against whom enforcement is sought admits in court (in pleadings or testimony) that a contract was made, up to the quantity admitted |
| **P**erformance | Goods that have been **received and accepted**, or for which **payment** has been made and accepted, but only for that quantity |

> **Example:** Honey Security, a merchant, orally agrees to buy \$5,000 of surveillance cameras from Polar Co., also a merchant. Polar Co. sends a signed written confirmation. If Honey Security does not object in writing within **10 days**, the contract is enforceable against it, even though Honey Security signed nothing.

### Other Defenses

The general contract defenses, such as **fraud**, **duress**, and **mistake**, apply to sales of goods as well. Two rules are specific to Article 2:

- **Unconscionability.** A court may refuse to enforce a contract or clause that was grossly unfair when the contract was made.
- **Statute of limitations.** A suit for breach of a sales contract must be brought within **4 years** after the breach occurs, which for a breach of warranty is usually when the goods are delivered, whether or not the buyer knows of the breach. The parties may shorten the period to as little as **1 year** but may not lengthen it.

### Commercial Impracticability

A seller is excused from delivering if performance becomes **commercially impracticable** because of an event whose nonoccurrence was a basic assumption of the contract. The defense is narrow. An increase in cost alone, even a large one, is usually not enough, because sellers are expected to bear ordinary market risk.

> **Example:** A government embargo cuts off the raw materials Panda Industries needs to fill an order. Panda may be excused under impracticability. If the price of those materials merely doubles, Panda generally is **not** excused.

If an agreed method of delivery becomes unavailable, a **commercially reasonable substitute** must be offered and accepted if one exists.

## Delivery, Risk of Loss, and Title

### Delivery Terms

Unless the parties agree otherwise, the seller's basic duty is to make the goods available at the **seller's place of business** and notify the buyer. When a carrier is involved, the contract is either a shipment contract or a destination contract, and a **shipment contract is presumed**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Shipment contract** | The seller must deliver the goods to a carrier and arrange reasonable transportation |
| **Destination contract** | The seller must deliver the goods to a specified destination |
| **FOB shipping point** | A shipment contract |
| **FOB destination** | A destination contract |
| **FAS (free alongside ship)** | A shipment contract: the seller delivers the goods alongside the named vessel |
| **CIF (cost, insurance, and freight)** | A shipment contract, even though the seller pays the freight and insurance to the destination |

### Identification

**Identification** occurs when specific goods are designated as the goods the contract covers. It gives the buyer an **insurable interest** in them. Existing goods are identified when the contract is made, unless the parties agree otherwise. Future goods are identified when the seller ships, marks, or otherwise designates them, and crops and unborn animals when they are planted or conceived.

### Risk of Loss

**Risk of loss** is the question of who bears the loss if the goods are damaged or destroyed without anyone's fault. The parties can allocate it by agreement. If they do not, these rules apply:

| Situation | Risk Passes to the Buyer When |
|---|---|
| **Shipment contract** | The seller delivers the goods to the **carrier** |
| **Destination contract** | The goods are **tendered at the destination** |
| **No carrier, merchant seller** | The buyer **receives** the goods |
| **No carrier, non-merchant seller** | The seller **tenders** delivery (makes the goods available) |

> **Example:** Bear Co., a merchant, sells office furniture to a customer who will pick it up. Bear Co. calls to say the furniture is ready, but a fire destroys it overnight before the customer arrives. Bear Co. bears the loss, because a merchant seller keeps the risk until the buyer actually receives the goods.

A party that **breaches** may bear the risk of loss even when it would otherwise have passed:

- If the seller ships **nonconforming goods** that the buyer may reject, the risk stays with the seller until the seller cures the defect or the buyer accepts the goods.
- If the **buyer** breaches (for example, by repudiating) after conforming goods have been identified, the buyer bears the risk for a commercially reasonable time, to the extent the seller's insurance does not cover the loss.

> **Example:** Kodiak Inc. ships nonconforming goods to Grizzly Inc. under a shipment contract, and the goods are damaged in transit. Risk would normally pass to Grizzly at the carrier, but because Kodiak's shipment was nonconforming, Kodiak bears the loss.

### Sale on Approval and Sale or Return

| Feature | Sale on Approval | Sale or Return |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | The buyer takes the goods to **try them**, usually as a consumer | The buyer takes the goods to **resell** them, usually as a merchant |
| **Title and risk** | Stay with the **seller** until the buyer approves | Pass to the **buyer** on delivery |
| **Buyer's creditors** | Cannot reach the goods before approval | Can reach the goods while the buyer holds them |
| **Return** | At the seller's risk and expense | At the buyer's risk and expense |

### Passage of Title

Unless the parties agree otherwise, title passes when the seller **completes physical delivery**: at the carrier in a shipment contract, and at the destination in a destination contract. If the goods are not to be moved, title passes when the seller delivers a document of title or, if there is none, when the contract is made, provided the goods are identified.

## Warranties

### Express Warranties

An **express warranty** arises from an **affirmation of fact or promise** about the goods, a **description** of them, or a **sample or model**, if it is part of the **basis of the bargain**. The buyer does not have to prove reliance, and the seller need not use the words "warrant" or "guarantee." Statements of opinion or sales talk (**puffery**), such as "the best product on the market," do not create a warranty.

> **Example:** Salmon Entertainment's sales brochure says a sound system "produces 500 watts of power." That is an express warranty, and fine print stating "no warranties, express or implied" does not remove it.

### Implied Warranty of Title

In every sale, the seller warrants that it has **good title**, that the transfer is **rightful**, and that the goods are free of **liens or claims** the buyer does not know about. A **merchant** seller also warrants that the goods do not **infringe** a third party's patent, trademark, or copyright. This warranty can be disclaimed only by **specific language** or by circumstances that tell the buyer the seller does not claim full title, such as a sheriff's sale.

> **Example:** Bear Co. buys equipment from Polar Co. and later learns that a bank holds a security interest in it that Polar Co. never disclosed. Polar Co. has breached the warranty of title.

### Implied Warranty of Merchantability

Every sale by a **merchant** who deals in goods of the kind includes a warranty that the goods are **merchantable**. Merchantable goods are fit for the **ordinary purposes** for which such goods are used, are of **fair average quality** for goods of that description, would **pass without objection in the trade**, are adequately **packaged and labeled**, and conform to any statements on the label.

> **Example:** Panda Industries, a pump manufacturer, sells a pump that fails in normal use within weeks of delivery. Panda has breached the implied warranty of merchantability.

### Implied Warranty of Fitness for a Particular Purpose

This warranty arises when the seller has reason to know the buyer's **particular purpose**, the seller has reason to know the buyer is **relying on the seller's skill or judgment** to choose suitable goods, and the buyer does rely on it. **Any seller**, merchant or not, can give this warranty.

> **Example:** Honey Security tells Kodiak Inc., a chemical supplier, that it needs a solution that removes adhesive residue from camera lenses without harming their coatings, and Kodiak recommends Product X. If Product X damages the coatings, Kodiak has breached the warranty of fitness for a particular purpose, even if Product X works well for ordinary cleaning.

### Disclaimers

| Warranty | How to Disclaim It |
|---|---|
| **Express** | It effectively cannot be disclaimed; a disclaimer that contradicts an express warranty is ignored |
| **Title** | Only by specific language or circumstances showing the seller does not claim full title |
| **Merchantability** | The disclaimer must mention **"merchantability"** and, if written, must be **conspicuous** |
| **Fitness for a particular purpose** | The disclaimer must be **in writing** and **conspicuous** |
| **All implied warranties of quality** | Phrases such as **"as is"** or **"with all faults"** disclaim both merchantability and fitness |

An "as is" sale does **not** disclaim express warranties or the warranty of title.

## Remedies

### Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance

If a party clearly indicates **before performance is due** that it will not perform, the other party may wait a commercially reasonable time for performance, or treat the contract as breached and pursue any remedy, and in either case may suspend its own performance. The repudiating party may **retract** the repudiation until its performance is due, unless the other party has canceled, materially changed its position, or said it considers the repudiation final.

When **reasonable grounds for insecurity** arise about the other party's performance, a party may **demand adequate assurance in writing** and suspend its own performance until it receives it. Failure to provide adequate assurance within a reasonable time, not more than **30 days**, is a repudiation.

> **Example:** Grizzly Inc. has contracted to buy \$100,000 of inventory from Polar Co., then learns that Polar Co. has defaulted on payments to several suppliers. Grizzly may demand written assurance that Polar Co. will perform. If Polar Co. gives none within 30 days, Grizzly may treat the contract as repudiated.

### Seller's Remedies

When the **buyer** breaches or repudiates, the seller may:

| Remedy | Description |
|---|---|
| **Cancel** | End the contract and its unperformed obligations |
| **Withhold delivery** | Refuse to deliver goods not yet shipped |
| **Stop delivery in transit** | Stop goods in a carrier's possession if the buyer is **insolvent** (any size shipment), or, for any other breach, only for a carload, truckload, planeload, or larger shipment |
| **Resell and recover the difference** | Resell in a commercially reasonable manner and recover the contract price minus the resale price, plus incidental damages |
| **Recover damages for nonacceptance** | The contract price minus the market price at the time and place of tender, plus incidental damages, minus expenses saved |
| **Recover the price** | Available only if the buyer accepted the goods, if conforming goods were lost or damaged after the risk passed to the buyer, or if the seller cannot resell identified goods at a reasonable price |

### Buyer's Remedies

When the **seller** breaches or repudiates, the buyer may:

| Remedy | Description |
|---|---|
| **Reject nonconforming goods** | Under the **perfect tender rule**, if the goods fail to conform in **any** respect, the buyer may reject all of them, accept all of them, or accept any commercial units and reject the rest |
| **Revoke acceptance** | Possible only if the defect **substantially impairs** the goods' value and the buyer accepted either expecting a cure that never came or without discovering a defect that was hard to find or concealed by the seller's assurances |
| **Cover** | Buy substitute goods in good faith and without unreasonable delay, and recover the cover price minus the contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages |
| **Recover damages for nondelivery** | The market price when the buyer learned of the breach minus the contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages |
| **Recover damages for accepted goods** | For accepted goods that are defective, the loss resulting in the ordinary course from the breach, such as the difference in value |
| **Specific performance or replevin** | Specific performance when the goods are **unique**; replevin of identified goods when the buyer cannot cover |

The perfect tender rule is softened by the seller's **right to cure**. If the buyer rejects nonconforming goods before the time for performance has expired, the seller may notify the buyer and deliver conforming goods within the contract period.

:::note[Perfect Tender Versus Substantial Performance]

Under common law, substantial performance satisfies a contract. Under the UCC, a buyer may reject goods that fail to conform in **any** respect, subject to the seller's right to cure. This contrast is a favorite exam question.

:::

### Limits on Damages

Both parties must take reasonable steps to **mitigate** their losses. The parties may agree in advance to **liquidated damages** in an amount that is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual harm; an unreasonably large amount is void as a penalty. The UCC remedies are **compensatory**: they aim to put the injured party in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed, and **punitive damages** are not available for breach alone.

## Entrusting

**Entrusting** means handing goods to a **merchant who deals in goods of that kind**, for any reason, such as repair. Under **UCC §2-403**, the merchant then has the power to transfer **all of the entruster's rights** to a **buyer in the ordinary course of business**, even though the merchant had no right to sell.

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Entruster** | The owner delivers goods to a merchant |
| **Merchant** | Deals in goods of that kind |
| **Buyer in the ordinary course** | Buys in good faith, without knowing the sale violates the owner's rights, from the merchant's regular stock |
| **Effect** | The buyer takes the entruster's title; the owner's remedy is against the merchant |

> **Example:** Salmon Entertainment leaves a vintage guitar at a music store that both repairs and sells guitars. The store mistakenly sells the guitar to a customer who buys it in good faith. The customer gets good title, and Salmon Entertainment's remedy is a claim against the store.

The rule protects only a buyer in the ordinary course. A buyer who knows the merchant has no right to sell does not qualify. Neither does a buyer from a merchant who does not deal in goods of that kind, such as a customer who buys a car from a repair shop that does not sell cars.

## Summary

| Topic | Key Rule |
|---|---|
| Scope | Sales of goods; predominant purpose test for mixed contracts |
| Merchant rules | Firm offers, additional terms, written confirmations, merchantability |
| Open terms | Contract can form without price or delivery terms; quantity is generally required |
| Firm offer | Merchant, signed writing, assurance; at most 3 months |
| Battle of the forms | Additional terms become part of a contract between merchants unless an exception applies |
| Modification | No new consideration needed if made in good faith |
| Statute of Frauds | \$500 or more; SWAP exceptions |
| Statute of limitations | 4 years from breach; may be shortened to 1 year |
| Risk of loss | Shipment: at carrier; destination: on tender there; no carrier: merchant on receipt, non-merchant on tender |
| Warranties | Express, title, merchantability (merchants), fitness (any seller) |

| Entrusting | Buyer in the ordinary course takes the entruster's title |
