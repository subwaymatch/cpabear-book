# Government Regulation of Business

## Introduction

Federal law touches almost every business, but the REG blueprint limits this topic to five areas: **employment taxes**, **worker classification**, **qualified health plans** under the Affordable Care Act, **bankruptcy**, and **anti-bribery** rules under the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. These are the federal rules a CPA meets most often when advising a business client or preparing its returns. This chapter covers employment taxes first, then the closely related question of who counts as an employee, then the Affordable Care Act and the FCPA. Bankruptcy is covered in [Debtor-Creditor Relationships](./debtor-creditor-relationships.md#bankruptcy).

## Employment Taxes

An employer has three jobs with respect to its employees' pay: withhold taxes from each paycheck, pay its own share of payroll taxes, and report and deposit everything on time.

### FICA

The **Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA)** funds Social Security and Medicare. The employee and the employer each pay the same amount:

| Tax | Rate (employee and employer each) | Wages Subject to Tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Social Security** | **6.2%** | Wages up to the annual wage base, which is indexed (\$184,500 for 2026) |
| **Medicare** | **1.45%** | All wages, with no cap |
| **Additional Medicare tax** | **0.9%**, employee only | Wages above \$200,000 (\$250,000 on a joint return); the employer withholds it once an employee's wages pass \$200,000 |

The employer **withholds** the employee's share, along with federal income tax, and pays its own matching share. It reports these amounts quarterly on **Form 941** and gives each employee a **Form W-2** by January 31. The employer's share is a deductible business expense; the employee's share is not deductible by the employee. An employee who works for two employers can have too much Social Security tax withheld in total; the excess is claimed as a credit on the employee's income tax return.

> **Example:** Bear Co. pays an employee a \$100,000 salary in 2026. Bear Co. withholds \$6,200 of Social Security tax (6.2%) and \$1,450 of Medicare tax (1.45%) from her pay, and pays a matching \$7,650 of its own. No additional Medicare tax applies because her wages do not exceed \$200,000.

**Tips and overtime** remain fully subject to FICA and to income tax withholding. The new deductions for qualified tips and qualified overtime that took effect in 2025 reduce only the employee's **income tax**. Starting with 2026 wages, employers report qualified tips and qualified overtime separately on Form W-2. Employees must report tips of \$20 or more a month to their employer, and employers in food and beverage service, and now beauty services, can claim a business credit for their share of FICA on certain tips.

### FUTA

The **Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA)** tax is paid **only by the employer**. The rate is **6.0%** on the first **\$7,000** of each employee's wages each year. Employers receive a credit of up to **5.4%** for state unemployment taxes paid, so the usual net rate is **0.6%**, or \$42 per employee. FUTA is reported annually on **Form 940**.

### Self-Employment Tax

Self-employed people pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare themselves, as **self-employment tax** of 15.3% on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings. They deduct half of it in computing AGI. The full computation is in [Other Individual Taxes](../taxation-of-individuals/other-individual-taxes.md).

### The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty

The income tax and employee FICA an employer withholds are held **in trust** for the government. If they are not paid over, the IRS can assess a penalty equal to **100%** of the unpaid trust fund taxes against any **responsible person** (such as an owner, officer, or bookkeeper with authority over payments) who **willfully** failed to pay them. The penalty is personal, so it can be collected from the individual even if the business has failed.

## Worker Classification

### Why It Matters

For an **employee**, the business must withhold income tax and FICA, pay the employer's share of FICA and FUTA, and issue a Form W-2. For an **independent contractor**, it does none of these. It simply reports the payments on **Form 1099-NEC** once they reach the reporting threshold, which rose from \$600 to **\$2,000** for payments made after 2025 (and is indexed after 2026). The contractor pays self-employment tax. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can leave the business owing the employment taxes it should have withheld and paid, plus penalties and interest.

### The Common Law Control Test

For employment taxes, the IRS asks whether the business has the **right to control** what work is done **and how** it is done. A business that controls only the result is dealing with an independent contractor. The IRS groups the evidence into three categories:

| Category | Points Toward an Employee | Points Toward a Contractor |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavioral control** | The business gives detailed instructions on how, when, and where to work, and trains the worker in its methods | The worker decides how to do the job |
| **Financial control** | The business pays regular wages, reimburses expenses, and provides the tools; the worker has no real chance of profit or loss | The worker invests in their own equipment, pays their own expenses, can make or lose money, and offers services to the public |
| **Relationship of the parties** | Employee benefits, an indefinite relationship, work that is a key part of the regular business | A written contract for a specific project, no benefits, a set end date |

No single factor decides the question, and a contract that calls the worker an "independent contractor" does not control if the facts show otherwise. A business or worker can ask the IRS for a determination on **Form SS-8**.

> **Example:** Kodiak Partners hires a bookkeeper who sets her own hours, uses her own computer and software, works for several other clients, and bills a flat fee each month. Kodiak tells her what reports it needs but not how to prepare them. The evidence points to an **independent contractor**. If Kodiak instead set her hours, gave her a desk and company software, and paid her an hourly wage with benefits, she would likely be an **employee**.

For minimum wage and overtime purposes, the **Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)** uses a separate "economic reality" test, and the Department of Labor's rule interpreting it has been revised several times in recent years. The IRS common law test for employment taxes has not changed. Agency law draws the same employee and contractor line for tort liability, as described in [Agency](./agency.md#employees-and-independent-contractors).

## The Affordable Care Act

The **Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA)** shapes health coverage through employer requirements, individual tax credits, and a small-employer credit.

### The Employer Mandate

An **applicable large employer (ALE)**, one with **50 or more** full-time employees (including full-time equivalents) in the prior year, must offer health coverage to its full-time employees (those averaging **30 or more** hours a week) or risk a payment under the employer shared responsibility rules (§4980H). The payment is triggered only when at least one full-time employee buys coverage on an exchange and receives a premium tax credit. There are two versions:

- **No offer of coverage:** if the ALE fails to offer minimum essential coverage to at least 95% of its full-time employees and their dependents, it owes an annual amount for each full-time employee, minus the first 30.
- **Coverage that is unaffordable or below minimum value:** if the ALE offers coverage, but it is not affordable or does not provide minimum value, it owes an amount for each full-time employee who receives a premium tax credit.

The dollar amounts are indexed each year. ALEs report their offers of coverage to the IRS and employees on **Forms 1094-C and 1095-C**. The IRS has **6 years** after the due date of those forms (or the date they were filed, if later) to assess a §4980H payment, and an employer has at least **90 days** to respond to the IRS's first letter proposing one.

Individuals no longer face a federal penalty for going without coverage. The individual shared responsibility payment was reduced to zero beginning in 2019.

### The Premium Tax Credit

The **premium tax credit (§36B)** is a **refundable** credit that helps individuals and families with household income of at least 100% of the federal poverty line pay for coverage bought on an exchange. A person who is offered affordable employer coverage that provides minimum value is not eligible. Most people receive the credit in advance, paid directly to the insurer, and reconcile it on **Form 8962** when they file.

Several changes now apply:

- **Income ceiling.** The temporary expansion that removed the 400% of poverty ceiling expired after 2025. For 2026, the credit is again generally limited to households with income up to 400% of the poverty line.
- **Repayment.** Beginning in 2026, OBBBA removed the cap on repaying **excess advance payments**, so a taxpayer whose income turns out higher than estimated repays the full excess.
- **Eligibility.** Beginning in 2026, no credit is allowed for coverage bought through an income-based special enrollment period, or for certain lawfully present immigrants with income below the poverty line. Further OBBBA limits on which immigrants qualify begin in 2027 and are not expected to be eligible for testing until July 1, 2027.

### The Small Business Health Care Credit

A small employer with **fewer than 25** full-time equivalent employees and low average wages that pays at least half of its employees' premiums for coverage bought through the Small Business Health Options Program (SHOP) can claim a credit of up to **50%** of its premium contributions (35% for tax-exempt employers). The credit is available for two consecutive years.

## Foreign Corrupt Practices Act

The **Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 (FCPA)** has two parts.

### Anti-Bribery Provisions

U.S. companies, their officers, employees, and agents, and anyone acting in the United States may not **corruptly** pay, offer, or promise **anything of value** to a **foreign official**, a foreign political party, or a candidate for foreign office to obtain or keep business or to win **preferential treatment** from a foreign government. The prohibition covers cash, gifts, travel, and entertainment, and it reaches payments made through intermediaries when the payer knows some of the money will be passed on as a bribe.

There is one narrow exception and two defenses:

- **Facilitating payments** (sometimes called grease payments) to speed up **routine governmental actions**, such as processing a visa or connecting utilities, are not bribes. A payment to win a contract or a favorable decision never qualifies.
- It is a defense that the payment was **lawful under the written laws** of the foreign country.
- It is a defense that the payment was a **reasonable and bona fide expenditure**, such as travel and lodging, directly related to promoting products or performing a contract.

### Accounting Provisions

Companies with securities registered with the SEC (**issuers**) must keep **books and records** that accurately and fairly reflect their transactions in reasonable detail, and must maintain a system of **internal accounting controls** that provides reasonable assurance that transactions are authorized and properly recorded. These provisions apply to every issuer, whether or not it does business abroad.

Violations can bring criminal fines, civil penalties, and imprisonment for individuals. The statute has not changed recently, although the Justice Department narrowed its enforcement priorities in 2025. That shift affects which cases are brought, not what the law prohibits.

> **Example:** Honey Security, a publicly traded U.S. company, pays \$100,000 to a foreign defense official to win a government contract and records the payment as a "consulting fee." The payment violates the anti-bribery provisions, and mislabeling it violates the books and records provisions. A \$50 payment to a clerk to process a routine customs form on time would likely be a permitted facilitating payment.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

On FCPA questions, ask what the payment was for. A payment to **obtain or keep business** or to win preferential treatment is prohibited, no matter how small. A payment only to speed up a **routine, nondiscretionary** action the official must perform anyway is a facilitating payment.

:::

## Summary

| Topic | Key Rule |
|---|---|
| FICA | 6.2% Social Security (up to the wage base) plus 1.45% Medicare, paid by both employee and employer |
| Additional Medicare tax | 0.9% on wages over \$200,000; employee only |
| FUTA | Employer only; 6.0% on the first \$7,000, usually 0.6% after the state credit |
| Tips and overtime | Still subject to FICA; the new deductions affect income tax only |
| Trust fund recovery penalty | 100% of unpaid withheld taxes, assessed against responsible persons |
| Worker classification | Common law right-to-control test: behavioral, financial, relationship |
| Form 1099-NEC threshold | \$2,000 for payments made after 2025 |
| Employer mandate | ALEs (50 or more full-time employees) must offer affordable coverage or face §4980H payments |
| Premium tax credit | Refundable; reconciled on Form 8962; no repayment cap from 2026 |
| FCPA anti-bribery | No corrupt payments to foreign officials to obtain business; facilitating payment exception |
| FCPA accounting | Issuers must keep accurate books and adequate internal controls |
