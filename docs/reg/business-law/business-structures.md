# Business Structures

## Introduction

The legal form of a business decides who is liable for its debts, who runs it, how ownership can change hands, how the business ends, and how it is taxed. The REG blueprint asks you to summarize how each common form is formed, operated, and terminated, and to identify the rights, duties, and authority of owners and managers in a given scenario. This chapter moves from the simplest form to the most formal: sole proprietorships, general partnerships, limited partnerships, LLCs and LLPs, and corporations. It ends with the fiduciary duties of managers and a side-by-side comparison. The tax rules for each entity are covered in the Taxation of Entities chapters.

---

## Sole Proprietorship

A **sole proprietorship** is one individual doing business without forming a separate legal entity. Nothing needs to be filed to create one, although a business that uses a name other than the owner's may need to register an assumed name ("doing business as"). The owner makes every decision, keeps every dollar of profit, and has **unlimited personal liability** for every business debt. Profits are reported on the owner's Form 1040 (Schedule C) and are subject to self-employment tax. The business ends when the owner stops it or dies.

> **Example:** A CPA runs a small tax practice as a sole proprietorship. All of its income flows to her personal return, and her personal assets are exposed to any malpractice claim against the practice.

## General Partnership

### Formation and Operation

A **general partnership** is an association of two or more persons carrying on a business as co-owners for profit. It is governed by the partnership agreement and, where the agreement is silent, by the state's partnership act, usually based on the **Revised Uniform Partnership Act (RUPA)**. No filing is required: a partnership can be formed by written or oral agreement, or simply by conduct, such as sharing the profits of a business.

Unless the partners agree otherwise, RUPA supplies these default rules:

| Topic | Default Rule |
|---|---|
| **Profits and losses** | Shared **equally**, regardless of capital contributed; losses are shared the same way as profits |
| **Management** | Each partner has an **equal** vote. Ordinary matters are decided by a **majority**; matters outside the ordinary course and amendments to the agreement need **unanimous** consent |
| **Compensation** | No salary for partners, except for winding up the business |
| **Authority** | Each partner is an **agent** of the partnership and can bind it in the ordinary course of business |
| **Information** | Each partner may inspect the books and records |
| **Transfer of interest** | A partner may transfer the right to receive distributions (the **transferable interest**), but the transferee does not become a partner or gain management rights without the consent of all partners |

### Liability

All partners are **jointly and severally liable** for partnership obligations, with **unlimited personal liability**. Under RUPA, a creditor generally must first pursue the partnership's assets before going after a partner's personal assets. A person who joins an existing partnership is not personally liable for debts incurred before joining, although the new partner's capital contribution is at risk.

> **Example:** Grizzly Partners has three partners who share profits equally. One partner, acting in the ordinary course of business, signs a lease for the partnership. The partnership is bound, and if its assets are not enough, all three partners are personally liable for the lease, even though two of them never approved it.

### Dissociation and Dissolution

A partner **dissociates** by withdrawing, dying, being expelled, or going bankrupt. Under RUPA, dissociation does not automatically end the partnership. In many cases the partnership simply buys out the departing partner's interest and continues.

The partnership itself **dissolves** when, for example, a partner of an at-will partnership gives notice of withdrawal, the term or purpose of the partnership ends, all partners agree, the business becomes illegal, or a court orders it. Dissolution starts **winding up**: the partners finish existing business, sell assets, and distribute the proceeds, first to **creditors** (including partners who lent money to the partnership) and then to the partners according to their capital accounts.

## Limited Partnership

A **limited partnership (LP)** has at least one **general partner**, who manages the business and has unlimited liability, and at least one **limited partner**, who invests but does not manage. An LP exists only after it files a **certificate of limited partnership** with the state.

| Feature | General Partner | Limited Partner |
|---|---|---|
| **Liability** | Unlimited personal liability | Limited to the partner's capital contribution |
| **Management** | Full management authority | No management role; cannot bind the LP |
| **Withdrawal** | May dissolve the LP unless the remaining partners continue it | Generally does not dissolve the LP |

Under the **Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act (RULPA)**, a limited partner who **participates in control** of the business can lose limited liability to third parties who reasonably believed the limited partner was a general partner. Newer statutes based on the 2001 uniform act remove that risk, but the control rule still appears on the exam.

## Limited Liability Company and Limited Liability Partnership

### LLC

A **limited liability company (LLC)** combines the liability shield of a corporation with the flexibility of a partnership. It is formed by filing **articles of organization** (often called a certificate of formation) with the state. Its internal affairs are governed by an **operating agreement**.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Liability** | Members are not personally liable for the LLC's debts |
| **Management** | **Member-managed** (every member manages and can bind the LLC) or **manager-managed** (only the designated managers manage); member-managed is the usual default |
| **Fiduciary duties** | Owed by the members in a member-managed LLC, and by the managers in a manager-managed LLC |
| **Transfer of interest** | A member may transfer economic rights; the transferee becomes a member only with the other members' consent, unless the operating agreement says otherwise |
| **Taxation** | A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default, and a single-member LLC is disregarded; either may elect to be taxed as a corporation |
| **Termination** | Dissolves as the operating agreement or statute provides; after winding up, it files articles of dissolution |

> **Example:** Three friends form Salmon Entertainment LLC, and their operating agreement names two of them as managers. All three have limited liability. Only the two managers can bind the LLC, and they owe the LLC fiduciary duties. Unless the LLC elects otherwise, it is taxed as a partnership and each member reports a share of income from Schedule K-1.

### LLP

A **limited liability partnership (LLP)** is a general partnership that has registered with the state as an LLP. It is popular with accounting and law firms. An LLP partner is **not** personally liable for the negligence or misconduct of **other** partners or of employees the partner does not supervise. Each partner remains liable for the partner's **own** negligence and for the work of those the partner directly supervises, and the firm's assets remain exposed. In many states, an LLP shield covers all partnership obligations, including contract debts.

> **Example:** Kodiak Partners LLP is an accounting firm. One partner commits malpractice on an audit. The firm's assets and that partner's personal assets are exposed, but the other partners are **not** personally liable for the malpractice.

## Corporations

### Formation

A corporation is formed by one or more **incorporators** who file **articles of incorporation** with the state. Under the **Revised Model Business Corporation Act (RMBCA)**, the articles must state:

- The corporate name, including "Corporation," "Incorporated," "Company," "Limited," or an abbreviation
- The number of shares the corporation is authorized to issue
- The registered office and registered agent
- The name and address of each incorporator

The corporation exists once the articles are filed. The board then adopts **bylaws**, which govern internal procedures, and elects officers.

People often sign contracts for a corporation before it exists. A **promoter** who does so is personally liable on those contracts. The corporation becomes liable only if it **adopts** the contract after it is formed, and the promoter remains liable unless the other party agrees to a **novation** releasing the promoter.

### Owners and Managers

| Role | Rights and Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Shareholders** | Elect and remove directors; vote on fundamental changes (mergers, amendments to the articles, sale of substantially all assets, dissolution); receive dividends once declared; inspect corporate records for a proper purpose; sue on the corporation's behalf in a **derivative suit** when the board will not. Shareholders do **not** manage the business |
| **Board of directors** | Sets policy, appoints and removes officers, declares dividends, and approves major transactions. Directors act as a body at meetings with a quorum, or by unanimous written consent |
| **Officers** | Run day-to-day operations as agents of the corporation (CEO, CFO, secretary, and others) |

A shareholder has a **preemptive right** to buy a share of new stock issues only if the articles provide one. A shareholder who dissents from a merger or similar fundamental change may have **appraisal rights**, the right to be paid the fair value of the shares in cash.

### Limited Liability and Piercing the Veil

Shareholders are generally liable only to the extent of their investment. A court may **pierce the corporate veil** and hold shareholders personally liable when the corporation is a mere **alter ego** of its owners, when corporate formalities are ignored and funds are commingled, when the corporation was **inadequately capitalized** from the start, or when the corporate form is used to commit **fraud**.

> **Example:** The sole shareholder of Cub Records pays personal expenses from the corporate bank account, holds no board meetings, and keeps no minutes. A court may pierce the veil and hold the shareholder personally liable for Cub Records' debts.

### C and S Corporations

Under state law, an S corporation is an ordinary corporation. The difference is a federal tax election.

| Feature | C Corporation | S Corporation |
|---|---|---|
| **Taxation** | The corporation pays tax on its income, and shareholders pay tax again on dividends | Income and losses pass through to shareholders (Form 1120-S, Schedule K-1) |
| **Shareholders** | Any number; any type of owner | At most **100**; only individuals who are U.S. citizens or residents, estates, certain trusts, and certain tax-exempt organizations |
| **Classes of stock** | Any number | **One** class (differences in voting rights are allowed) |

To become an S corporation, the corporation files **Form 2553**, and **all** shareholders must consent. The detailed rules are in the [S Corporation](../taxation-of-entities/s-corporation.md) chapter.

### Dissolution

A corporation can end in three ways:

- **Voluntary dissolution:** the board recommends dissolution, the shareholders approve it, and the corporation files **articles of dissolution**.
- **Administrative dissolution:** the state dissolves a corporation that fails to file annual reports, pay fees, or keep a registered agent.
- **Judicial dissolution:** a court orders dissolution, for example when directors are deadlocked, those in control act illegally or fraudulently, or corporate assets are being wasted.

After dissolution, the corporation **winds up**: it collects its assets, pays or provides for its **creditors**, and distributes what remains to its **shareholders**.

## Fiduciary Duties of Managers

Directors, officers, general partners, and managers of an LLC owe fiduciary duties to the entity and its owners.

| Duty | Description |
|---|---|
| **Care** | Act with the care an ordinarily prudent person in a similar position would use, and make informed decisions |
| **Loyalty** | Put the entity's interests first; avoid self-dealing, conflicts of interest, and taking business opportunities that belong to the entity |
| **Good faith** | Act honestly, and not in conscious disregard of one's duties |

The **business judgment rule** protects directors and officers from liability for decisions made in good faith, on an informed basis, and in the honest belief that the action was in the corporation's best interest, even if the decision turns out badly. It does **not** protect decisions tainted by a conflict of interest or made in bad faith.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

When a question asks whether a director is liable for a decision that lost money, apply the business judgment rule first. Liability usually requires a conflict of interest, bad faith, or an uninformed decision, not just a bad outcome.

:::

## Comparison of Business Structures

| Feature | Sole Proprietorship | General Partnership | Limited Partnership | LLC | LLP | C Corp | S Corp |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Formation** | None | No filing required | State filing | State filing | State filing | State filing | State filing plus IRS election |
| **Owners' liability** | Unlimited | Unlimited (joint and several) | GP: unlimited; LP: limited | Limited | Limited (own acts excepted) | Limited | Limited |
| **Taxation** | Owner's return | Pass-through | Pass-through | Flexible | Pass-through | Entity-level tax | Pass-through |
| **Management** | Owner | All partners equally | General partners | Members or managers | All partners | Board and officers | Board and officers |
| **Maximum owners** | 1 | No limit | No limit | No limit | No limit | No limit | 100 |
| **Transfer of full ownership rights** | N/A | Requires consent | Requires consent | Requires consent | Requires consent | Shares freely transferable | Transferable, but eligibility rules apply |

## Summary

| Topic | Key Rule |
|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | No filing; unlimited liability; Schedule C |
| General partnership | No filing; equal management and profit sharing by default; joint and several liability |
| Partnership dissolution | Dissociation need not dissolve; winding up pays creditors first, then partners |
| Limited partnership | Certificate required; limited partners may lose protection by participating in control (RULPA) |
| LLC | Articles of organization; limited liability; member- or manager-managed |
| LLP | Shields partners from other partners' malpractice |
| Corporation formation | Articles filed by incorporators; promoter liable on pre-incorporation contracts unless novation |
| Shareholder rights | Vote, dividends when declared, inspection, derivative suits, appraisal rights |
| Piercing the veil | Alter ego, commingling, undercapitalization, fraud |
| Corporate dissolution | Voluntary, administrative, or judicial; creditors paid before shareholders |
| Fiduciary duties | Care, loyalty, good faith; business judgment rule |
