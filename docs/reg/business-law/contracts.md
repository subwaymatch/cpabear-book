# Contracts

## Introduction

A **contract** is a promise, or set of promises, that the law will enforce. Contracts are the backbone of business law, and on the exam they come up in two forms: a scenario that asks whether a valid contract was ever formed, and a scenario that asks whether a party performed, was excused, or owes a remedy. This chapter follows the life of a contract in that order: formation, defenses, the Statute of Frauds and the parol evidence rule, third-party rights, performance and discharge, and remedies for breach.

Two bodies of law govern contracts. **Common law** (judge-made law) governs contracts for services, real estate, and employment. **Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC)** governs contracts for the sale of goods and changes several common law rules. This chapter teaches the common law and notes the main UCC differences; the [Sales Article of the UCC](./sales-article-of-the-ucc.md) chapter covers Article 2 in full.

---

## Types of Contracts

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Bilateral** | A promise exchanged for a promise; the most common type |
| **Unilateral** | A promise exchanged for an act; formed only when the act is performed |
| **Express** | Terms stated in words, orally or in writing |
| **Implied in fact** | Terms inferred from the parties' conduct |
| **Quasi-contract (implied in law)** | Not a true contract; a court imposes an obligation to pay for a benefit received, to prevent unjust enrichment |

Courts also describe contracts by their legal effect. A **valid** contract is fully enforceable. A **void** contract has no legal effect at all, such as an agreement to commit a crime. A **voidable** contract is valid, but one party may choose to cancel it, such as a contract signed by a minor. An **unenforceable** contract is valid but cannot be enforced in court, such as an oral contract that the Statute of Frauds requires to be in writing.

## Formation

A valid contract requires **mutual assent** (an offer and an acceptance), **consideration**, **capacity**, and **legality**.

### Offer

An offer must be made with **intent to be bound** (judged objectively), must be **definite** in its terms, and must be **communicated** to the offeree. Advertisements, price lists, and catalogs are generally invitations to negotiate, not offers.

> **Example:** Polar Co. writes to Kodiak Inc.: "We will sell you 1,000 widgets at \$10 each, delivery within 30 days." This is an offer. It identifies the parties, the subject matter, the quantity, and the price, and it shows intent to be bound.

An offer ends in any of these ways:

- **Revocation** by the offeror before acceptance. An offeror may generally revoke at any time, even after promising to keep the offer open, unless the offeree paid for that promise (an **option contract**) or the UCC's merchant firm offer rule applies.
- **Rejection** or a **counteroffer** by the offeree
- **Lapse** of the time stated in the offer, or of a reasonable time if none is stated
- **Death or incapacity** of either party, or destruction of the subject matter, before acceptance

### Acceptance

Under the common law **mirror image rule**, an acceptance must match the offer exactly. A response that changes the terms is a **counteroffer**, which rejects the original offer. (The UCC relaxes this rule for sales of goods.)

Under the **mailbox rule**, an acceptance sent by an authorized means is effective when it is **dispatched**, not when it arrives. Every other communication (offers, revocations, rejections, and counteroffers) is effective only when **received**.

> **Example:** On May 1, Grizzly Inc. mails an acceptance of Panda Industries' offer. On May 2, before the letter arrives, Panda phones to revoke the offer. A contract was formed on May 1, because the acceptance took effect when it was mailed and the revocation came too late.

### Consideration

**Consideration** is something of legal value bargained for in exchange: a legal detriment to the promisee or a legal benefit to the promisor. Courts do not ask whether the exchange was fair, only whether there was one. Two things are **not** consideration:

- **Past consideration:** a promise to pay for something already done.
- **A preexisting duty:** a promise to do what one is already obligated to do. Under common law, a modification that increases one party's price without any new duty for the other party is unenforceable. Under the UCC, a good-faith modification of a contract for the sale of goods needs no new consideration.

A promise can be enforced without consideration under **promissory estoppel**, when the promisor should expect the promisee to rely on it, the promisee does rely on it to their detriment, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcing it.

### Capacity and Legality

**Minors** may disaffirm (cancel) most contracts until shortly after they reach the age of majority; the contract is voidable at the minor's option. Minors remain liable for the reasonable value of **necessaries**, such as food and shelter. People who are **mentally incompetent** or **intoxicated** when they contract may also be able to avoid the contract. A contract whose purpose is **illegal** is void.

## Defenses Based on Defective Assent

Even when an offer was accepted, a party may be able to avoid the contract if the agreement was not genuine.

| Defense | Description | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraud in the inducement** | A knowing misrepresentation of a material fact, intended to induce reliance, on which the other party justifiably relied | Voidable by the defrauded party |
| **Innocent misrepresentation** | A material misstatement made without knowledge that it was false | Voidable (rescission, but no damages) |
| **Mutual mistake** | Both parties are mistaken about a basic fact of the deal | Voidable by the adversely affected party |
| **Unilateral mistake** | Only one party is mistaken | Generally binding, unless the other party knew or should have known of the mistake |
| **Duress** | Agreement obtained by an improper threat | Voidable (void if physical force is used) |
| **Undue influence** | Unfair persuasion by someone in a position of trust or dominance | Voidable |

## The Statute of Frauds

Most contracts may be oral. The **Statute of Frauds** lists the exceptions: contracts that are unenforceable unless evidenced by a **writing signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought**. A common mnemonic is **MY LEGS**:

| Letter | Category | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **M** | Marriage | Promises made in consideration of marriage, such as a prenuptial agreement |
| **Y** | Year | Contracts that **cannot** be performed within one year from the date they are made |
| **L** | Land | Contracts to sell or transfer an interest in real property |
| **E** | Executor | An executor's promise to pay the estate's debts from the executor's own funds |
| **G** | Goods | Sales of goods for **\$500 or more** (UCC §2-201) |
| **S** | Surety | A promise to pay the debt of another person |

The one-year rule applies only if performance within one year is **impossible** under the contract's terms. If the contract could possibly be completed within a year, it falls outside the rule.

> **Example:** Grizzly Partners hires an employee "for life." The contract need not be in writing, because the employee could die within a year, which would complete performance.

---

## The Parol Evidence Rule

When the parties sign a writing intended as their **final and complete agreement** (an integrated contract), the **parol evidence rule** bars evidence of earlier or contemporaneous agreements, oral or written, that contradict or add to it. Evidence is still admissible to show:

- **Fraud, duress, or mistake** in forming the contract
- The meaning of an **ambiguous** term
- A **condition precedent** that had to occur before the contract took effect
- That the writing was **not** intended as the complete agreement

The rule does not apply to agreements made **after** the writing, so a later modification can always be proved.

## Third-Party Rights

### Third-Party Beneficiaries

| Type | Description | Can Enforce the Contract? |
|---|---|---|
| **Intended beneficiary** | A person the contracting parties meant to benefit | Yes, once the beneficiary's rights **vest** |
| **Incidental beneficiary** | A person who benefits only indirectly | No |

> **Example:** Panda Industries hires a builder to construct a warehouse, and the contract requires the builder to pay Cub Supply, a materials vendor, directly. Cub Supply is an **intended beneficiary** and can sue the builder if it is not paid.

### Assignment and Delegation

An **assignment** transfers contract **rights** to a third party. Most rights can be assigned, unless the contract prohibits it, the assignment would materially change the other party's duties or risk, or the rights are personal in nature. A **delegation** transfers contract **duties**. Duties requiring personal skill cannot be delegated, and the delegating party stays liable if the delegate fails to perform, unless all parties agree to a **novation** that substitutes the new party and releases the original one.

## Performance and Discharge

### Conditions and the Standard of Performance

Many duties depend on **conditions**. A **condition precedent** must occur before a duty arises (for example, a buyer's duty to close only if financing is approved). A **condition subsequent** ends an existing duty if it occurs. **Concurrent conditions** require both parties to perform at the same time, as when a buyer pays and a seller delivers at closing.

Under common law, a party who has **substantially performed** in good faith has met its obligation and may recover the contract price, minus damages for minor defects. A **material breach**, by contrast, excuses the other party from performing and gives it a claim for damages. For sales of goods, the UCC instead applies a **perfect tender** rule.

An **anticipatory repudiation**, a clear statement before performance is due that a party will not perform, lets the other party treat the contract as breached and sue immediately.

### Ways a Contract Is Discharged

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Performance** | Full performance by both parties discharges the contract |
| **Agreement** | **Mutual rescission** (both parties agree to cancel), **novation** (a new party replaces an original one), or **accord and satisfaction** (the parties agree to a substitute performance, and it is carried out) |
| **Impossibility** | Performance becomes objectively impossible, for example because the subject matter is destroyed, a party to a personal services contract dies, or a new law makes performance illegal |
| **Commercial impracticability** | An unforeseen event makes performance extremely and unreasonably difficult or costly |
| **Frustration of purpose** | An unforeseen event destroys the main purpose of the contract for one party, even though performance is still possible |
| **Other operation of law** | The statute of limitations runs, the debt is discharged in bankruptcy, or a party materially alters a written contract without consent |

> **Example:** Salmon Entertainment rents a rooftop terrace overlooking a stadium for one evening to host a party during a championship parade. The parade is canceled. The owner can still provide the terrace, but the only reason Salmon rented it is gone. The contract may be discharged by **frustration of purpose**.

## Remedies for Breach

| Remedy | Description |
|---|---|
| **Compensatory damages** | Put the injured party where it would have been if the contract had been performed |
| **Consequential damages** | Losses that flow from the breach and were **reasonably foreseeable** when the contract was made |
| **Liquidated damages** | An amount agreed in the contract; enforceable if actual damages were hard to estimate and the amount is reasonable, but void as a penalty if excessive |
| **Specific performance** | A court order to perform; available only when money damages are inadequate because the subject matter is **unique**, such as land or a rare item. Not available to force someone to perform personal services |
| **Rescission and restitution** | Cancel the contract and return each party what it gave |

The injured party has a **duty to mitigate**: it must take reasonable steps to limit its losses and cannot recover damages it could reasonably have avoided. **Punitive damages** are generally **not** available for breach of contract. They are possible only when the breach also involves a tort such as fraud.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

In remedy questions, check two limits before computing damages: was the loss **foreseeable** when the contract was made, and could the injured party have **mitigated** it? Losses that fail either test are not recoverable.

:::

## Summary

| Topic | Key Rule |
|---|---|
| Valid contract | Offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, legality |
| Offer ends | Revocation, rejection or counteroffer, lapse, death or incapacity |
| Mirror image rule | Common law acceptance must match the offer exactly |
| Mailbox rule | Acceptance effective on dispatch; everything else on receipt |
| Consideration | Bargained-for legal value; not past consideration or a preexisting duty |
| Defective assent | Fraud, misrepresentation, mutual mistake, duress, undue influence make a contract voidable |
| Statute of Frauds | MY LEGS contracts need a signed writing |
| Parol evidence | Cannot contradict a final writing, with exceptions; later modifications are provable |
| Intended beneficiary | Can enforce once rights vest |
| Delegation | Delegating party stays liable unless there is a novation |
| Discharge | Performance, agreement, impossibility, impracticability, frustration, operation of law |
| Remedies | Compensatory, consequential (if foreseeable), liquidated, specific performance (unique subject matter) |
