# Ethics and Responsibilities in Tax Practice

## Introduction

A tax practitioner serves two parties at once. The client wants the lowest tax the law allows, and the tax system depends on returns that are honest and complete. Two bodies of rules keep that balance. **Treasury Department Circular 230** governs people who practice before the IRS, and the **Internal Revenue Code (IRC)** imposes penalties directly on anyone who prepares a return for pay. On the exam, you will be asked to recall these rules and to apply them to short scenarios.

This chapter starts with Circular 230: who may practice, what duties practitioners owe, and how the IRS disciplines them. It then turns to the Code's definition of a tax return preparer and the penalties preparers face.

---

## Circular 230: Practice Before the IRS

### Who May Practice

**Practice before the IRS** covers every matter connected with a presentation to the IRS: preparing and filing documents, corresponding with the IRS, giving written advice that could affect a filing, and representing a client at conferences, hearings, and meetings. Circular 230 (31 C.F.R. Part 10) says who may do this and sets the standards they must meet.

| Practitioner | How Authorized | Representation Rights |
|---|---|---|
| **CPAs** | Licensed by a state board of accountancy | Unlimited: any client, any matter |
| **Attorneys** | Licensed by a state bar | Unlimited |
| **Enrolled agents (EAs)** | Pass the IRS Special Enrollment Examination, or qualify through IRS work experience | Unlimited |
| **Enrolled actuaries and enrolled retirement plan agents** | Enrolled by the Joint Board or the IRS | Limited to retirement plan and actuarial matters |
| **Annual Filing Season Program (AFSP) participants** | Unenrolled preparers who complete the program's annual education | Limited: only clients whose returns they prepared and signed, and only before revenue agents, customer service staff, and similar IRS employees during an examination |

Any other paid preparer may prepare returns but may not represent clients before the IRS.

### Duties of Every Practitioner

Circular 230 imposes a short list of affirmative duties:

- **Furnishing information.** A practitioner must promptly provide records or information the IRS lawfully requests, unless the practitioner believes in good faith that the information is privileged.
- **Knowledge of a client's omission.** A practitioner who learns that a client has not complied with the tax law, or has made an error or omission on a return, must promptly tell the client and explain the consequences.
- **Diligence as to accuracy.** A practitioner must use due diligence in preparing and filing returns and in every representation made to the IRS or to clients.
- **Prompt disposition.** A practitioner may not unreasonably delay any matter before the IRS.
- **Competence.** A practitioner may take on only work they are competent to do. Competence means the knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation the matter requires. A practitioner can become competent through study or by consulting someone who already is.

The duty on discovering an omission is to advise the **client**, not the IRS. Circular 230 does not require the practitioner to report the error, and the decision to amend belongs to the client. If the same error would carry into a return the practitioner is preparing now, the return-position rules below still apply to that new return.

A practitioner may generally rely in good faith on information the client provides, without verifying it. The practitioner may not ignore what the information implies, and must ask further questions when it looks incorrect, inconsistent, or incomplete.

> **Example:** Grizzly Inc. gives its CPA a list of deductible business expenses totaling \$500,000. Several items look personal, such as family vacations and clothing. Due diligence requires the CPA to ask about those items rather than accept the client's labels.

### Conflicts of Interest

A **conflict of interest** exists when representing one client is directly adverse to another client, or when there is a significant risk that the representation will be materially limited by the practitioner's own interests or duties to someone else. A practitioner may still go ahead if all three conditions are met:

1. The practitioner reasonably believes they can represent each affected client competently and diligently.
2. The representation is not prohibited by law.
3. Each affected client gives **informed consent, confirmed in writing**. The written confirmation must come within a reasonable time, and no later than 30 days after the consent. The practitioner keeps it for at least 36 months after the engagement ends.

> **Example:** Polar Co. and Kodiak Inc. are both clients of the same CPA firm. Polar Co. deducts payments to Kodiak Inc. that Kodiak Inc. does not report as income. The firm needs informed, written consent from both clients before it continues to represent them.

### Return Positions and Written Advice

A practitioner may not willfully, recklessly, or through gross incompetence sign a return, or advise a client to take a position, that lacks a **reasonable basis** or that would expose the preparer to the Code's penalty for an unreasonable position. A practitioner also must tell the client about any penalties that are reasonably likely to apply to a position and explain how disclosure could avoid them. The confidence levels behind these standards appear in the ladder of authority in [Federal Tax Procedures](./federal-tax-procedures.md#standards-for-return-positions).

When a practitioner gives **written advice** on a federal tax matter, the advice must rest on reasonable factual and legal assumptions, consider all relevant facts the practitioner knows or should know, and relate the law to those facts. The practitioner may not take into account the chance that a return will not be audited or that an issue will not be raised.

AICPA members also follow the AICPA's Statements on Standards for Tax Services, revised effective January 1, 2024. The REG blueprint's reference list names Circular 230 and the Code rather than those statements, so this chapter focuses on the federal rules.

### Fees, Records, and Advertising

**Contingent fees.** A practitioner generally may not charge a fee that depends on the outcome of a matter before the IRS. There are three exceptions:

- Services in connection with an IRS examination of, or challenge to, an original return, or to an amended return or refund claim filed within 120 days after the client received written notice of an examination
- Services on a refund claim filed solely for statutory interest or penalties
- Services in a judicial proceeding under the Code

Separately, a practitioner may not charge an **unconscionable** fee for any IRS matter.

**Client records.** At the client's request, a practitioner must promptly return the client's records needed to comply with federal tax obligations, even during a fee dispute. The practitioner may keep copies. What the practitioner may hold back while unpaid is its own work product, such as a return it prepared but has not yet delivered.

**Advertising and solicitation.** Practitioners may advertise, but not with false, fraudulent, coercive, or misleading statements. They may publish fee information such as hourly rates and fixed fees. An enrolled agent may describe the credential as "enrolled to represent taxpayers before the Internal Revenue Service" but may not use the word "certified" or imply employment by the IRS.

### Best Practices

Circular 230 §10.33 lists **best practices** for tax advisors: communicate clearly with the client about the terms of the engagement, establish the facts and evaluate any assumptions, reach a conclusion supported by the law and the facts, advise the client of the significance of that conclusion, and act fairly and with integrity before the IRS. Best practices are aspirations. Failing to follow them does not, by itself, lead to discipline.

### Sanctions

The IRS **Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR)** enforces Circular 230. For incompetence, disreputable conduct, or a willful violation, it may impose:

| Sanction | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Censure** | Public reprimand; the practitioner keeps the right to practice |
| **Suspension** | Loss of the right to practice for a set period |
| **Disbarment** | Loss of the right to practice; the practitioner may ask for reinstatement after 5 years |
| **Monetary penalty** | Up to the gross income derived from the conduct; may be imposed on the practitioner, the firm, or both, alone or in addition to another sanction |

## Tax Return Preparers

### Who Is a Tax Return Preparer?

Under IRC §7701(a)(36), a **tax return preparer** is any person who prepares for compensation, or who employs others to prepare for compensation, all or a substantial portion of a tax return or claim for refund. Every paid preparer must have a **Preparer Tax Identification Number (PTIN)**. Three parts of the definition drive most exam questions:

- **Compensation is required.** Someone who prepares a return for a friend or relative for free is not a tax return preparer.
- **A substantial portion is enough.** A person who prepares a schedule, or gives advice on an entry, that is a substantial portion of the return is a preparer even without preparing the whole return.
- **Some helpers are excluded.** A person is not a preparer if they provide only typing, data entry, or other mechanical help, prepare a return for their regular employer, or prepare a return as a fiduciary (such as an executor).

### Signing and Nonsigning Preparers

Several people often work on one return, so the regulations divide preparers into two groups:

- **Signing preparer:** the individual preparer with primary responsibility for the overall substantive accuracy of the return. That person signs it, regardless of who spent the most hours or holds the highest rank.
- **Nonsigning preparer:** anyone else who prepares all or a substantial portion of the return, or gives advice on it, for compensation. A nonsigning preparer is still a preparer and is still subject to the penalties below.

> **Example: CPA Bear Tax Services**
>
> The firm is preparing a complex Form 1040 for a new client.
>
> - **Alex**, an intern, enters the W-2s, 1099s, and the client's personal data into the tax software.
> - **Taylor**, a senior associate, prepares the Schedule C for the client's sole proprietorship and computes MACRS depreciation on several new assets. This is a substantial portion of the return.
> - **Morgan**, the tax manager, reviews the work, resolves a passive activity question, confirms that each position has substantial authority, and finalizes the return.
>
> Alex is **not** a preparer, because the work was mechanical. Taylor **is** a preparer, a nonsigning one, because Taylor prepared a substantial portion for pay. Morgan has primary responsibility for the return's overall accuracy and is the **signing preparer**.

## Penalties on Tax Return Preparers

### Understatements Due to Unreasonable Positions: §6694(a)

A preparer is penalized when an understatement on a return results from a position the preparer knew or should have known was **unreasonable**. Whether a position is reasonable depends on whether it was disclosed:

| Position | Standard the Preparer Needs |
|---|---|
| **Not disclosed** | **Substantial authority** |
| **Disclosed** (Form 8275 or 8275-R, or on the return) | **Reasonable basis** |
| **Tax shelter or reportable transaction** | A reasonable belief that the position is **more likely than not** to be sustained (disclosure does not help) |

The penalty is the **greater of \$1,000 or 50%** of the income the preparer derived (or will derive) from the return. It does not apply if the preparer shows reasonable cause and good faith.

> **Example:** Marcus, a CPA, prepares a client's return and claims an aggressive deduction without disclosing it. The position lacks substantial authority. Marcus earned a \$3,000 fee. His penalty is the greater of \$1,000 or 50% × \$3,000 = \$1,500, so the penalty is **\$1,500**.

### Willful or Reckless Conduct: §6694(b)

A more severe penalty applies when the understatement results from a **willful** attempt to understate the tax or from a **reckless or intentional disregard** of rules and regulations. The penalty is the **greater of \$5,000 or 75%** of the income derived from the return. The two penalties do not stack: the §6694(b) penalty is reduced by any §6694(a) penalty paid for the same position.

> **Example:** Sarah, a preparer, fabricates deductions to cut a client's tax and earns a \$10,000 fee. Her penalty is the greater of \$5,000 or 75% × \$10,000 = \$7,500, so the penalty is **\$7,500**.

### Compliance Failures: §6695

Section 6695 penalizes routine compliance failures, whether or not any tax is understated:

| Failure | Section | How the Penalty Works |
|---|---|---|
| Not giving the taxpayer a copy of the return | §6695(a) | Per failure, with an annual cap |
| Not signing the return | §6695(b) | Per failure, with an annual cap |
| Not including the preparer's identifying number (PTIN) | §6695(c) | Per failure, with an annual cap |
| Not keeping a copy or list of returns prepared for **3 years** | §6695(d) | Per failure, with an annual cap |
| Not filing correct information about the preparers a firm employs | §6695(e) | Per failure, with an annual cap |
| Endorsing or negotiating a client's refund check | §6695(f) | Per check |
| Failing due diligence for the earned income credit, child tax credit, American Opportunity credit, or head of household status | §6695(g) | Per failure |

The dollar amounts are indexed for inflation. For returns filed in 2026, the penalty is \$65 per failure (capped at \$32,500 a year) for subsections (a) through (e), and \$650 per check or per due-diligence failure.

> **Example:** Jamie files 600 returns during the 2026 filing season and leaves the PTIN off 10 of them. Using the 2026 amount, the penalty is 10 × \$65 = **\$650**.

A preparer must never endorse or cash a client's refund check, even to deposit it into a client trust account. The refund should go directly to the client, for example by direct deposit to the client's own account.

### Other Penalties

- **Aiding and abetting an understatement (§6701).** Anyone, not only a preparer, who helps prepare a return or other document knowing it will be used to understate tax owes **\$1,000** per document (**\$10,000** if it relates to a corporation's tax).
- **Disclosing or using return information without consent.** The civil penalty (§6713) is **\$250** per disclosure, up to **\$10,000** a year. Knowing or reckless disclosure is also a misdemeanor (§7216), punishable by a fine of up to **\$1,000**, up to **1 year** in prison, or both.

> **Example:** Derek, a preparer, shares a client's income information with a mortgage broker without the client's written consent. Derek faces a \$250 civil penalty under §6713 and possible criminal prosecution under §7216.

## Summary

| Topic | Key Rule |
|---|---|
| Who may practice | CPAs, attorneys, and enrolled agents have unlimited rights; AFSP participants have limited rights |
| Client omission | Advise the client promptly; no duty to notify the IRS |
| Conflicts of interest | Allowed with informed consent confirmed in writing within 30 days |
| Contingent fees | Generally prohibited, with three exceptions (examinations, interest and penalty claims, litigation) |
| Client records | Return them on request, even in a fee dispute |
| Circular 230 sanctions | Censure, suspension, disbarment, monetary penalty |
| Tax return preparer | Prepares all or a substantial portion of a return for compensation |
| §6694(a) | Greater of \$1,000 or 50% of the preparer's income from the return |
| §6694(b) | Greater of \$5,000 or 75%, reduced by any §6694(a) penalty |
| §6695 | Indexed per-failure penalties for compliance failures |
