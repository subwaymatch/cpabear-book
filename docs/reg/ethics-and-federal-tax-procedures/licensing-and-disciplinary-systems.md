# Licensing and Disciplinary Systems

## Introduction

No national body issues a CPA license. Each state, the District of Columbia, and the U.S. territories license their own CPAs through a **board of accountancy**, and that board can also take the license away. The REG blueprint asks you to understand and explain the role and authority of these boards, so this chapter starts there. It covers what the boards require to earn and keep a license, how they discipline licensees, how a CPA licensed in one state can practice in another, and how the boards fit alongside the AICPA and federal regulators.

---

## State Boards of Accountancy

### Role and Authority

There are 55 boards of accountancy: one for each state, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the Northern Mariana Islands. Each board is a **government agency** created by its jurisdiction's accountancy law, so its rules have the force of law. A board typically:

- Grants and renews individual CPA licenses and the permits firms need to practice
- Sets the education, examination, and experience requirements for licensure
- Sets continuing professional education (CPE) requirements
- Requires firms that perform audits and other attest work to undergo periodic **peer review**
- Investigates complaints and disciplines licensees

The boards work together through the **National Association of State Boards of Accountancy (NASBA)**. Most state accountancy laws are based on the **Uniform Accountancy Act (UAA)**, a model law written jointly by NASBA and the AICPA. Because each state adopts the UAA in its own way, the details vary, and the exam tests the common framework rather than any one state's rules.

The key contrast is with the **AICPA**. The AICPA is a voluntary professional association, and its Code of Professional Conduct binds only its members. A state board's authority comes from statute and reaches every licensee in the state, whether or not that person belongs to the AICPA.

### Licensing Requirements

Every jurisdiction builds its license on the "three Es": education, examination, and experience. In recent years, many states have added a second route to the license alongside the traditional one.

| Requirement | Traditional Pathway | Newer Pathway (adopted state by state) |
|---|---|---|
| **Education** | 150 semester hours, including a bachelor's degree, with required accounting and business courses | A bachelor's degree with an accounting concentration |
| **Examination** | Pass all four sections of the Uniform CPA Examination | Same |
| **Experience** | Typically 1 year of supervised experience | 2 years of experience |

The AICPA and NASBA approved model legislation for the newer pathway on May 14, 2025. It applies only in states that adopt it, so a candidate must check the rules of the board where they plan to be licensed.

Each board also sets the **credit window** for the exam, the period in which all four sections must be passed. The NASBA model rule is a rolling 30 months, and some boards allow longer.

> **Example:** A staff accountant at Polar Co. has a bachelor's degree with an accounting concentration and has passed all four sections of the CPA Exam. Her state has adopted the newer pathway, which requires two years of experience. Once she completes those two years, she can apply to her state board for a license.

Many states let candidates **sit for the exam** before they meet the full education requirement for licensure, for example once they hold a bachelor's degree. Sitting for the exam and being licensed are separate steps.

### Continuing Professional Education

To keep a license active, a CPA must complete **CPE**. Requirements vary by board, but a typical rule calls for:

- About **40 hours a year**, often measured as 80 hours over two years or 120 hours over three
- A minimum number of hours in accounting and auditing for CPAs who perform attest work
- An **ethics** component, often a few hours each reporting period
- Courses from qualified sponsors, such as sponsors registered with NASBA

A CPA who does not meet the CPE requirement can face nonrenewal or suspension of the license.

## How State Boards Discipline Licensees

### Grounds for Discipline

A board may discipline a CPA for conduct that includes:

- **Fraud or deceit** in obtaining a license
- **Dishonesty, fraud, or gross negligence** in performing professional services
- Violating the state accountancy law or the board's rules
- Failing to follow professional standards, such as auditing or accounting standards
- **Conviction of a felony**, or of a crime involving dishonesty or fraud
- **Discipline by another authority**, such as another state board, the SEC, the PCAOB, or the IRS

### The Process

Discipline usually begins with a **complaint** from a client, another CPA, a government agency, or the board's own review. The board investigates, and if it finds grounds, the licensee receives notice and an opportunity for a **hearing**. A licensee who disagrees with the board's final decision can generally ask a court to review it.

### Sanctions

| Sanction | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Reprimand or censure** | A formal finding of wrongdoing; the license continues |
| **Probation** | The license continues subject to conditions, such as extra CPE or review of the CPA's work |
| **Suspension** | The license is inactive for a set period |
| **Revocation** | The license is taken away |
| **Fine** | A monetary penalty, often combined with another sanction |

> **Example:** A CPA issues an unmodified audit opinion for Kodiak Inc. without performing adequate audit procedures. After an investigation and hearing, the state board suspends her license for two years and requires 80 hours of auditing CPE before reinstatement.

Only the board that issued a license can suspend or revoke it. The AICPA can expel a member, but expulsion does not affect the license, although it may lead the board to open its own investigation.

## Practicing in Other States

**Mobility**, also called the **practice privilege**, lets a CPA licensed in one state serve clients in another state without getting a second license. Under the UAA approach most states follow, a CPA who holds a license in good standing can practice across state lines without notice or fees, as long as the CPA's qualifications meet the model's standards. The 2025 model legislation that added the newer licensure pathway also moves mobility to an **individual-based** test, so the privilege depends on the individual CPA's qualifications.

The privilege comes with a condition: by practicing in a state, the CPA **consents to the jurisdiction** of that state's board. The CPA therefore answers to both the home state board and the board of any state where the CPA practices. A firm that performs attest work for clients based in a state generally needs a permit from that state as well.

## How State Boards Fit With Other Regulators

| Regulator | Who It Covers | Primary Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **State boards of accountancy** | Every CPA and CPA firm licensed in the jurisdiction | Licensing, CPE, peer review requirements, discipline up to revocation |
| **AICPA** | Voluntary members only | Professional standards, the Code of Professional Conduct, peer review programs, expulsion from membership |
| **SEC** | Accountants who practice before the SEC | May suspend or bar an accountant from practicing before the SEC (Rule 102(e)) |
| **PCAOB** | Firms registered to audit public companies | Auditing standards for public company audits, inspections, sanctions |
| **IRS Office of Professional Responsibility** | Anyone who practices before the IRS | Circular 230 sanctions, from censure to disbarment |

## Summary

| Topic | Key Point |
|---|---|
| Who licenses CPAs | Each of the 55 boards of accountancy, under state law |
| Model law | The Uniform Accountancy Act, written by NASBA and the AICPA |
| Licensure pathways | 150 hours plus about 1 year of experience, or (where adopted) a bachelor's degree with an accounting concentration plus 2 years |
| Credit window | Set by each board; the NASBA model is a rolling 30 months |
| CPE | Typically 40 hours a year, including ethics |
| Discipline | From reprimand to revocation, after notice and a hearing |
| AICPA | Can expel members but cannot revoke a license |
| Mobility | Practice across state lines without a second license, subject to the host board's jurisdiction |
