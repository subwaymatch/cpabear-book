# Sole Proprietorship

A **sole proprietorship** is the default form for a business with one individual owner who has not formed a separate legal entity. Legally and for federal income tax, the owner and the business are the same person: there is no separate return, and the business's income and expenses go straight onto the owner's Form 1040.

That simplicity makes the sole proprietorship the baseline against which the exam compares partnerships, LLCs, and corporations. This chapter covers Schedule C reporting, the deductions that are limited or disallowed, self-employment tax, the business interest limit, and how losses and hobby rules apply. A single-member LLC that has not elected corporate treatment is taxed exactly the same way.

## Core legal and tax profile

- **Owner:** One individual.
- **Liability:** Unlimited personal liability for business debts and lawsuits.
- **Federal return:** None for the business. Activity is reported on **Schedule C** of the owner's Form 1040.
- **Tax character:** Income, deductions, gains, and losses belong directly to the owner.

---

## Schedule C reporting

All business income and deductible expenses go on **Schedule C (Profit or Loss From Business)**. Net profit flows to Schedule 1 of Form 1040 and is also the starting point for self-employment tax on **Schedule SE**.

### Income

Gross receipts include sales (less returns and allowances), fees for services, the fair market value of goods or services received in barter, interest on business accounts, and recoveries of amounts deducted in earlier years. All of it is income whether or not a payor issues an information return. For payments made after 2025, the threshold for a business to file Form 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC rose from \$600 to \$2,000, and payment platforms file Form 1099-K only when a seller has more than \$20,000 of payments and more than 200 transactions. Fewer forms do not mean less income: a return preparer still reconciles gross receipts to the owner's own records.

### Deductible business expenses

Expenses that are **ordinary and necessary** for the business are deductible on Schedule C:

| Expense | Deductibility |
|---------|--------------|
| Advertising, office rent, supplies, business insurance | Fully deductible |
| Employee wages and salaries | Deductible if reasonable |
| Depreciation | MACRS, with §179 and bonus depreciation available |
| Home office | If the space is used regularly and exclusively for the business |
| Business travel away from home | Transportation and lodging fully deductible; meals 50% |
| Vehicle expenses | Actual costs or the standard mileage rate |
| Health insurance for the owner | Not on Schedule C; deducted as an adjustment to income instead |

### Business meals and entertainment

Business meals are **50% deductible** when the meal has a clear business purpose, is not lavish, and the owner or an employee is present.

| Scenario | Deductible % |
|----------|-------------|
| Meal with a client or customer | **50%** |
| Meals while traveling away from home on business | **50%** |
| Holiday party or picnic for all employees | **100%** |
| Meals provided on the business premises for the employer's convenience (starting in 2026) | **0%** |
| Entertainment (sporting events, golf, concerts) | **0%** |
| Personal meals | **0%** |

Starting in 2026, meals an employer provides for its own convenience or through an on-site eating facility are no longer deductible at all, even though employees may still exclude them from income. Meals sold to customers are an exception.

> **Example:** Jamie operates Bear Co. as a sole proprietorship and spends \$2,400 on business lunches with clients during the year. She deducts \$1,200 (50% × \$2,400) on Schedule C. The other \$1,200 is nondeductible.

## Bad debts

A business bad debt is an **ordinary deduction** when a specific receivable becomes wholly or partly worthless. Only an **accrual-method** taxpayer can deduct a bad debt from an uncollected sale or fee, because only an accrual-method taxpayer has already included the revenue in income.

| Accounting Method | Bad Debt Deduction for Uncollected Receivables? |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Accrual** | Yes, when the specific receivable becomes worthless |
| **Cash** | No; the income was never reported, so the receivable has no basis |

> **Example:** Sarah operates Polar Co. on the accrual method. She bills a client \$8,000 and includes it in income. When the client goes bankrupt and the receivable becomes worthless, she deducts \$8,000 as a business bad debt on Schedule C. A cash-method owner in the same situation would deduct nothing, because the \$8,000 was never income.

## Business interest expense limitation

**Section 163(j)** limits the deduction for business interest expense to:

$$
\text{Business interest income} + 30\% \times \text{Adjusted taxable income (ATI)}
$$

For tax years beginning after 2024, **ATI** is again computed before depreciation, amortization, and depletion, a measure much like EBITDA, which makes the 30% limit more generous for capital-intensive businesses. Disallowed interest carries forward indefinitely. The limit applies at the owner level for a sole proprietorship.

Most sole proprietorships are exempt. A business whose average annual gross receipts for the prior three years do not exceed an indexed threshold (\$32 million for 2026) is not subject to §163(j).

## Nondeductible expenses

Some expenses are never deductible on Schedule C:

| Expense | Reason |
|---------|--------|
| Personal, living, and family expenses | Not business related |
| Federal income taxes | Specifically disallowed |
| Fines and penalties paid to a government | Public policy |
| Political contributions and most lobbying costs | Specifically disallowed |
| Bribes and kickbacks | Illegal payments |
| Dues to country clubs and social clubs | Specifically disallowed |
| Commuting between home and a regular workplace | Personal expense |
| Life insurance premiums when the owner or family is the beneficiary | Personal expense |

## Self-employment tax

Net earnings from the business are subject to **self-employment (SE) tax** in addition to income tax. SE tax is the owner's version of both the employer and employee halves of Social Security and Medicare.

1. Start with Schedule C net profit.
2. Multiply by **92.35%**.
3. Apply **12.4%** Social Security (up to the indexed wage base) and **2.9%** Medicare (no cap).
4. If combined wages and SE income exceed \$200,000 (\$250,000 MFJ), the owner also owes the **0.9%** additional Medicare tax.

The owner deducts **half** of the SE tax as an adjustment to income on Schedule 1, not on Schedule C.

> **Example:** Marcus operates Salmon Entertainment as a sole proprietorship and has \$120,000 of net profit. His SE earnings are \$120,000 × 92.35% = \$110,820, and his SE tax is \$110,820 × 15.3% = **\$16,955**. He deducts half, \$8,478, as an adjustment to income. See [Other Individual Taxes](../taxation-of-individuals/other-individual-taxes.md) for the full SE tax rules.

:::info

Self-employment tax is one of the biggest differences between a sole proprietorship and an S corporation. An S corporation owner who works in the business pays payroll taxes only on reasonable **wages**, not on the rest of the profit. A sole proprietor pays SE tax on **all** net earnings.

:::

## QBI deduction

Schedule C net profit from a qualified trade or business is **qualified business income**, so a sole proprietor may claim the §199A deduction of up to 20% of it on the same return. The deduction does not reduce net profit for SE tax purposes. See [Adjustments and Deductions](../taxation-of-individuals/adjustments-and-deductions.md).

## Losses and hobby activities

A Schedule C loss can offset the owner's other income, subject to the at-risk limit, the passive activity rules (if the owner does not materially participate), and the **excess business loss** limit, which the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21) made permanent. Any excess business loss becomes an NOL carryforward. See [Loss Limitations](../taxation-of-individuals/loss-limitations.md).

If the activity is not engaged in for profit, it is a **hobby** under §183. Hobby income is fully taxable, but hobby expenses are not deductible at all, so a hobby can produce tax on its income with no offsetting deductions. Whether an activity is a business depends on nine factors, and a profit in 3 of the last 5 years creates a presumption of a profit motive; both are covered in [Loss Limitations](../taxation-of-individuals/loss-limitations.md).

## Basis and sale of the business

There is no ownership interest separate from the business assets, so the owner tracks the **basis of each asset**. A sale of the business is treated as a sale of its individual assets, which can produce a mix of ordinary income (for example, on inventory and depreciation recapture), §1231 gain, and capital gain.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Topic | Sole Proprietorship |
| --- | --- |
| Formation | Easiest and least expensive structure |
| Tax filing | Simple reporting on the owner's return |
| Liability protection | None |
| Raising capital | Limited; there is no ownership interest to sell |
| Continuity | Tied closely to the owner |

## REG quick hits

- No separate tax return; report on **Schedule C**.
- SE tax: multiply net profit by 92.35%, then apply 15.3%; deduct half as an adjustment.
- Business meals are **50%** deductible; entertainment is **0%**; on-premises meals for the employer's convenience are **0%** starting in 2026.
- Bad debts from uncollected receivables are deductible only by **accrual-method** taxpayers.
- Business interest is limited to 30% of ATI (now computed before depreciation and amortization), but small businesses under the gross receipts threshold are exempt.
- Fines, penalties, political contributions, club dues, personal expenses, and federal income taxes are **never** deductible.
- Hobby income is taxable; hobby expenses are not deductible.
- Selling the business is a sale of its assets, not of an ownership interest.

## Bottom line

A sole proprietorship is easy to start and easy to report, but it offers no liability protection and no way to bring in investors. On the REG exam, expect Schedule C deductions, the meals rules, SE tax, the hobby rules, and the line between deductible and nondeductible expenses.
