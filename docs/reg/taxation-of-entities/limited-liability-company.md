# Limited Liability Company

A **limited liability company (LLC)** is a business entity created under state law that gives its owners, called **members**, corporate-style protection from the entity's debts. For federal income tax, though, an LLC is not a tax system of its own. It is taxed as a sole proprietorship, a partnership, a C corporation, or an S corporation, depending on how many members it has and what it elects.

The REG blueprint asks candidates to recall these classification options, so this chapter is short: the default rules, the elections, and what each choice means for the rest of the analysis.

## Core legal and tax profile

- **Owners:** Members (one or more).
- **Liability:** Members generally are not personally liable for the LLC's debts.
- **Federal return:** Depends on the tax classification.
- **Tax character:** Disregarded entity, partnership, C corporation, or S corporation.

## Default classification rules

Under the "check-the-box" regulations (Reg. §301.7701-3), a domestic LLC that makes no election is classified by its number of members:

| Members | Default Classification | Federal Reporting |
|---|---|---|
| One | **Disregarded entity** | On the owner's return (Schedule C, E, or F for an individual owner) |
| Two or more | **Partnership** | Form 1065, with a K-1 for each member |

## Elections

An LLC that wants a different result can elect it:

- **Corporate classification.** The LLC files **Form 8832** to be taxed as a C corporation. It then files Form 1120 and pays corporate tax, and its members are shareholders for tax purposes.
- **S corporation status.** An LLC that meets the S corporation requirements (domestic, no more than 100 eligible shareholders, one class of stock) can file **Form 2553**. Filing Form 2553 is treated as also electing corporate classification, so no separate Form 8832 is needed.

After an LLC changes its classification by election, it generally cannot change again for 60 months.

> **Example:** Cub Entertainment LLC has two members and makes no election, so it files Form 1065 as a partnership. After a few profitable years, it files Form 2553 to be taxed as an S corporation. From then on it files Form 1120-S, and a member who works in the business must be paid reasonable W-2 compensation.

## Why LLCs are popular

LLCs combine **limited liability**, which a partnership's general partners lack, with **tax flexibility**: the default pass-through treatment of a partnership or sole proprietorship, plus the option to elect corporate or S corporation status later. That combination makes LLCs common for closely held businesses, real estate ventures, and professional practices.

## How classification drives the analysis

Always identify how the LLC is taxed before analyzing basis, distributions, or owner compensation.

- **Disregarded entity:** use the sole proprietorship rules. The owner pays self-employment tax on net profit.
- **Partnership:** use the partnership rules for basis (including shares of LLC debt), distributions, and guaranteed payments. A member who works in the business is not an employee; payments for services are guaranteed payments, generally subject to self-employment tax.
- **C corporation:** use the corporate rules, including double taxation of distributed profits.
- **S corporation:** use the S corporation rules. Member-employees receive W-2 wages subject to payroll taxes, and their share of the remaining income is not self-employment income.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Topic | LLC |
| --- | --- |
| Liability protection | Generally strong |
| Tax flexibility | Very high |
| Administrative burden | Usually lower than a corporation |
| Investor preferences | Some institutional investors prefer corporations |
| Exam complexity | Must identify the tax classification first |

## REG quick hits

- An LLC is a **legal form**, not a federal tax regime.
- One member: disregarded by default. Two or more: partnership by default.
- Form 8832 elects corporate classification; Form 2553 elects S status (and is treated as electing corporate classification too).
- A classification change by election generally cannot be changed again for 60 months.
- Analyze the LLC under the rules for its classification.

## Bottom line

An LLC is often the most flexible choice for a closely held business. The exam trap is assuming every LLC is taxed the same way. Find the classification first, and the rest of the question follows the rules for that classification.
