# Venture Capital and Emerging-Growth Structures

**Venture capital** is a way of financing high-growth businesses, not a separate kind of tax entity. It involves two layers of entities: the **fund** that pools investors' money, and the **portfolio companies** the fund invests in. Each layer tends to use a particular structure for tax and practical reasons.

For REG, the useful takeaway is the structural logic: why funds are usually partnerships, why the startups they back are usually C corporations, and why S corporations rarely fit. This short chapter draws on the entity chapters that come before it.

## Venture capital fund structure

Most venture capital funds are organized as a **limited partnership** or as an **LLC taxed as a partnership**. That structure gives the fund:

- Pass-through taxation, so fund income is taxed once, to the investors
- A clean split between active managers and passive investors
- Flexible allocations of profits among the partners

### Roles inside the fund

- **General partner (GP):** manages the fund and makes the investment decisions.
- **Limited partners (LPs):** supply most of the capital and do not manage day-to-day operations; their losses are generally passive.
- **Management company:** often an affiliate of the GP that receives a management fee for running the fund.

## Portfolio company structure

The startup that receives venture money is usually a **C corporation**, very often a Delaware corporation. Investors prefer C corporations because they can:

- Issue **preferred stock** alongside common stock
- Admit many investors of any type
- Offer stock options and other equity compensation easily
- Accept institutional, foreign, and tax-exempt investors without passing taxable business income through to them
- Qualify for the **qualified small business stock (QSBS)** exclusion of §1202, if its requirements are met. For stock issued after July 4, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21) allows a 50% exclusion after 3 years, 75% after 4 years, and 100% after 5 years, and it raised the per-company gain cap to \$15 million and the gross assets test to \$75 million.

## Why S corporations and LLCs are less common in VC-backed companies

### S corporation limits

An S corporation is usually a poor fit for venture capital because it cannot have ineligible shareholders (such as the fund itself, if it is a partnership, or a corporate investor), more than 100 shareholders, or more than one class of stock. Venture deals almost always involve a partnership investor and preferred stock, so the election would terminate.

### LLC limits

An LLC taxed as a partnership can work at an early stage, but it becomes less attractive once investors want preferred equity, standardized exit terms, or corporate-style stock options. Tax-exempt and foreign investors also generally avoid partnerships that run operating businesses, because the business income would flow through to them.

## CPA exam connections

A REG question will not ask for fund documents, but it can test the logic:

- Venture funds commonly use partnership-style entities.
- Operating companies seeking institutional growth capital commonly use C corporations.
- Preferred stock and broad investor bases fit a C corporation, not an S corporation.

## Quick comparison

| Structure | Typical VC Use |
| --- | --- |
| Limited partnership | Common for the investment fund |
| LLC taxed as a partnership | Also common for the fund or the manager |
| C corporation | Common for the portfolio company |
| S corporation | Usually a poor fit for VC financing |
| Sole proprietorship | Not practical for venture-backed growth |

## REG quick hits

- Venture capital is a financing approach, not an entity type.
- VC funds are commonly LPs or LLCs taxed as partnerships.
- Venture-backed startups are commonly C corporations.
- S corporation eligibility limits conflict with venture financing.

## Bottom line

When a REG question mentions venture capital, match the investors to the structure: passive investors pool their money in a partnership-style fund, and the high-growth operating business is usually a C corporation.
