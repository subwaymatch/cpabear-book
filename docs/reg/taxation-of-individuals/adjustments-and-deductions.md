# Adjustments and Deductions

Deductions are where much of the planning, and much of the exam's arithmetic, happens on an individual return. They come in two layers. **Adjustments** (also called above-the-line deductions) are subtracted from gross income to reach **adjusted gross income (AGI)**, the figure that drives most floors and phase-outs later on the return. **Deductions from AGI** are then subtracted to reach taxable income: the larger of the standard deduction or itemized deductions, plus a group of deductions that every taxpayer can claim, led by the qualified business income (QBI) deduction.

This chapter follows that order. It reflects the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025); its provisions with 2024 or 2025 effective dates have been eligible for REG testing since July 1, 2026. Many limits below are indexed for inflation, and the exam does not test indexed amounts, so where one helps it appears once, labeled with its year.

---

## Adjustments to Gross Income

Adjustments are available whether or not the taxpayer itemizes. Because they reduce AGI, they also loosen every AGI-based limit that comes later, such as the 7.5% floor on medical expenses and the percentage limits on charitable gifts. The adjustments most often tested are:

| Adjustment | IRC Section | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Educator expenses | §62(a)(2)(D) | Indexed cap per educator |
| Student loan interest | §221 | \$2,500 |
| HSA contributions | §223 | Indexed annual limit |
| Moving expenses (Armed Forces and certain intelligence-community employees only) | §217 | Actual costs |
| Penalty on early withdrawal of savings | §62(a)(9) | Amount forfeited |
| Alimony (agreements executed before 2019) | §215 | Amount paid |
| Traditional IRA contributions | §219 | Indexed annual limit |
| One-half of self-employment tax | §164(f) | 50% of SE tax |
| Self-employed health insurance | §162(l) | Net SE income from the business |
| Self-employed retirement plans | §404 | Plan limits |

## Educator Expenses

An **eligible educator** is a K-12 teacher, instructor, counselor, principal, or aide who works at least 900 hours during the school year. An eligible educator may deduct unreimbursed classroom costs such as books, supplies, computer equipment, and supplementary materials, up to an indexed cap (\$300 for 2025 and \$350 for 2026). The cap applies per educator, so two eligible spouses filing jointly can each claim it.

Starting in 2026, OBBBA adds a second layer. Unreimbursed educator expenses above the cap are deductible as an itemized deduction, with no dollar cap and no 2%-of-AGI floor.

> **Example:** Sarah teaches fifth grade and spends \$475 on classroom supplies in 2026. She deducts \$350 as an adjustment to income. If she itemizes, the remaining \$125 is an itemized deduction. If she takes the standard deduction, the \$125 gives her no benefit.

A later law, P.L. 119-111 (September 2026), extends the above-the-line deduction to early childhood educators for 2026 and later years. Under the AICPA's six-month rule, that change should become eligible for testing on April 1, 2027.

## Student Loan Interest

A taxpayer may deduct up to **\$2,500** a year of interest on a qualified education loan, meaning a loan taken out solely to pay qualified higher education expenses. The \$2,500 is fixed by statute, but the deduction phases out over an indexed MAGI range. It is not available to a taxpayer who files MFS or who can be claimed as someone else's dependent.

> **Example:** Marcus pays \$3,200 of interest on his federal student loans. His MAGI is below the phase-out range, so he deducts the \$2,500 maximum.

## Health Savings Accounts (HSAs)

A taxpayer covered by a **high-deductible health plan (HDHP)**, and by no other health coverage that is not an HDHP, may contribute to a health savings account (HSA) and deduct the contribution as an adjustment. Contributions made by an employer are excluded from the employee's income instead, so they are not deducted a second time. The annual limits are indexed (\$4,400 for self-only coverage and \$8,750 for family coverage in 2026). A taxpayer age 55 or older may add a \$1,000 catch-up contribution, an amount fixed by statute.

Distributions used for qualified medical expenses are tax free. A distribution used for anything else is included in gross income and, before age 65, also carries a 20% additional tax.

OBBBA widened HSA eligibility starting in 2026. Bronze and catastrophic plans bought through an exchange now count as HDHPs, and a direct primary care arrangement no longer disqualifies a taxpayer (its fees, up to a monthly cap, are qualified medical expenses). The rule letting an HDHP cover telehealth before the deductible is now permanent.

> **Example:** Jamie (age 42, self-only HDHP coverage) contributes \$4,400 to an HSA in 2026 and deducts the full amount. A \$1,200 withdrawal to pay for a dental procedure is tax free. If Jamie instead withdrew \$800 for a vacation, the \$800 would be taxable income plus a \$160 additional tax (20%).

## Moving Expenses

OBBBA permanently repealed the moving expense deduction, along with the exclusion for employer reimbursements of moving costs, for almost everyone. Two groups keep it: active-duty members of the Armed Forces who move under military orders for a permanent change of station, and, beginning in 2026, certain intelligence-community employees and new appointees who must relocate. For those taxpayers, the deductible costs are the reasonable costs of moving household goods and of traveling to the new home, including lodging but not meals.

## Alimony

Whether alimony is deductible depends entirely on **when** the divorce or separation instrument was executed.

| Instrument Executed | Payor | Payee |
|---|---|---|
| **Before January 1, 2019** | Deductible (adjustment to income) | Included in gross income |
| **After December 31, 2018** | **Not** deductible | **Not** included in gross income |

A pre-2019 instrument that is modified later keeps the old treatment unless the modification expressly adopts the new rules.

Under a pre-2019 instrument, a payment counts as deductible alimony only if it is made in cash (or by check) under a written divorce or separation instrument, the instrument does not label it as something other than alimony, the spouses do not live in the same household when it is paid, and the obligation ends at the payee's death.

:::warning

**Child support** is never deductible by the payor and never taxable to the payee, whenever the agreement was signed. If a payment drops when a child-related event occurs (for example, the child turns 18), the amount of the drop is treated as child support.

:::

> **Example:** Derek pays his former spouse \$2,000 a month under a divorce decree entered in 2017, of which \$800 is designated as child support. Derek deducts \$1,200 a month (\$14,400 a year) as alimony, and his former spouse includes the same \$14,400 in income. If the decree had been entered in 2020, none of the \$2,000 would be deductible or taxable.

## IRA Contributions

A taxpayer with earned income may contribute to an individual retirement account (IRA) up to an indexed annual limit (\$7,000 for 2025), plus a catch-up contribution at age 50 or older. The limit applies per person, not per account. On a joint return, a nonworking spouse may contribute to a spousal IRA based on the working spouse's earned income.

| Type | Contributions | Distributions |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional (deductible)** | Deductible as an adjustment | Taxed as ordinary income |
| **Traditional (nondeductible)** | Not deductible, so they create basis | Only the earnings are taxed |
| **Roth** | Not deductible | **Tax free** if qualified |

A traditional IRA contribution is fully deductible unless the taxpayer (or spouse) is an **active participant** in an employer retirement plan. If either one is, the deduction phases out over an indexed MAGI range. Roth contributions are never deductible, and the ability to make them phases out at higher MAGI levels.

> **Example:** Priya (age 35) earns \$70,000 and participates in her employer's 401(k). She contributes \$7,000 to a traditional IRA for 2025. Because she is an active participant, her deduction depends on where her MAGI falls in the phase-out range. If her MAGI is below the range, the full \$7,000 is deductible.

OBBBA also created **Trump accounts** (§530A), a type of traditional IRA for a child under 18. Contributions began July 4, 2026, are limited to \$5,000 a year, and are **not deductible**. An employer may contribute up to \$2,500 a year per employee without the contribution being taxable to the employee (see [Gross Income](./gross-income.md)), and Treasury deposits \$1,000 for each eligible U.S.-citizen child born in 2025 through 2028. No distributions are allowed before the year the child turns 18.

## Self-Employed Adjustments

Self-employed individuals get several adjustments that mirror benefits an employer would otherwise provide.

**One-half of self-employment tax.** The taxpayer deducts 50% of the self-employment tax computed on Schedule SE. See [Other Individual Taxes](./other-individual-taxes.md) for the computation.

**Self-employed health insurance.** A self-employed individual may deduct 100% of health insurance premiums for the taxpayer, spouse, and dependents. The deduction cannot exceed the net self-employment income of the business that sponsors the plan, and it is not available for any month in which the taxpayer or spouse is eligible for an employer-subsidized health plan.

**Retirement plans.** Contributions to a SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or solo 401(k) are deductible within each plan's limits, and those dollar limits are indexed. The rule to know is the SEP percentage. A SEP allows contributions of up to 25% of compensation, but for a self-employed owner the contribution itself reduces the compensation base. The limit therefore works out to **20%** of net self-employment earnings after subtracting the deduction for half of SE tax.

> **Example:** Dana has \$150,000 of net profit from her consulting business. Her SE tax is \$150,000 × 92.35% × 15.3% = \$21,194, so the deduction for half of it is \$10,597. Her maximum SEP contribution is 20% × (\$150,000 - \$10,597) = 20% × \$139,403 = **\$27,881**, which she deducts as an adjustment to income.

## Standard Deduction

After computing AGI, a taxpayer subtracts the **larger** of the standard deduction or itemized deductions. OBBBA made the larger standard deduction permanent. The basic amounts are indexed; for 2026 they are \$16,100 for single or MFS, \$24,150 for head of household, and \$32,200 for MFJ or a qualifying surviving spouse.

Taxpayers who are 65 or older or blind add an **additional standard deduction** for each condition. The amount is indexed and is larger for unmarried taxpayers (for 2026, \$1,650 per condition if married and \$2,050 if unmarried), so a married taxpayer who is both 65 and blind adds two amounts. A taxpayer who can be claimed as someone else's dependent is limited to the greater of a small fixed amount (\$1,350 for 2026) or earned income plus \$450.

Some taxpayers cannot use the standard deduction at all: an MFS filer whose spouse itemizes, a nonresident alien, and a taxpayer filing a short-year return because of a change in accounting period.

## Itemized Deductions

Itemized deductions are reported on **Schedule A**. The main categories are medical expenses, taxes, interest, charitable contributions, casualty losses, and gambling losses. Miscellaneous itemized deductions subject to the 2%-of-AGI floor, such as unreimbursed employee expenses and investment fees, are permanently eliminated under OBBBA, apart from the educator expense exception described above.

## Medical and Dental Expenses

A taxpayer may deduct unreimbursed medical and dental expenses paid for the taxpayer, spouse, and dependents, but only the amount that exceeds **7.5% of AGI**. Expenses are deducted in the year **paid**, whenever the care was provided.

Qualifying expenses include payments to physicians, dentists, and other practitioners; hospital and nursing home fees; prescription drugs and insulin; health insurance premiums paid with after-tax dollars; transportation for medical care; and long-term care costs (premiums for long-term care insurance are subject to age-based limits). Cosmetic surgery (unless it corrects a deformity from disease, accident, or a congenital condition), over-the-counter medicines other than insulin, gym memberships, and funeral costs do not qualify.

> **Example:** Grace has AGI of \$80,000 and pays \$9,500 of unreimbursed medical expenses. The floor is \$80,000 × 7.5% = \$6,000, so she deducts \$9,500 - \$6,000 = **\$3,500** on Schedule A.

## State and Local Taxes (SALT)

Individuals may deduct certain taxes paid during the year, subject to a cap on the total.

| Tax | Deductible? |
|---|---|
| State and local **income** taxes | Yes, or sales taxes instead (not both) |
| State and local **general sales** taxes | Yes, if elected instead of income taxes |
| State and local **real property** taxes | Yes |
| State and local **personal property** taxes based on value | Yes |
| **Foreign** income taxes not claimed as a credit | Yes |
| Federal income, Social Security, and excise taxes | No |
| Estate, inheritance, and gift taxes | No |
| Fines and penalties | No |

### The SALT Cap

The combined deduction for state and local income (or sales), real property, and personal property taxes is capped. OBBBA raised the cap from \$10,000 to **\$40,000 for 2025** and **\$40,400 for 2026**. It grows 1% a year through 2029 and drops back to \$10,000 in 2030. An MFS filer gets half. These amounts are written into the statute rather than indexed, so they are fair game on the exam.

The higher cap phases down for high-income taxpayers. It is reduced by **30% of MAGI above a threshold** (\$500,000 for 2025 and \$505,000 for 2026, also rising 1% a year; half for MFS), but never below \$10,000 (\$5,000 MFS).

> **Example:** In 2026, Carmen and Ben file jointly with MAGI of \$300,000. They pay \$30,000 of state income tax and \$15,000 of real property tax, a total of \$45,000. Their SALT deduction is limited to **\$40,400**. If their MAGI were \$555,000 instead, the cap would drop by 30% × (\$555,000 - \$505,000) = \$15,000, to \$25,400.

:::caution

The cap applies only to taxes claimed as personal itemized deductions. Taxes paid in a trade or business or rental activity are deducted on Schedule C, E, or F with no cap. Many states also let a partnership or S corporation elect to pay state income tax at the entity level (a **pass-through entity tax**, or PTET). The entity deducts that tax in computing its ordinary income, so it never reaches the owners' Schedule A cap. Notice 2020-75 permits this, and OBBBA did not limit it.

:::

A refund of state income tax deducted in an earlier year may be taxable under the tax benefit rule, and the SALT cap can shrink the taxable part. See [Gross Income](./gross-income.md).

## Charitable Contributions

A contribution to a qualified charity is deductible as an itemized deduction. How much is deductible depends on what is given and to whom. A **public charity** is a church, school, hospital, or other broadly supported charity; a **private nonoperating foundation** is typically funded by one family or company and makes grants.

| Gift | Amount Deductible | AGI Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash to a public charity | Amount given | 60% |
| Ordinary income property (inventory, property held 1 year or less) to a public charity | Lesser of basis or FMV | 50% |
| Long-term capital gain property to a public charity | FMV | 30% |
| Cash or ordinary income property to a private nonoperating foundation | Amount given (lesser of basis or FMV for property) | 30% |
| Long-term capital gain property to a private nonoperating foundation | Generally basis (FMV for publicly traded stock) | 20% |

When a taxpayer makes several kinds of gifts, the limits are applied in a set order, and total charitable deductions generally cannot exceed 60% of AGI. Any excess carries forward for **5 years**, subject to the same limits each year.

Three OBBBA changes apply starting in 2026:

1. **The 60% limit for cash is permanent.** It had been scheduled to expire after 2025.
2. **A 0.5% floor.** Itemized charitable deductions are allowed only to the extent the year's total exceeds 0.5% of AGI.
3. **A deduction for non-itemizers.** A taxpayer who takes the standard deduction may deduct up to \$1,000 (\$2,000 MFJ) of cash gifts to public charities. Gifts to donor-advised funds and supporting organizations do not qualify.

Every gift needs records. A gift of \$250 or more requires a written acknowledgment from the charity, noncash gifts over \$500 are reported on Form 8283, and noncash gifts over \$5,000 (other than publicly traded securities) generally require a qualified appraisal.

> **Example (cash):** In 2026, Nora has AGI of \$80,000 and gives \$6,000 of cash to her church. The 60% limit (\$48,000) does not bind. The 0.5% floor is \$400, so if Nora itemizes she deducts **\$5,600**. If she takes the standard deduction instead, she can still deduct **\$1,000** as a non-itemizer.

> **Example (property):** In 2026, Hank has AGI of \$100,000. He gives a public charity inventory from his sole proprietorship with a basis of \$10,000 and an FMV of \$14,000, plus stock held for three years with an FMV of \$30,000. The inventory is ordinary income property, so it counts at its \$10,000 basis (limit \$50,000). The stock counts at its \$30,000 FMV (limit \$30,000). The \$40,000 total is within the limits, and after the \$500 floor (0.5% × \$100,000) Hank deducts **\$39,500**.

## Interest Expense

Whether an individual can deduct interest depends on what the borrowed money was used for. The categories most often tested are qualified residence interest and personal interest.

### Qualified Residence Interest

Interest on debt secured by a **qualified residence** (the main home and one other home) is an itemized deduction if the debt is **acquisition indebtedness**: debt incurred to buy, build, or substantially improve the residence that secures it. OBBBA made the post-2017 rules permanent:

- Interest is deductible on up to **\$750,000** of acquisition debt (\$375,000 MFS). Debt incurred on or before December 15, 2017 keeps the older \$1,000,000 limit (\$500,000 MFS).
- A home equity loan or line of credit is deductible only if its proceeds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home that secures it. Proceeds used to pay off credit cards, take a vacation, or buy a car produce nondeductible interest.
- Starting in 2026, **mortgage insurance premiums** are again treated as qualified residence interest. The deduction is reduced by 10% for each \$1,000 of AGI over \$100,000 (\$500 and \$50,000 for MFS).

> **Example:** David and Elena bought their home with a \$400,000 mortgage. In Year 4 they borrow \$45,000 on a home equity line of credit and use all of it to remodel the kitchen and replace the HVAC system. They also take out an \$8,000 unsecured personal loan to buy computer parts for a hobby. In Year 4 they pay \$22,000 of mortgage interest, \$3,100 of HELOC interest, and \$650 of personal loan interest.
>
> - The mortgage is acquisition debt, well under \$750,000, so the **\$22,000** is deductible.
> - The HELOC proceeds substantially improved the home that secures the loan, so the HELOC is also acquisition debt. Total acquisition debt of \$445,000 is still under the limit, so the **\$3,100** is deductible.
> - The personal loan is not secured by the home and was not used to improve it. The \$650 is nondeductible personal interest.
>
> Their deductible interest is \$22,000 + \$3,100 = **\$25,100**.

### Investment Interest

Interest on money borrowed to buy taxable investments is deductible only up to **net investment income**. Any excess carries forward indefinitely.

### Personal Interest

Personal (consumer) interest is **not deductible**. Common examples are credit card finance charges, interest on personal loans, and late-payment charges. Interest on a car loan was always in this group, but for 2025 through 2028 interest on a loan to buy a new personal vehicle qualifies for the separate car loan interest deduction described later in this chapter.

## Casualty and Theft Losses

A personal casualty or theft loss is deductible only if it results from a **federally declared disaster**, except to the extent of personal casualty gains. OBBBA made this rule permanent, and starting in 2026 losses from certain **state-declared disasters** also qualify.

The deductible loss is computed in four steps:

1. The loss is the **lesser** of the decline in the property's FMV or its adjusted basis.
2. Subtract insurance and other reimbursements.
3. Subtract **\$100** for each casualty event.
4. Subtract **10% of AGI** from the total of all personal casualty losses for the year.

**Example:** A federally declared tornado damages Victor's home, which has a basis of \$300,000. The home's FMV falls by \$40,000, and insurance pays \$25,000. Victor's AGI is \$90,000.

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Loss (lesser of \$40,000 FMV decline or \$300,000 basis) | \$40,000 |
| Less: insurance reimbursement | (\$25,000) |
| Less: \$100 per-event floor | (\$100) |
| Less: 10% of AGI (\$90,000 × 10%) | (\$9,000) |
| **Deductible casualty loss** | **\$5,900** |

Business casualty losses are not subject to the disaster requirement or to the \$100 and 10% floors.

A separate law, P.L. 119-108 (September 2026), gives more generous treatment to a **qualified disaster loss**: a loss from a major disaster whose incident period began between December 28, 2019 and December 31, 2026. The floor is \$500 per event, there is no 10%-of-AGI floor, and the loss is deductible even by a taxpayer who takes the standard deduction. If Victor's loss qualified, he would deduct \$15,000 - \$500 = \$14,500. Under the AICPA's six-month rule, these rules should become eligible for testing on April 1, 2027.

## Gambling Losses

Gambling winnings are included in gross income in full. Gambling losses are deductible only as an itemized deduction and only up to the year's gambling winnings; an excess cannot be carried to another year. Starting in 2026, OBBBA limits the deduction further: only **90%** of wagering losses (including a professional gambler's business expenses) can be deducted, still capped at winnings. As a result, a gambler who exactly breaks even now has taxable income.

> **Example:** In 2026, Lee wins \$10,000 at a casino and loses \$10,000 over the year. Lee includes \$10,000 in gross income and, if itemizing, deducts 90% × \$10,000 = \$9,000 of losses. The net \$1,000 is taxable even though Lee broke even. If Lee had lost \$15,000, the deduction would be the lesser of \$13,500 (90% × \$15,000) or the \$10,000 of winnings, so \$10,000.

## Overall Limit for the 37% Bracket

OBBBA permanently replaced the old "Pease" limitation with a simpler cap on the value of itemized deductions for taxpayers in the top bracket. Starting in 2026, itemized deductions are reduced by **2/37** of the lesser of (1) the itemized deductions or (2) the amount by which taxable income (computed before this limit) plus itemized deductions exceeds the start of the 37% bracket. The effect is that each dollar of itemized deductions saves a top-bracket taxpayer about 35 cents instead of 37. The limit is applied after every other limit, such as the SALT cap and the charitable floor.

> **Example:** In 2026, Theo and Ana file jointly. Before this limit, their taxable income is \$900,000 and their itemized deductions are \$100,000. Taxable income plus itemized deductions is \$1,000,000, which is \$231,300 above the \$768,700 start of the 2026 joint 37% bracket. The reduction is 2/37 of the lesser of \$100,000 or \$231,300: 2/37 × \$100,000 = **\$5,405**.

## Deductions Available With or Without Itemizing

A few deductions are subtracted from AGI in addition to the standard or itemized deduction. They reduce taxable income but not AGI, so they do not help with AGI-based floors and phase-outs. The permanent one is the QBI deduction. OBBBA added four temporary ones for 2025 through 2028: the senior deduction and the deductions for qualified tips, qualified overtime, and car loan interest. All four are claimed on Schedule 1-A, use MAGI (AGI plus certain excluded foreign income) for their phase-outs, and have dollar amounts fixed by statute rather than indexed.

### Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction

Section 199A lets owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships, S corporations, and certain trusts deduct up to **20%** of their qualified business income. OBBBA made the deduction permanent at 20%.

**What counts as QBI.** QBI is the net ordinary income from a qualified trade or business conducted in the United States. It does not include investment items (capital gains, dividends, and interest not allocable to the business), wages earned as an employee, reasonable compensation paid to an S corporation shareholder-employee, guaranteed payments to a partner, or qualified tips deducted under the tips deduction. A net QBI loss carries forward and reduces next year's QBI.

**The basic rule.** The deduction is the **lesser** of 20% of QBI or 20% of taxable income before the QBI deduction (minus net capital gain). The examples below assume no capital gains.

**Limits at higher incomes.** Once taxable income exceeds an indexed threshold (\$201,750 single and \$403,500 MFJ for 2026), two limits phase in over the next **\$75,000 (\$150,000 MFJ)**. OBBBA widened that range from \$50,000 (\$100,000) starting in 2026.

1. **SSTB limit.** A **specified service trade or business (SSTB)** is one in health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial or brokerage services, investing and investment management, or any business whose principal asset is the reputation or skill of its owners or employees. Engineering and architecture are not SSTBs. Above the phase-in range, SSTB income produces no deduction.
2. **W-2 wage and property limit.** For any other business, the deduction for that business cannot exceed the **greater** of 50% of the W-2 wages it paid, or 25% of those wages plus 2.5% of the unadjusted basis immediately after acquisition (UBIA) of its qualified property.

Within the phase-in range, both limits apply only partly; an exam question in that range supplies the figures needed.

**Minimum deduction.** Starting in 2026, a taxpayer with at least \$1,000 of QBI from businesses in which the taxpayer materially participates gets a deduction of at least **\$400**. Both amounts are indexed after 2026.

> **Example 1 (below the threshold):** Elise, who files single, is allocated \$100,000 of QBI from Grizzly Partners' landscaping business. Her taxable income before the QBI deduction is \$150,000. Her deduction is the lesser of 20% × \$100,000 = \$20,000 or 20% × \$150,000 = \$30,000, so **\$20,000**. At her income, the SSTB and wage limits do not apply.

> **Example 2 (taxable income limit):** Cub Records, a sole proprietorship, produces \$200,000 of QBI, but its owner's taxable income before the deduction is only \$180,000. The deduction is the lesser of \$40,000 or \$36,000, so **\$36,000**.

> **Example 3 (above the range):** In 2026, Alex and Jordan file jointly with taxable income of \$700,000, above the \$553,500 top of the joint phase-in range. Their manufacturing business (not an SSTB) has QBI of \$400,000, W-2 wages of \$60,000, and UBIA of \$2,000,000. The tentative deduction is 20% × \$400,000 = \$80,000. The wage and property limit is the greater of 50% × \$60,000 = \$30,000 or (25% × \$60,000) + (2.5% × \$2,000,000) = \$65,000. Their deduction is **\$65,000**, well under 20% of taxable income (\$140,000). If the business were an accounting practice, an SSTB, the deduction would be zero.

:::tip[CPA Exam Strategy]

Work a QBI question in this order:

1. Is the income QBI from a qualified trade or business?
2. Is taxable income at or below the threshold? If so, the deduction is 20% of QBI, limited to 20% of taxable income.
3. Is the business an SSTB with taxable income above the phase-in range? If so, the deduction is zero.
4. Otherwise, above the range, apply the W-2 wage and property limit to each business.

:::

### Senior Deduction (2025-2028)

For 2025 through 2028, each taxpayer who is 65 or older by year-end may deduct an extra **\$6,000**, on top of the additional standard deduction for age. On a joint return, each qualifying spouse gets one. Each \$6,000 is reduced by 6% of MAGI over \$75,000 (\$150,000 MFJ), so it disappears at MAGI of \$175,000 for a single filer and \$250,000 on a joint return. The qualifying person's SSN must be on the return, and married taxpayers must file jointly. The deduction does not change how Social Security benefits are taxed.

> **Example:** Ruth, who is single and 70, has MAGI of \$95,000. Her senior deduction is \$6,000 - 6% × (\$95,000 - \$75,000) = \$6,000 - \$1,200 = **\$4,800**.

### Qualified Tips and Qualified Overtime (2025-2028)

These two deductions take part of a worker's pay out of taxable income. The pay itself is still gross income and is still subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes and to withholding; only the income tax deduction is new.

**Qualified tips (§224).** A worker may deduct up to **\$25,000** per return of qualified tips: voluntary cash or charged tips, including tip-pool shares, received in an occupation that Treasury lists as customarily receiving tips. The tips must be reported on a Form W-2, a Form 1099, or Form 4137, and they cannot be received in an SSTB. A mandatory service charge is not a tip. A self-employed worker's deduction cannot exceed the net income of the business, and tips deducted this way are not QBI.

**Qualified overtime (§225).** An employee may deduct up to **\$12,500 (\$25,000 MFJ)** of qualified overtime compensation. Only the premium required by the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) counts: the "half" in time-and-a-half. The regular rate paid for overtime hours does not count, and neither does overtime paid only because of state law, a union contract, or employer policy, or any premium above the FLSA half-time amount. An employee who is exempt from the FLSA overtime rules gets no deduction.

**Shared rules.** Each deduction is reduced by \$100 for every \$1,000 of MAGI over \$150,000 (\$300,000 MFJ). The worker's SSN must be on the return, and married taxpayers must file jointly.

**How the exam treats wages.** The REG blueprint tells candidates to assume that references to an individual's wages or compensation do **not** include tips or overtime unless the question says otherwise. A stated salary is just salary, so apply these deductions only when a question gives tip or overtime amounts separately.

> **Example (tips):** Kai, a single restaurant server, has \$18,000 of qualified tips and MAGI of \$60,000. He deducts **\$18,000**.

> **Example (overtime):** Mia, a single warehouse employee with a regular rate of \$30 an hour, works 200 overtime hours at \$45 an hour, for \$9,000 of overtime pay. Her qualified overtime is only the premium: 200 × \$15 = **\$3,000** (one third of the \$9,000). If her MAGI were \$170,000, the deduction would be reduced by 20 × \$100 = \$2,000, to \$1,000.

### Car Loan Interest (2025-2028)

For 2025 through 2028, an individual may deduct up to **\$10,000** a year of interest on a loan used to buy a new vehicle for personal use. The loan must be incurred after 2024 and secured by a first lien on the vehicle. The vehicle must be a car, minivan, van, SUV, pickup, or motorcycle under 14,000 pounds, its final assembly must have occurred in the United States, and its original use must begin with the taxpayer. Leases, used vehicles, and commercial vehicles do not qualify, and the vehicle identification number (VIN) must be on the return. The deduction is reduced by \$200 for each \$1,000 (or part of \$1,000) of MAGI over \$100,000 (\$200,000 MFJ).

> **Example:** In 2026, Omar, who files single, pays \$4,000 of interest on a loan for a new U.S.-assembled pickup that he uses personally. His MAGI is \$110,000, so the deduction is reduced by 10 × \$200 = \$2,000, leaving **\$2,000**.
