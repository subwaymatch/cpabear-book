# Gross Income

Gross income is where every individual return begins. IRC §61 defines it as "all income from whatever source derived," so the working rule is simple: an increase in wealth is taxable unless a specific provision excludes it. That is why the exam's gross income questions are mostly about the exceptions.

This chapter starts with who must file, then works through the inclusions the exam tests most, the rules for capital gains (including digital assets), the major exclusions, the doctrines that decide whose income it is and when, and the final return in the year of death. It reflects the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025), whose provisions with 2024 or 2025 effective dates have been eligible for REG testing since July 1, 2026. Filing status and the dependency tests are covered in the next chapter, [Filing Status and Dependency](./filing-status.md).

---

## Who Must File

An individual generally must file a return when gross income reaches the standard deduction for his or her filing status, including the additional amount for age 65 or older (but not the additional amount for blindness). Because the standard deduction is indexed, so are these thresholds. Three special cases are worth remembering:

- A **married filing separately** taxpayer must file with gross income of just \$5, so nearly every MFS taxpayer files.
- A taxpayer with **net self-employment earnings of \$400 or more** must file, whatever his or her gross income, because self-employment tax is owed.
- A **dependent's** filing threshold depends on the mix of earned and unearned income, because it is based on the dependent's limited standard deduction.

A taxpayer below the threshold should still file to recover withheld tax or to claim a refundable credit.

## Compensation for Services

### Wages, Salaries, Bonuses, and Tips

All compensation for services is gross income, whether it is paid in cash, in property, or in digital assets (valued at fair market value). Tips are included, and an employee who receives \$20 or more of cash tips in a month must report them to the employer. Overtime pay is wages like any other pay.

The deductions for qualified tips and qualified overtime that apply for 2025 through 2028 do not exclude anything from gross income. They are subtracted later in computing taxable income (see [Adjustments and Deductions](./adjustments-and-deductions.md)), and the tips and overtime remain subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes.

> **Example:** Polar Co. pays its controller a \$120,000 salary and also pays for a \$5,000 country club membership that the controller uses for personal recreation. The controller's gross income is \$125,000.

### Fringe Benefits

A fringe benefit is taxable compensation unless a specific section excludes it. The exclusions the exam uses most are:

| Benefit | Exclusion |
|---|---|
| Employer-paid health insurance and medical reimbursements (§§105, 106) | Fully excluded |
| Group-term life insurance (§79) | Cost of the first \$50,000 of coverage |
| Dependent care assistance (§129) | \$5,000 a year, rising to \$7,500 (\$3,750 MFS) starting in 2026 |
| Educational assistance (§127) | \$5,250 a year, including employer payments on the employee's student loans (made permanent by OBBBA) |
| Qualified transportation: transit passes and parking (§132(f)) | Indexed monthly limit (\$340 for 2026) |
| No-additional-cost services, employee discounts, working condition and de minimis fringes (§132) | Excluded |
| Meals and lodging on the employer's premises for the employer's convenience (§119) | Excluded |
| Employer contributions to a Trump account (§128) | Up to \$2,500 a year per employee, starting in 2026 |

OBBBA also permanently ended two exclusions. Bicycle commuting reimbursements are now taxable, and so are moving expense reimbursements, except for members of the Armed Forces and certain intelligence-community employees.

### Guaranteed Payments From a Partnership

A **guaranteed payment** is a partnership's payment to a partner for services or for the use of capital, fixed without regard to partnership income. It is ordinary income to the partner, reported on Schedule E, whether or not the partnership has income for the year, and a guaranteed payment for services is subject to self-employment tax. See [Pass-Through Entity Reporting](./pass-through-entity-reporting.md).

## Investment Income

### Interest

Interest on bank accounts, certificates of deposit, corporate bonds, and U.S. Treasury obligations is taxable. Interest on **state and local (municipal) bonds** is excluded under §103, although interest on private activity bonds can be an AMT preference. Treasury interest runs the other way at the state level: it is taxable federally but exempt from state income tax.

### Dividends

**Qualified dividends** are taxed at the long-term capital gain rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%. To qualify, the dividend must come from a U.S. corporation (or a qualifying foreign corporation), and the shareholder must have held the stock for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. Other dividends are taxed at ordinary rates.

### Rents

Rental income is included in gross income and reduced by rental expenses such as mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, repairs, and depreciation. A net rental loss is generally passive.

> **Example:** Kodiak Inc.'s sole shareholder owns a duplex that produces \$18,000 of rent and \$22,000 of deductible expenses, a \$4,000 loss. Because the owner actively participates and has AGI under \$100,000, the loss can offset nonpassive income under the \$25,000 rental allowance described in [Loss Limitations](./loss-limitations.md).

## Retirement Income and Social Security

Distributions from a traditional IRA, a 401(k), or a pension are ordinary income, except for any part that returns the taxpayer's after-tax contributions (basis). A **qualified Roth distribution** (the account has been open at least 5 years and the owner is 59½ or older, disabled, or deceased) is entirely tax free. A distribution before age 59½ generally also carries a 10% additional tax, with exceptions for death, disability, and a series of substantially equal periodic payments, among others. A rollover completed within 60 days is not taxable.

**Social Security benefits** are partly taxable once **provisional income** (AGI before the benefits, plus tax-exempt interest, plus half of the benefits) passes a base amount. The base amounts are fixed by statute:

| Provisional Income | Single, HOH | MFJ | Portion of Benefits Taxable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below the first base amount | Under \$25,000 | Under \$32,000 | None |
| Between the base amounts | \$25,000 to \$34,000 | \$32,000 to \$44,000 | Up to 50% |
| Above the second base amount | Over \$34,000 | Over \$44,000 | Up to 85% |

An MFS filer who lived with his or her spouse at any time during the year has a base amount of zero, so up to 85% of benefits are taxable. The new senior deduction did not change these rules.

## Other Common Inclusions

**Alimony** received under a divorce or separation instrument executed before 2019 is income; under a later instrument it is not. See [Adjustments and Deductions](./adjustments-and-deductions.md) for the full rules.

**Unemployment compensation** is fully taxable.

**Prizes and awards** are taxable at fair market value, unless the winner of certain achievement awards assigns the award directly to a charity.

**Gambling winnings** are included in full. Gambling losses are deductible only as an itemized deduction, limited to winnings, and starting in 2026 only 90% of losses count.

**Damages** depend on what they compensate. Compensatory damages for a **personal physical injury or physical sickness** are excluded under §104(a)(2). Damages for emotional distress not caused by a physical injury, for lost profits, or for back pay are taxable. **Punitive damages** are always taxable, even in a physical injury case.

> **Example:** Dana is injured in a car accident and receives \$300,000 of compensatory damages for her injuries plus \$100,000 of punitive damages. She excludes the \$300,000 and includes the \$100,000 in gross income.

**Discharge of indebtedness.** When a lender forgives debt, the borrower generally has gross income equal to the amount forgiven. The main exclusions apply when the debt is discharged in a Title 11 bankruptcy; when the taxpayer is insolvent (to the extent of the insolvency); for qualified farm debt or qualified real property business debt; and for qualified principal residence debt within the dates Congress has set. Student loans discharged because of the student's death or total and permanent disability are permanently excluded starting in 2026, but the broader exclusion for most other student loan discharges ended after 2025. A taxpayer who excludes canceled debt generally reduces tax attributes such as NOLs and basis.

> **Example:** Ivan owes \$200,000 on a business loan, and the lender accepts \$150,000 in full settlement, canceling \$50,000. Just before the discharge, Ivan has assets of \$500,000 and liabilities of \$600,000, so he is insolvent by \$100,000. Because his insolvency exceeds the \$50,000 discharged, he excludes the entire \$50,000.

## Digital Assets

The 2027 REG blueprint names digital assets in its capital gain task. The IRS treats a **digital asset** (convertible virtual currency such as bitcoin, a stablecoin, or a non-fungible token) as **property**, not currency. That one rule explains most of the results.

**Ordinary income when received.** Digital assets paid as wages are W-2 wages, and digital assets paid to an independent contractor are self-employment income on Schedule C. Staking and mining rewards, and new tokens received in a hard-fork airdrop, are ordinary income equal to their fair market value when the taxpayer gains control of them. That value becomes the taxpayer's basis in the units received.

**Capital gain or loss on disposition.** Selling a digital asset for dollars, exchanging it for a different digital asset, or spending it on goods or services is a taxable disposition. The gain or loss is the amount realized minus basis (generally cost plus fees), and it is long-term if the asset was held more than one year. A like-kind exchange is not available, because §1031 applies only to real property. An NFT that represents a collectible can be taxed at the 28% collectibles rate. Buying a digital asset with dollars, holding it, or moving it between one's own wallets is not a taxable event.

**Reporting.** Brokers report sales on **Form 1099-DA** (gross proceeds since 2025, and basis for assets acquired in the account starting in 2026), the taxpayer reports each sale on Form 8949 and Schedule D, and every Form 1040 asks a yes-or-no question about digital asset transactions. When reviewing a return, compare the Forms 1099-DA and the taxpayer's own records against the gains reported, and remember that income received in digital assets (wages, staking rewards) does not appear on a 1099-DA at all. [Gains and Losses](../property-transactions/gains-and-losses.md) covers basis tracking, reporting, and the wash sale rule in more detail.

> **Example:** In June 2025, Marcus buys 10 units of a digital asset for \$5,000 plus a \$50 fee, a basis of \$5,050. In August 2026 he exchanges all 10 units for a different token worth \$8,000. He has a **long-term capital gain of \$2,950** (\$8,000 - \$5,050), and his basis in the new token is \$8,000. In September 2026 he receives staking rewards worth \$300; he reports **\$300 of ordinary income** and takes a \$300 basis in those units.

## Capital Gains, Gifts, and Inherited Property

Gains from selling stocks, bonds, digital assets, and other investments are included in gross income. Each gain is classified as **long-term** if the asset was held more than one year and **short-term** otherwise, and the classification drives the netting process and the tax rate. How the taxpayer acquired the asset can change both basis and holding period:

- **Gift:** the recipient generally takes the donor's basis, and the donor's holding period tacks on. If the property was worth less than the donor's basis at the time of the gift, a sale at a loss uses that lower fair market value instead.
- **Inheritance:** basis is generally the fair market value at the date of death, and the holding period is automatically long-term.

> **Example:** Lena receives stock as a gift. The donor's basis was \$4,000, the donor held it for three years, and it was worth \$9,000 on the date of the gift. Two months later Lena sells it for \$10,000, a long-term gain of \$6,000. Lena also inherits stock worth \$20,000 at the owner's death (the decedent's basis was \$5,000) and sells it a month later for \$21,000, a long-term gain of \$1,000.

See [Determination of Basis](../property-transactions/determination-of-basis.md) and [Gains and Losses](../property-transactions/gains-and-losses.md) for the full basis rules and the netting of gains and losses.

## Major Exclusions

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Municipal bond interest (§103) | Excluded (private activity bond interest may be an AMT preference) |
| Gifts and inheritances (§102) | The property received is excluded; income it later earns is taxable |
| Life insurance proceeds paid at death (§101) | Excluded. If the policy was sold for value, proceeds above the buyer's cost and later premiums are taxable. Interest on proceeds paid in installments is taxable. |
| Scholarships (§117) | Amounts for tuition, required fees, books, supplies, and equipment are excluded for a degree candidate. Amounts for room and board, or paid for teaching or other services, are taxable. |
| Damages for physical injury or sickness (§104) | Excluded (punitive damages are not) |
| Qualified 529 plan distributions (§529) | Excluded when used for qualified education expenses (see below) |
| HSA distributions for medical expenses (§223) | Excluded |
| Qualifying fringe benefits | Excluded within the limits shown above |

For **529 plans**, OBBBA broadened what counts as a qualified expense. Since July 4, 2025, K-12 expenses beyond tuition (curriculum and books, tutoring, standardized test fees, and educational therapies for students with disabilities) and recognized postsecondary credential programs qualify. Starting in 2026, the annual limit on K-12 distributions rises from \$10,000 to \$20,000.

## Timing and Attribution Rules

### Constructive Receipt

Income is taxable when it is credited to the taxpayer's account, set apart, or otherwise made available without substantial restriction, even if the taxpayer has not physically received it. A taxpayer cannot defer income by declining to pick it up.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

A year-end bonus check that is available for pickup on December 30 is taxable that year even if the employee waits until January 2 to collect it.

:::

### Assignment of Income

Income is taxed to the person who **earns** it. In *Lucas v. Earl* (1930), the Supreme Court described this as taxing the fruit to the tree that grew it. A taxpayer cannot shift income by directing that it be paid to someone else.

> **Example:** Salmon Entertainment's lead performer directs that \$50,000 of her concert fees be paid directly to her brother. She still includes the \$50,000 in her own gross income.

### Community Property

In the community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin), income earned during the marriage generally belongs half to each spouse. Spouses who file separately each report half of the community income.

### The Tax Benefit Rule

Under §111, recovering an amount that was deducted in an earlier year is income only to the extent the earlier deduction **reduced tax**. The exam usually tests this with a **state income tax refund**.

- If the taxpayer took the standard deduction in the year the tax was paid, the refund is not taxable, because the tax was never deducted.
- If the taxpayer itemized, the taxable portion is the **lesser** of the refund or the amount by which the prior year's itemized deductions exceeded the standard deduction.

> **Example:** Two taxpayers each receive an \$800 state income tax refund this year. Assume each was entitled to a \$15,000 standard deduction last year. Taxpayer A itemized \$15,500 last year, \$500 more than the standard deduction, so only **\$500** of the refund is taxable. Taxpayer B itemized \$16,500, a \$1,500 benefit, so the entire **\$800** is taxable.

The SALT cap adds one more step. Tax paid above the cap was never deducted, so the part of a refund that merely reverses that excess is not income. Suppose a taxpayer paid \$45,000 of state and local taxes last year but could deduct only the \$40,400 cap. Of a \$6,000 refund, the first \$4,600 reverses tax that was never deducted, so at most \$1,400 is taxable, and then only to the extent it passes the lesser-of test above.

## Income in the Year of Death

When a taxpayer dies, a **final Form 1040** covers the period from January 1 through the date of death. For a cash-method taxpayer, it includes only income actually or constructively received before death. Income the decedent had earned but not yet received, such as a final paycheck, accrued interest, or an IRA balance, is **income in respect of a decedent (IRD)**. IRD is taxed to whoever receives it (the estate or an heir), keeps the character it would have had to the decedent, and gets no step-up in basis. A surviving spouse may still file a joint return for the year of death.

> **Example:** Walt, a cash-method taxpayer, dies on June 30. Before his death he received \$60,000 of salary and \$1,000 of interest. In July his employer pays his final \$5,000 paycheck to his estate. Walt's final return reports \$61,000. The \$5,000 is IRD, taxable to the estate.

## Income From Pass-Through Entities

Owners of S corporations, partnerships, and LLCs taxed as partnerships report their share of the entity's income whether or not any cash is distributed, and each item keeps its character (ordinary income, capital gain, tax-exempt interest) on the owner's return.

> **Example:** Grizzly Partners earns \$300,000 of ordinary business income and \$40,000 of long-term capital gain. A 25% partner reports \$75,000 of ordinary income and \$10,000 of long-term capital gain, regardless of distributions.

See [Pass-Through Entity Reporting](./pass-through-entity-reporting.md) for how Schedule K-1 items flow to Form 1040.

## Summary

| Concept | Key Rule |
|---|---|
| IRC §61 | Gross income is all income from whatever source derived, unless excluded |
| Filing threshold | Generally the standard deduction for the filing status; \$5 for MFS; \$400 of net SE earnings |
| Tips and overtime | Included in gross income; any deduction comes later |
| Fringe benefits | Taxable unless a specific section excludes them |
| Qualified dividends | Taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% |
| Social Security | Up to 85% taxable, based on provisional income |
| Punitive damages | Always taxable |
| Digital assets | Property: income at FMV when received; gain or loss on sale, exchange, or spending |
| Gifts and inheritances | Excluded from income; carryover basis for gifts, FMV basis for inherited property |
| Constructive receipt | Income is taxable when made available, not when collected |
| Assignment of income | Income is taxed to the person who earns it |
| Tax benefit rule | A recovery is taxable only to the extent the earlier deduction reduced tax |
| Year of death | Final return through date of death; IRD is taxed to the recipient |
