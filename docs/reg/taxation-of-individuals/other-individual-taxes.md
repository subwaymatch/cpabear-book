# Other Individual Taxes

The regular income tax is not the only tax on an individual return. Self-employed people owe self-employment tax, high earners can owe two Medicare surtaxes, a child's investment income can be taxed at the parent's rate, and almost everyone without enough withholding must make estimated payments during the year. The exam tests each of these separately, usually with a short computation.

This chapter covers them in that order: **self-employment tax**, the **additional Medicare tax**, the **net investment income tax**, the **kiddie tax**, and **estimated tax** with its safe harbors.

---

## Self-Employment Tax

**Self-employment (SE) tax** is the self-employed person's version of the Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes that an employer and employee split. Because there is no employer, the self-employed person pays both halves.

| Component | Rate | Applies To |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security (OASDI) | 12.4% | Earnings up to the wage base (indexed; \$184,500 for 2026) |
| Medicare (HI) | 2.9% | All earnings, with no cap |
| **Total** | **15.3%** | |

SE tax is computed on **92.35%** of net earnings from self-employment (for a sole proprietor, Schedule C net profit). The 92.35% factor stands in for the deduction an employer would get for its half of FICA. If the taxpayer also has W-2 wages, those wages use up part of the Social Security wage base first, so less SE income is exposed to the 12.4% portion. The 2.9% Medicare portion always applies in full.

The taxpayer then deducts **half of the SE tax** as an adjustment to income on Schedule 1. That deduction lowers AGI but does not lower the earnings subject to SE tax.

> **Example:** Jamie runs a consulting business as a sole proprietor and has \$120,000 of net profit on Schedule C and no wages.
>
> 1. SE earnings: \$120,000 × 92.35% = \$110,820
> 2. SE tax: \$110,820 × 15.3% = **\$16,955**
> 3. Deduction for half of SE tax: \$16,955 × 50% = **\$8,478**
>
> Jamie reports \$16,955 of SE tax on Schedule SE and an \$8,478 adjustment to income on Schedule 1.

Tips and overtime are earnings like any other for these taxes. The income tax deductions for qualified tips and qualified overtime do not reduce FICA or SE tax.

## Additional Medicare Tax

An **additional 0.9% Medicare tax** applies to wages and self-employment income above a threshold. The thresholds are fixed by statute and are not indexed, so they can be tested.

| Filing Status | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Married filing jointly | \$250,000 |
| Married filing separately | \$125,000 |
| All others (single, HOH, QSS) | \$200,000 |

An employer must withhold the extra 0.9% on an employee's wages above \$200,000, whatever the employee's filing status, and the employee settles any difference on Form 8959. There is **no employer match** for this tax. A self-employed person combines wages and SE income in applying the threshold.

> **Example:** Sarah, who files single, earns \$280,000 of W-2 wages. Her additional Medicare tax is (\$280,000 - \$200,000) × 0.9% = **\$720**, which her employer withholds during the year.

## Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

The **net investment income tax** is a **3.8%** tax on the **lesser** of (1) net investment income or (2) modified AGI above a threshold. The thresholds are fixed by statute: \$250,000 for MFJ and a qualifying surviving spouse, \$125,000 for MFS, and \$200,000 for everyone else. They match the additional Medicare tax thresholds except for a qualifying surviving spouse, who uses \$200,000 for the Medicare tax but \$250,000 here.

**Net investment income** includes interest, dividends, annuities, royalties, and rents; net capital gains from stocks, bonds, digital assets, and investment real estate; and income from businesses in which the taxpayer does not materially participate. It does **not** include wages, self-employment income, Social Security benefits, tax-exempt interest, distributions from qualified retirement plans and IRAs, or income from a business in which the taxpayer materially participates.

$$
\text{NIIT} = 3.8\% \times \min(\text{Net investment income},\ \text{MAGI} - \text{Threshold})
$$

> **Example:** Marcus and Priya file jointly with MAGI of \$310,000, including \$45,000 of dividends, capital gains, and passive rental income. Their MAGI exceeds the threshold by \$60,000. The tax applies to the lesser amount, \$45,000: 3.8% × \$45,000 = **\$1,710**, reported on Form 8960.

A high-income taxpayer can owe all three taxes at once: regular income tax, the 3.8% NIIT on investment income, and the 0.9% additional Medicare tax on earned income.

## Kiddie Tax

The **kiddie tax** keeps parents from shifting investment income to a child in a lower bracket. A child's **net unearned income** above a threshold is taxed at the **parent's** marginal rate.

The kiddie tax applies to a child who has unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains) above the threshold, does not file a joint return, has at least one living parent, and at year-end is:

- under age 18, or
- age 18, with earned income not more than half of his or her own support, or
- a full-time student age 19 through 23, with earned income not more than half of his or her own support.

The threshold works in layers, using an indexed base amount (\$1,350 for 2026):

| Unearned Income | Treatment |
|---|---|
| First \$1,350 | Offset by the child's standard deduction (tax free) |
| Next \$1,350 | Taxed at the child's own rate |
| Above \$2,700 | Taxed at the parent's rate |

A dependent child's standard deduction is the greater of the base amount or earned income plus \$450, so a child with earned income can shelter more of his or her own income. Earned income itself, such as wages from a summer job, is always taxed at the child's own rate.

> **Example:** Derek, age 16, is claimed as a dependent by his parents. In 2026 he has no earned income and \$5,700 of interest and dividends. The first \$1,350 is tax free, the next \$1,350 is taxed at Derek's 10% rate, and the remaining \$3,000 is taxed at his parents' rate. If his parents are in the 32% bracket, that \$3,000 is taxed at 32% instead of 10%.

## Estimated Tax and the Underpayment Penalty

A taxpayer whose withholding does not cover his or her tax, typically a self-employed person or an investor, must make **quarterly estimated tax payments**.

| Installment | Covers Income Earned | Due Date |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | January 1 to March 31 | April 15 |
| 2nd | April 1 to May 31 | June 15 |
| 3rd | June 1 to August 31 | September 15 |
| 4th | September 1 to December 31 | January 15 of the next year |

### When the Penalty Applies

No penalty applies if the tax owed after withholding and refundable credits is **less than \$1,000**. Otherwise, the taxpayer avoids the penalty by paying, through withholding and timely estimates, at least the **lesser** of:

| Safe Harbor | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Current year | **90%** of this year's tax |
| Prior year | **100%** of last year's tax |
| Prior year, high income | **110%** of last year's tax if last year's AGI exceeded \$150,000 (\$75,000 MFS) |

The prior-year safe harbor requires that a prior-year return was filed for a full 12-month year. A taxpayer who had **no** tax liability for a full prior year (and was a U.S. citizen or resident) owes no penalty at all.

The penalty works like interest. It is figured separately for each installment, at the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, so a large payment in the fourth quarter does not cure an underpayment in the first. The penalty is not deductible. Withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year unless the taxpayer elects otherwise, which is why extra withholding late in the year can fix earlier shortfalls when estimates cannot.

> **Example:** Nadia is self-employed and expects 2026 tax of \$20,000. Her 2025 tax was \$18,000, and her 2025 AGI was under \$150,000. The required annual payment is the lesser of 90% × \$20,000 = \$18,000 or 100% × \$18,000 = \$18,000. She should pay at least **\$4,500** (\$18,000 ÷ 4) by each due date. If her 2025 AGI had been over \$150,000, the prior-year safe harbor would be 110% × \$18,000 = \$19,800, and the current-year figure of \$18,000 would be the lower requirement.

:::tip[CPA Exam Strategy]

On an estimated tax question, check the safe harbors first. If the taxpayer's payments meet the 90% current-year test or the 100% (or 110%) prior-year test, installment by installment, there is no penalty, however much tax is due with the return.

:::
