# Communications with Audit Committees

The auditor works for the shareholders, but day to day the auditor deals with management, the very people whose financial statements are being audited. The audit committee closes that gap. It oversees the financial reporting process, and it can do so only if the auditor tells it what the audit found. This chapter covers what the auditor must communicate under AU-C 260 (nonissuers) and PCAOB AS 1301 (issuers), the special rules for uncorrected misstatements and independence, and the form and timing of the communications.

**Those charged with governance** are the people responsible for overseeing the entity's strategic direction and its accountability, including the financial reporting process. In a public company this is usually the **audit committee** of the board. In a small private company it may be the full board, or even the owner who also runs the business.

---

## What the Auditor Communicates

The required communications fall into four groups.

### The Auditor's Responsibilities

The auditor explains that it is responsible for forming and expressing an opinion on the financial statements, that the audit is designed to provide **reasonable, not absolute, assurance**, and that the audit does not relieve management or those charged with governance of their own responsibilities. The auditor also explains that it considers internal control to design its procedures, not to express an opinion on internal control (unless it is performing an integrated audit). These points are usually covered in the engagement letter, which can be shared with the committee.

Setting expectations early matters. If a board member believes the auditor guarantees that the statements are accurate, the right answer is that the auditor provides reasonable assurance, and the communication should say so.

### Planned Scope and Timing

The auditor gives an overview of the audit: how it plans to address the **significant risks** it has identified, including the risk of fraud; its approach to internal control; the timing of the work; and, where helpful, the materiality it is using and its use of specialists or other auditors. The auditor keeps this overview general enough that it does not undermine the audit. Telling the committee exactly which locations will receive surprise inventory counts, for example, would make the counts predictable.

**Example.** Polar Inc.'s auditor tells the audit committee that it will take a fully substantive approach to accounts receivable this year, rather than relying on controls, because turnover in the billing department has raised the risk of error.

### Significant Findings from the Audit

This is the heart of the communication. The auditor discusses:

- **Qualitative aspects of the entity's accounting practices**: the appropriateness of significant accounting policies, how management makes significant estimates, the clarity and neutrality of the disclosures, and significant unusual transactions.
- **Significant difficulties encountered**, such as long delays in getting information, an unreasonably short deadline, or management's unwillingness to provide its going concern evaluation.
- **Disagreements with management** about accounting, the scope of the audit, or the wording of the report, **whether or not they were resolved**. A disagreement resolved in the auditor's favor still tells the committee something about how management approaches its reporting.
- **Uncorrected misstatements** (discussed below).
- **Other significant matters**, such as circumstances that will change the form or content of the auditor's report, the written representations the auditor is requesting, and matters discussed with management about the auditor's appointment or retention.

Fraud and internal control deficiencies have their own communication requirements. Fraud involving management or employees with significant roles in internal control, and any other fraud that causes a material misstatement, is communicated to those charged with governance. Significant deficiencies and material weaknesses must be communicated **in writing** (see [Misstatements and Internal Control Deficiencies](./misstatements-and-internal-control-deficiencies.md)).

### Additional Communications for Issuers

AS 1301 requires a longer list for issuers. Among other items, the auditor communicates the company's **critical accounting policies and practices** and **critical accounting estimates**, its evaluation of the company's related party transactions and significant unusual transactions, difficult or contentious matters on which the auditor consulted outside the engagement team, and any consultations management had with other accountants. The auditor also provides the audit committee with a **draft of the auditor's report**, including the critical audit matters, and discusses it.

## Uncorrected Misstatements

The auditor accumulates every misstatement found during the audit other than those that are **clearly trivial**, and asks management to correct them. Those that management declines to correct are communicated to those charged with governance, along with:

- Each uncorrected misstatement and its effect, with material ones identified individually
- The **aggregate effect** on the financial statements, including the effect of uncorrected misstatements from prior periods
- A request that the uncorrected misstatements be corrected

**Example.** The auditor of Kodiak Partners finds three misstatements totaling \$85,000 that management declines to correct. Each is immaterial, and so is their total. The auditor still communicates all three to the audit committee, with the reasoning for treating them as immaterial. The committee may direct management to correct them anyway.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

The auditor communicates uncorrected misstatements whether or not they are material. The cutoff is **clearly trivial**, not materiality.

:::

## Independence Communications

For **issuers**, PCAOB Rule 3526 requires the auditor, before accepting an initial engagement and at least annually after that, to:

- Describe **in writing** all relationships between the firm (and its affiliates) and the company (or persons in financial reporting oversight roles) that may reasonably be thought to bear on independence
- **Discuss** with the audit committee the potential effects of those relationships on independence
- **Affirm in writing** that the firm is independent under PCAOB rules
- Document the discussion

The audit committee also pre-approves the audit and any permitted non-audit services the firm provides.

For **nonissuers**, AU-C 260 contains no comparable requirement for a written independence communication. Auditors of nonissuers may still discuss independence matters with those charged with governance, for example when nonattest services raise threats that the auditor has addressed with safeguards.

## Two-Way Communication

Communication runs both ways. Those charged with governance tell the auditor about matters relevant to the audit, such as their view of the entity's risks, any suspected fraud, and areas they want the auditor to focus on. The auditor evaluates whether this two-way communication has been **adequate**. If it has not, the auditor considers the effect on the risk assessment and on its ability to obtain sufficient evidence, and may need to take action, which can include modifying the opinion for a scope limitation or withdrawing from the engagement.

**Example.** In a private session, Cub Entertainment's audit committee chair tells the auditor that several employees have complained about the CFO overriding expense approval controls. The auditor adds this to the fraud risk assessment and expands testing of journal entries and expense reports approved by the CFO.

---

## Form and Timing

Under AICPA standards, significant findings must be communicated **in writing** when, in the auditor's judgment, oral communication would not be adequate. Significant deficiencies and material weaknesses are always communicated in writing. Other matters, such as the planned scope and timing, may be communicated orally, but the auditor documents what was said, to whom, and when. Under PCAOB standards, communications may be oral or written unless a rule requires writing (as Rule 3526 does for independence), and they must be made before the auditor's report is issued.

| Matter | Timing |
|---|---|
| Planned scope and timing | Early in the engagement, during planning |
| Significant findings and uncorrected misstatements | On a timely basis during the audit; issuer communications must be made before the report is issued |
| Independence (issuers) | Before accepting the engagement, and at least annually |
| Significant deficiencies and material weaknesses | Nonissuers: no later than **60 days** after the report release date. Issuers: before the report is issued |
