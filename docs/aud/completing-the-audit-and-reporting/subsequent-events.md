# Subsequent Events

The balance sheet captures one moment, but the auditor's report is usually dated weeks or months later, and business does not stop in between. A customer goes bankrupt, a lawsuit settles, a warehouse burns down. Some of these events tell us more about conditions that already existed at year-end; others are new. The first kind changes the numbers, the second kind may need to be disclosed, and the auditor has to look for both. This chapter explains the two types of subsequent events, the procedures the auditor performs to find them, how the report date limits the auditor's responsibility, what happens when the auditor learns of a fact after the report date, and when to dual date the report.

For nonissuers, the standard is **AU-C 560**, *Subsequent Events and Subsequently Discovered Facts*. For issuers, the corresponding standards are **AS 2801**, *Subsequent Events*, and **AS 2905**, *Subsequent Discovery of Facts Existing at the Date of the Auditor's Report*. The concepts are the same.

---

## Two Types of Subsequent Events

**Subsequent events** are events that occur between the date of the financial statements and the date of the auditor's report. The accounting rules (FASB ASC 855) sort them by one question: **did the condition exist at the balance sheet date?**

**Recognized subsequent events** (often called Type 1) provide additional evidence about conditions that **existed at the balance sheet date**. Because the condition was already there, the financial statements are **adjusted** to reflect the better information. Examples include:

- a customer that owed money at year-end files for bankruptcy because of financial problems that had been building before year-end;
- a lawsuit over an event that happened before year-end settles for an amount different from what was accrued; and
- the discovery of an error or fraud that already existed in the year-end balances.

**Nonrecognized subsequent events** (Type 2) relate to conditions that **arose after the balance sheet date**. The financial statements are **not adjusted**, but material events are **disclosed** so that the statements are not misleading. Examples include:

- a fire, flood, or other casualty after year-end;
- issuing stock or debt after year-end;
- a business combination completed after year-end;
- a decline in the market value of investments after year-end; and
- a lawsuit arising from an event that occurred after year-end.

The same kind of event can fall on either side of the line. A customer's bankruptcy is usually a recognized event, because the customer's weak finances existed at year-end. But if a customer that was healthy at year-end goes bankrupt because its only plant burned down in February, the condition arose after year-end and the event is nonrecognized.

| Feature | Recognized (Type 1) | Nonrecognized (Type 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Did the condition exist at the balance sheet date? | Yes | No |
| Treatment | **Adjust** the financial statements | **Disclose** if material; do not adjust |
| Example | Bankruptcy of a customer already in financial trouble at year-end | Fire that destroys a warehouse in February |

**Example:** Bear Co. has a \$2 million receivable at December 31, 20X4 from a customer that had been paying late for months. On January 20, 20X5, before the auditor's report is dated, the customer files for bankruptcy. The bankruptcy confirms that the receivable was impaired at year-end, so Bear Co. adjusts its allowance in the December 31 statements.

**Example:** On February 5, 20X5, a fire destroys Polar Inc.'s main warehouse. The warehouse was intact at December 31, so the 20X4 statements are not adjusted. Polar Inc. discloses the fire and an estimate of its financial effect.

The entity evaluates subsequent events through the date its financial statements are issued (or, for many nonpublic entities, available to be issued, a date they must disclose). The auditor's responsibility to search for them runs through the date of the auditor's report.

---

## Procedures to Identify Subsequent Events

The auditor performs procedures designed to identify subsequent events that require adjustment or disclosure, covering the period from the date of the financial statements through the date of the auditor's report, or as close to it as practicable. Under AU-C 560, these procedures include:

- obtaining an understanding of the procedures management has established to identify subsequent events;
- **inquiring of management** and, when appropriate, those charged with governance about whether any subsequent events have occurred;
- **reading the minutes** of meetings of the owners, management, and those charged with governance held after the balance sheet date, and asking about matters discussed at meetings for which minutes are not yet available; and
- **reading the entity's latest interim financial statements**, if any.

Two other procedures are timed to support the same period. The auditor's inquiry of the entity's **legal counsel** about litigation, claims, and assessments should be dated close to the report date, and management's **written representations** are dated as of the report date and include a representation that all subsequent events requiring adjustment or disclosure have been reflected.

Inquiries of management typically cover whether there have been:

- new commitments, borrowings, or guarantees;
- sales or acquisitions of assets, or plans to make them;
- issuances of stock or debt, or agreements to merge or liquidate;
- assets destroyed or appropriated, for example by fire, flood, or government action;
- developments in contingencies or in significant estimates;
- unusual accounting adjustments made or contemplated; and
- events that call into question the accounting policies used or the entity's ability to continue as a going concern.

**Example:** While performing subsequent events procedures for Kodiak Partners, the auditor reads the January and February board minutes. They show that the board approved the acquisition of a competitor on January 15, 20X5. The acquisition is a nonrecognized subsequent event, so the auditor confirms that it is disclosed in the December 31, 20X4 financial statements.

## The Report Date Ends the Search

The auditor's report is dated **no earlier than** the date on which the auditor has obtained sufficient appropriate evidence to support the opinion, including evidence that the complete financial statements (with notes) have been prepared and that management has taken responsibility for them. That date matters because it marks the end of the auditor's responsibility to search for subsequent events. After the report date, the auditor has **no obligation to perform any audit procedures** on the financial statements. The auditor responds only to facts that come to the auditor's attention.

Those facts, known as **subsequently discovered facts**, are facts that become known to the auditor after the report date and that, had they been known at that date, might have caused the auditor to revise the report. How the auditor responds depends on whether the report has been released.

### Before the Report Is Released

If such a fact comes to light after the report date but **before the report release date** (the date the auditor gives the entity permission to use the report), the auditor:

1. discusses the matter with management and, when appropriate, those charged with governance;
2. determines whether the financial statements need revision; and
3. if they do, asks how management intends to address the matter.

If management revises the financial statements, the auditor performs the necessary procedures on the revision and either dates the report as of a later date or dual dates it (both are explained below). If management does not make a revision the auditor believes is necessary, the auditor modifies the opinion before releasing the report.

### After the Report Is Released

If the auditor learns of such a fact **after the report has been released**, the steps begin the same way: discuss the matter with management and those charged with governance, determine whether the financial statements need revision, and if so ask how management intends to address it.

If management **revises** the financial statements, the auditor performs the necessary procedures on the revision and issues a new report on the revised statements. The new report includes an **emphasis-of-matter or other-matter paragraph** that refers to the note explaining the revision and to the auditor's earlier report. The auditor also assesses whether management's steps are timely and appropriate to inform anyone who received the original statements.

If management **does not take the necessary steps** (it neither revises the statements nor informs the people relying on them), the auditor notifies management and those charged with governance that the auditor will seek to prevent future reliance on the report. If they still do not act, the auditor takes appropriate action to prevent reliance, which may include notifying regulators and other known users, usually after consulting legal counsel.

**Example:** After Honey Entertainment's audited statements have been issued, the auditor learns that a large receivable was fictitious: a former employee fabricated it, and it never existed. Because the fact existed at the report date and would have changed the report, the auditor discusses it with management, which agrees to restate. The auditor audits the restatement, issues a new report on the revised statements, and confirms that management notifies the bank and other users who received the original statements.

:::warning

Do not confuse this situation with ordinary subsequent events. After the report date, the auditor has no duty to search for new information. The duty to act arises only when a fact that existed at the report date **comes to the auditor's attention**.

:::

## Dual Dating

Suppose a subsequent event comes to the auditor's attention after the report date but before the report is released, and management revises the financial statements to reflect it. The auditor has two choices for dating the report.

**Dual dating.** The report keeps its original date for everything except the revised matter, which receives a later date. For example: "March 5, 20X5, except for Note 15, as to which the date is March 22, 20X5." The auditor's procedures after the original date are limited to the revised matter, and so is the auditor's responsibility.

**A single later date.** The auditor redates the entire report as of the later date. This extends the auditor's responsibility for all subsequent events through the new date, so the auditor must extend the subsequent events procedures to that date.

| Approach | Report date | Auditor's responsibility after the original date |
|---|---|---|
| **Dual dating** | Original date, plus a later date for the specific matter | Limited to the specific matter |
| **Single later date** | Later date for the entire report | All subsequent events through the later date |

**Example:** Grizzly Industries' report was originally dated March 5, 20X5. On March 22, the auditor learns that a major lawsuit was filed on March 18 over an accident that happened in March. Grizzly adds a disclosure in Note 15. The auditor dual dates the report: "March 5, 20X5, except for Note 15, as to which the date is March 22, 20X5." The auditor's responsibility for other events still ends on March 5.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

Dual dating is usually preferred because it limits the auditor's added work and responsibility to one matter. Choosing a single later date is allowed, but it means extending all subsequent events procedures to the new date.

:::

## Summary

| Topic | Key point |
|---|---|
| **Recognized (Type 1) events** | Condition existed at the balance sheet date: **adjust** |
| **Nonrecognized (Type 2) events** | Condition arose after the balance sheet date: **disclose** if material |
| **Subsequent events procedures** | Inquiry of management, reading minutes and the latest interim statements, understanding management's procedures, plus legal letters and written representations dated near the report date |
| **Search period** | Balance sheet date through the date of the auditor's report |
| **After the report date** | No duty to search; respond to subsequently discovered facts that come to the auditor's attention |
| **After release, management cooperates** | Audit the revision and issue a new report that refers to the revision and to the earlier report |
| **After release, management refuses** | Notify management and governance, then act to prevent reliance on the report |
| **Dual dating** | Extends responsibility only for the specific matter |
| **Single later date** | Extends responsibility for all matters through the later date |
