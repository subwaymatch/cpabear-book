# Comparative Statements and Consistency

Most financial statements show two or more years side by side, and readers compare them. That comparison is only meaningful if the years were prepared on the same basis, so the auditor must evaluate, and sometimes report on, anything that breaks comparability. This chapter covers the consistency evaluation under AU-C 708 (and PCAOB AS 2820 for issuers), the reporting that follows a change in accounting principle or a restatement, how the auditor reports on prior periods in comparative statements, and what changes when the prior period was audited by someone else or was not audited at all.

---

## What Affects Consistency

The 2027 blueprint names three factors that can affect the comparability or consistency of financial statements: a **change in accounting principle**, the **correction of a material misstatement** in previously issued statements, and a **material change in classification**. The first two are consistency matters that the auditor's report must address. The third usually is not.

### Change in Accounting Principle

A change in accounting principle is a change from one generally accepted principle to another, including a change in the method of applying a principle. Changes come in two kinds:

- **Voluntary changes**, such as Bear Co. switching from FIFO to weighted-average cost for inventory. Management must justify that the new principle is **preferable**, and the auditor evaluates that justification, along with whether the new principle is generally accepted and whether the change is properly accounted for and disclosed.
- **Changes required by a new standard**, such as adopting a new FASB standard on leases or credit losses. Preferability is established by the standard setter.

Under ASC 250, most changes in principle are applied **retrospectively**, restating the prior periods presented, unless the new standard says otherwise.

A change in **accounting estimate**, such as revising an asset's useful life, is not a consistency matter. The one exception is a change in estimate effected by a change in principle, such as a change in depreciation method. ASC 250 accounts for it prospectively, like an estimate, but for the auditor's consistency evaluation it is treated as a **change in accounting principle**.

### Correction of a Material Misstatement

When management restates previously issued statements to correct a material misstatement, comparability is affected in the same way, and the auditor's report must address it.

### Material Change in Classification

A reclassification, such as moving a line item from operating expenses to cost of sales in both years, generally is not a consistency matter, because both periods are presented on the same basis after the change. The auditor evaluates whether the reclassification and its disclosure are appropriate. If the "reclassification" is really the correction of a misstatement or a change in principle, it is treated as such.

## Reporting a Consistency Matter

If a change in accounting principle or the correction of a material misstatement has a material effect on the financial statements, the auditor's report on a nonissuer includes an **emphasis-of-matter paragraph** (AU-C 706, applied through AU-C 708). The paragraph appears in its own section, describes the change or the correction, refers to the note that explains it, and states that the auditor's opinion is not modified. For issuers, AS 2820 requires an **explanatory paragraph** with the same purpose.

**Example language:**

> *As discussed in Note 4 to the financial statements, in 20X5 the Company changed its method of accounting for inventory from the first-in, first-out method to the weighted-average method. Our opinion is not modified with respect to this matter.*

How long the paragraph stays depends on how the change was applied. For a change in principle applied **retrospectively** to all periods presented, the paragraph is needed only in the year of the change. For a change that is not applied retrospectively (Grizzly Industries switching from double-declining-balance to straight-line depreciation, for example), the paragraph continues in later years' reports until every period presented uses the new principle.

The opinion itself is modified only if something is wrong with the change: the new principle is not generally accepted, the method of accounting for the change is inappropriate, management cannot justify a voluntary change as preferable, or the disclosure is inadequate. If the effect is material, those problems lead to a qualified or adverse opinion.

## Reporting on Prior Periods

### The Continuing Auditor Updates the Prior-Period Opinion

When the same auditor audited both years presented, the current report expresses an opinion on both. The auditor **updates** the opinion on the prior period, taking into account what was learned in the current audit.

| Situation | What the auditor does |
|---|---|
| Nothing has changed | Expresses the same opinion on the prior period in the current report |
| The prior period was restated to correct a material misstatement | Expresses an opinion on the restated prior period and includes an emphasis-of-matter paragraph about the restatement |
| The opinion on the prior period now differs from the one originally issued (for example, a qualification has been resolved because the statements were revised) | Expresses the new opinion and adds an **other-matter paragraph** giving the date and type of the earlier opinion, the substantive reasons for it, and the fact that the updated opinion differs |

**Example.** Kodiak Partners restates its 20X4 financial statements to correct a material revenue recognition error. The same firm audited both years. The 20X5 report expresses an opinion on the restated 20X4 statements and includes an emphasis-of-matter paragraph about the restatement.

If the auditor learns that previously issued statements are materially misstated and management will not correct them, the auditor takes steps to prevent future reliance on the earlier report, as described in [Subsequent Events](./subsequent-events.md).

### A Predecessor Audited the Prior Period

When the prior year was audited by a different firm, the predecessor's report may or may not be presented again.

**If the predecessor's report is reissued**, both reports appear. Management, not the successor, asks the predecessor to reissue. Before doing so, the predecessor reads the current financial statements, compares the prior-period statements it reported on with those now presented, and obtains **representation letters** from management and from the successor auditor (the successor's letter states whether its audit revealed anything that might materially affect the prior-period statements).

**If the predecessor's report is not reissued**, the successor includes an **other-matter paragraph** stating that the prior-period statements were audited by another auditor, the date of that report, the type of opinion, and, if the opinion was modified, the reasons. The successor does not name the predecessor unless the predecessor's practice was acquired by or merged with the successor's.

> *The financial statements of Sloth Security as of and for the year ended December 31, 20X4, were audited by other auditors, whose report dated March 15, 20X5, expressed an unmodified opinion on those statements.*

If the prior-period statements have since been restated, the other-matter paragraph says that the predecessor reported on the statements **before** the restatement. The successor may also be engaged to audit the restatement adjustments and report on them.

The communication between successor and predecessor **before** the engagement is accepted is a separate matter, covered in [Engagement Acceptance and Terms](../ethics-professional-responsibilities-and-general-principles/engagement-acceptance-and-terms.md).

### The Prior Period Was Not Audited

If the prior-period statements were reviewed or compiled rather than audited, the current audit report includes an **other-matter paragraph** that states the level of service performed in the prior period, the date of that report, any material modifications noted in it, and (for a review) that a review is substantially less in scope than an audit and does not result in an opinion, or (for a compilation) that the accountant did not provide any assurance. The auditor's opinion covers only the current period.

## Initial Audits and Opening Balances

In the first year a firm audits an entity, the auditor must obtain sufficient appropriate evidence that the **opening balances** do not contain misstatements that materially affect the current period, and that accounting policies have been applied consistently (or that changes were properly accounted for and disclosed). Reviewing the predecessor's working papers is often the most efficient source of that evidence, supplemented by procedures in the current year, such as testing the collection of opening receivables.

| Opening balance situation | Effect on the current report |
|---|---|
| Opening balances are supported | No effect |
| The auditor cannot obtain sufficient evidence about opening balances | Qualified opinion or disclaimer on the results of operations and cash flows; the ending balance sheet may still receive an unmodified opinion |
| Opening balances contain a misstatement that materially affects the current period and is not properly corrected | Qualified or adverse opinion, depending on pervasiveness |

---

## Comparative Statements and Comparative Information

U.S. practice usually presents **comparative financial statements**, in which the prior-period statements are complete and the auditor's opinion covers each period presented. Issuers generally present three years of income statements and cash flows and two balance sheets, although smaller reporting companies may present two years. Sometimes, however, prior-period amounts are presented only as **comparative information** within the current statements. In that case the auditor's opinion addresses only the current period, and the auditor evaluates whether the prior-period amounts are appropriately presented and agree with the statements they came from.
