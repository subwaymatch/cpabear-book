# Going Concern

Financial statements are normally prepared on the assumption that the entity will keep operating: it will sell its inventory in the ordinary course and pay its debts as they come due, rather than being forced to liquidate. When that assumption is in question, users need to know, and the auditor has to decide whether the doubt is real and whether the financial statements say enough about it. This chapter covers the look-ahead period (where the AICPA and PCAOB differ), the procedures that surface going concern problems, the conditions that raise doubt, the evaluation of management's plans, and the reporting that results. AU-C 570 governs nonissuers and PCAOB AS 2415 governs issuers.

---

## Management's Evaluation and the Auditor's

Under U.S. GAAP (ASC 205-40), **management** must evaluate whether conditions and events, considered in the aggregate, raise **substantial doubt** about the entity's ability to continue as a going concern. Substantial doubt exists when it is **probable** that the entity will be unable to meet its obligations as they become due within the look-ahead period. Management must disclose when substantial doubt exists, and also when substantial doubt was raised but its plans alleviate it.

The **auditor** evaluates whether substantial doubt exists and whether the disclosures are adequate. For nonissuers, the auditor considers going concern during risk assessment (including whether management has made a preliminary evaluation), evaluates management's evaluation, and stays alert to new conditions throughout the audit. For issuers, AS 2415 bases the evaluation on the procedures performed for other audit objectives; the auditor does not need to design procedures solely to look for going concern problems. In both cases the auditor is not predicting the future. The auditor is judging whether the evidence available up to the report date points to probable trouble within the look-ahead period.

---

## The Look-Ahead Period

The two standard setters look ahead from different starting points.

| Framework or standard | Look-ahead period |
|---|---|
| **Nonissuer under U.S. GAAP** (AU-C 570, applying ASC 205-40) | **One year after the date the financial statements are issued** (or available to be issued) |
| **Issuer** (PCAOB AS 2415) | A reasonable period, **not to exceed one year beyond the date of the financial statements** being audited (the balance sheet date) |
| **Governmental entity** (GASB) | **Twelve months beyond the financial statement date**, plus information already known about conditions shortly after that period |

AU-C 570 looks to the period the financial reporting framework requires. If the framework sets no period, AU-C 570 uses one year after the date the financial statements are issued or available to be issued.

**Example.** For a December 31, 20X4 year-end with financial statements issued on March 15, 20X5, a nonissuer's evaluation under U.S. GAAP runs through **March 15, 20X6**. An issuer's evaluation under AS 2415 runs through **December 31, 20X5**. (Management of an issuer still applies the GAAP period in its own evaluation and disclosures, and the auditor evaluates whether those disclosures are adequate.)

## Procedures That Surface Going Concern Problems

Going concern problems usually show up through work the auditor is already doing:

- **Analytical procedures** that reveal recurring losses, falling margins, or deteriorating liquidity ratios
- **Review of subsequent events**, such as a lost customer or a missed payment in January
- **Review of debt agreements** for defaults, covenant violations, and maturities within the coming year
- **Reading minutes** of the board and its committees
- **Inquiry of the entity's lawyers** about litigation that could produce a judgment the entity cannot pay
- **Confirmation** of arrangements with related or third parties to provide or maintain financial support

**Example.** While auditing Polar Inc., the engagement team notes three consecutive years of net losses, negative working capital, and a technical default on a bank loan. Together these conditions trigger a full going concern evaluation.

---

## Conditions and Events That May Raise Substantial Doubt

The blueprint asks candidates to identify the factors that may indicate substantial doubt. They fall into a few groups:

- **Negative trends**: recurring operating losses, working capital deficiencies, negative cash flows from operations, adverse key financial ratios
- **Other signs of financial difficulty**: default on loans or similar agreements, dividends in arrears, denial of usual trade credit by suppliers, the need to restructure debt or sell substantial assets to raise cash
- **Internal matters**: work stoppages or other labor difficulties, dependence on the success of one project, uneconomic long-term commitments, the need to significantly revise operations
- **External matters**: legal proceedings or new legislation that could threaten operations, loss of a key franchise, license, or patent, loss of a principal customer or supplier, an uninsured or underinsured catastrophe

:::tip[Exam Tip]

No single condition automatically means substantial doubt exists. The auditor considers conditions and events **in the aggregate**, and then considers management's plans to deal with them.

:::

## Evaluating Management's Plans

When conditions raise substantial doubt, the auditor evaluates management's plans to mitigate them. Under ASC 205-40 and AU-C 570, the plans **alleviate** substantial doubt only if it is probable that they will be effectively implemented **and** probable that, once implemented, they will mitigate the conditions within the look-ahead period.

| Management's plan | What the auditor examines |
|---|---|
| **Borrow or refinance** | Is there a commitment from a lender, and is the borrower likely to meet its conditions? |
| **Restructure debt** | Has the creditor agreed? Are the revised terms ones the entity can meet? |
| **Sell assets** | Are the assets marketable, are there restrictions on selling them, and will the proceeds be enough? |
| **Issue equity** | Are investors identified and committed, or is the offering still hypothetical? |
| **Cut or delay spending** | Is the plan realistic, and can the entity still operate afterward? |

When the plans depend on forecasts, the auditor evaluates the significant assumptions behind them. The auditor also obtains **written representations** from management about its plans and their feasibility, and communicates the going concern findings to those charged with governance.

**Example.** Grizzly Industries faces negative cash flows and a line of credit that matures in four months. Management's plan is to "explore strategic alternatives." No buyer, lender, or investor has been identified and no agreements exist, so it is not probable that the plan will be effectively implemented. The plan does not alleviate substantial doubt.

## Reporting

### When Substantial Doubt Remains and Disclosure Is Adequate

The opinion is **not modified**. The auditor adds language that draws attention to the uncertainty and must use the terms **"substantial doubt"** and **"going concern."** The auditor should not use conditional language, such as saying the entity "may" be unable to continue if financing is not obtained.

- **Nonissuers (AU-C 570):** the report includes a **separate section** with the heading "Substantial Doubt About the Entity's Ability to Continue as a Going Concern." It refers to the note that describes the conditions and management's plans and states that the opinion is not modified.
- **Issuers (AS 2415):** the report includes an **explanatory paragraph** describing the substantial doubt.

**Example language** (paraphrased from the AICPA illustration):

> *Substantial Doubt About the Company's Ability to Continue as a Going Concern*
>
> *The accompanying financial statements have been prepared assuming that Sloth Security will continue as a going concern. As discussed in Note 12 to the financial statements, the Company has suffered recurring losses from operations and has a net capital deficiency, and management has stated that these conditions raise substantial doubt about the Company's ability to continue as a going concern. Management's evaluation of the events and conditions and its plans regarding these matters are also described in Note 12. The financial statements do not include any adjustments that might result from the outcome of this uncertainty. Our opinion is not modified with respect to this matter.*

### Other Outcomes

| Situation | Effect on the report |
|---|---|
| Substantial doubt remains; disclosure adequate | Unmodified opinion with a going concern section (nonissuer) or explanatory paragraph (issuer) |
| Substantial doubt remains; disclosure **inadequate** | Qualified or adverse opinion, because the inadequate disclosure is a departure from GAAP |
| Substantial doubt **alleviated** by management's plans | No going concern section or paragraph, but the auditor still evaluates the disclosure GAAP requires about the conditions and the plans |
| Auditor cannot obtain enough evidence to evaluate going concern | Scope limitation: qualified opinion or disclaimer |
| Several significant uncertainties together (rare) | The auditor may disclaim an opinion instead of adding a going concern section |
| Liquidation is imminent and the statements use the **liquidation basis** | Going concern language does not apply; the auditor reports on the liquidation basis statements, typically with an emphasis-of-matter paragraph about the basis |

One more rule appears on the exam. If substantial doubt existed last year and has been removed this year, the going concern section in last year's report is **not repeated** when the prior-year statements are presented with the current year's.

:::warning[Common Trap]

A going concern section or paragraph does **not** turn an unmodified opinion into a qualified one. The opinion is modified only if the disclosure is inadequate (qualified or adverse) or the auditor cannot obtain enough evidence (qualified or disclaimer).

:::
