# Audit Reports: Unmodified and Modified Opinions

The auditor's report is the one part of the audit most users ever see, so its wording is tightly prescribed. A reader who knows the standard report can spot in seconds when something is different and what kind of difference it is. This chapter first walks through the standard unmodified report for nonissuers (AICPA standards) and issuers (PCAOB standards), including key audit matters and critical audit matters. It then covers the three modified opinions (qualified, adverse, and disclaimer) and how each changes the report.

**Issuers** are entities whose securities are registered with the SEC, and their audits follow PCAOB standards. **Nonissuers** are all other entities, and their audits follow the AICPA's Statements on Auditing Standards (GAAS).

---

## The Unmodified Opinion

An **unmodified opinion** says that the financial statements **present fairly, in all material respects**, the entity's financial position, results of operations, and cash flows in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework. The AICPA calls it "unmodified"; the PCAOB calls it "unqualified." Both mean a clean opinion.

Notice what the phrase does not say. It does not say the statements are correct, accurate, or free of error. It says they are fairly presented in all **material** respects, based on **reasonable** assurance, which is a high level of assurance but not a guarantee.

---

## The Nonissuer Report

Under AU-C 700, the auditor's report for a nonissuer is titled **"Independent Auditor's Report"** and is addressed as the circumstances require, usually to the board of directors, the shareholders, or those charged with governance. Its sections appear in this order:

1. **Opinion.** Identifies the entity and each financial statement audited, the dates or periods covered, and states the opinion, naming the framework (for example, "accounting principles generally accepted in the United States of America").
2. **Basis for Opinion.** States that the audit was conducted in accordance with GAAS, refers to the auditor's responsibilities section, states that the auditor is required to be independent and to meet other ethical responsibilities, and states that the auditor believes the evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate to provide a basis for the opinion.
3. **Sections that appear only when they apply**: a going concern section, emphasis-of-matter and other-matter paragraphs, and key audit matters (if the auditor is engaged to report them).
4. **Responsibilities of Management for the Financial Statements.** Management is responsible for preparing and fairly presenting the statements, for designing, implementing, and maintaining internal control relevant to that preparation, and (when the framework requires it) for evaluating whether conditions raise substantial doubt about going concern.
5. **Auditor's Responsibilities for the Audit of the Financial Statements.** The auditor's objectives are to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the statements are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, and to issue a report that includes an opinion. The section explains that reasonable assurance is not absolute, that the risk of not detecting fraud is higher than for error, and that the auditor exercises professional judgment and skepticism, assesses risks, considers internal control without expressing an opinion on it, evaluates accounting policies, estimates, and overall presentation, concludes on going concern, and communicates with those charged with governance.
6. **Other Information**, when the entity issues an annual report containing the financial statements (see [Other Information in Audited Documents](./other-information-in-audited-documents.md)), and any **other reporting responsibilities** required by law or regulation.
7. **The firm's signature, the city and state where the auditor practices, and the date.**

## The Issuer Report

Under PCAOB AS 3101, the report is titled **"Report of Independent Registered Public Accounting Firm"** and is addressed to the shareholders and the board of directors. Its structure is similar but more compact:

1. **Opinion on the Financial Statements**, which comes first, as in the nonissuer report.
2. **Basis for Opinion**, which states that the financial statements are the responsibility of management and that the auditor's responsibility is to express an opinion; that the firm is a public accounting firm **registered with the PCAOB** and required to be independent under the federal securities laws and SEC and PCAOB rules; that the audit was conducted under PCAOB standards to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the statements are free of material misstatement, whether due to error or fraud; and a brief description of the audit procedures.
3. **Critical Audit Matters**, discussed below.
4. **The firm's signature**, a statement of **auditor tenure** (the year the firm began serving consecutively as the company's auditor), the city and state of the office issuing the report, and the date.

| Element | Nonissuer (AICPA) | Issuer (PCAOB) |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Independent Auditor's Report | Report of Independent Registered Public Accounting Firm |
| Standards cited | Auditing standards generally accepted in the United States (GAAS) | Standards of the PCAOB |
| Management's responsibilities | Separate section | Stated within Basis for Opinion |
| Registration with PCAOB | Not applicable | Stated in Basis for Opinion |
| Reporting on significant matters | Key audit matters, only if the auditor is engaged to report them | Critical audit matters, required for most audits |
| Auditor tenure | Not required | Required |

## Dating the Report

The report is dated **no earlier than the date on which the auditor has obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to support the opinion. Under AICPA standards, that evidence includes evidence that the audit documentation has been reviewed, that all the statements (including disclosures) have been prepared, and that management has asserted that it takes responsibility for them. The report date is not the date the report is delivered.

**Example.** Bear Co.'s auditor obtains sufficient appropriate evidence on March 5, 20X5, for the December 31, 20X4 financial statements. The report is dated March 5, 20X5, even though it is delivered to the board on March 15.

The date matters because it marks the end of the auditor's responsibility for searching out subsequent events. If a subsequent event that requires disclosure comes to light after the report date but before the report is released, the auditor may **dual date** the report, keeping the original date for everything except the note describing the new event (see [Subsequent Events](./subsequent-events.md)).

## Critical Audit Matters (Issuers)

A **critical audit matter (CAM)** is any matter arising from the audit that was communicated or required to be communicated to the audit committee, and that:

1. Relates to accounts or disclosures that are **material** to the financial statements, and
2. Involved **especially challenging, subjective, or complex** auditor judgment.

CAMs are required in issuer audit reports except for audits of emerging growth companies, brokers and dealers, investment companies other than business development companies, and certain employee benefit plans. In deciding whether a matter is a CAM, the auditor considers factors such as the assessed risk of material misstatement (including significant risks), the degree of auditor judgment and subjectivity involved, the nature and extent of audit effort (including the need for specialized skill), the nature of the audit evidence obtained, and whether the matter involved significant unusual transactions.

For each CAM, the report must:

- **Identify** the CAM
- **Describe the principal considerations** that led the auditor to decide it was a CAM
- **Describe how** the CAM was addressed in the audit
- **Refer to** the relevant accounts or disclosures

If the auditor determines there are no CAMs, the report says so. Communicating a CAM is not a substitute for a modified opinion: a matter that requires a qualified or adverse opinion is explained in the basis for the modified opinion.

**Example.** Polar Inc. has long-term contracts with several performance obligations, and allocating the transaction price required especially subjective auditor judgment and significant effort. The CAM section identifies revenue recognition on these contracts, explains why it was challenging, describes the procedures (such as testing management's estimates of standalone selling prices), and refers to the revenue note.

## Key Audit Matters (Nonissuers)

**Key audit matters (KAMs)**, governed by AU-C 701, are the nonissuer counterpart of CAMs. They are the matters that, in the auditor's professional judgment, were of **most significance** in the audit of the current period, selected from the matters communicated with those charged with governance. Unlike CAMs, KAMs are **optional**: the auditor reports them only when engaged to do so, or when law or regulation requires it.

For each KAM, the auditor describes why it was considered one of the most significant matters, how it was addressed in the audit, and where it is disclosed in the financial statements, if applicable. If the auditor is engaged to report KAMs and concludes there are none, the report says so. The auditor does **not** report KAMs when disclaiming an opinion, because describing individual matters could suggest the auditor obtained more evidence than it did.

## Modified Opinions

When the auditor cannot issue a clean opinion, the type of modification depends on two questions:

1. **What is the problem?** Either the financial statements are **materially misstated** (a departure from the framework), or the auditor was **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence** (a scope limitation).
2. **How big is it?** The effect is either **material but not pervasive**, or **material and pervasive**.

| | **Material, not pervasive** | **Material and pervasive** |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial statements are materially misstated** | Qualified opinion ("except for") | Adverse opinion |
| **Auditor could not obtain sufficient appropriate evidence** | Qualified opinion ("except for") | Disclaimer of opinion |

An effect is **pervasive** if it is not confined to specific elements, accounts, or items; if it is confined but represents a substantial portion of the financial statements; or, for a disclosure, if it is fundamental to users' understanding of the statements.

### When the Statements Are Misstated

A **qualified opinion** says the statements are fairly presented **except for** the effects of the matter described. Kodiak Partners, for example, charges all lease payments to expense instead of recognizing right-of-use assets and lease liabilities under ASC 842. The effect is material, but it is confined to identifiable accounts, so the auditor issues a qualified opinion.

An **adverse opinion** says the statements are **not** fairly presented. It is reserved for misstatements so material and widespread that the statements as a whole are misleading. If Cub Entertainment fails to consolidate a material subsidiary, omits significant liabilities, and overstates revenue across its segments, an adverse opinion is appropriate.

### When the Auditor Cannot Obtain Enough Evidence

Scope limitations come from two sources. Some arise from **circumstances**: records destroyed in a fire, or an auditor engaged after year-end who could not observe the inventory count. Others are **imposed by management**, such as refusing to let the auditor confirm receivables. When management imposes a limitation, the auditor asks management to remove it; if management refuses, the auditor tells those charged with governance and tries alternative procedures. If the possible effects are material and pervasive, the auditor disclaims an opinion or, where practicable, withdraws from the engagement.

A **qualified opinion** fits a limitation confined to specific areas. Grizzly Industries' auditor, engaged after the year-end count, could not observe inventory or verify it by other means, but completed all other procedures. Inventory is material but the limitation does not pervade the statements, so the opinion is qualified.

A **disclaimer of opinion** states that the auditor **does not express an opinion**. When Panda Industries' management will not allow the auditor to confirm receivables, observe inventory, or access a key subsidiary's records, too much of the financial statements is unverifiable, and the auditor disclaims. A disclaimer does not say the statements are wrong; it says the auditor could not find out.

### How the Report Changes

| Modification | Opinion section heading | Key opinion language | Section explaining the reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qualified | Qualified Opinion | "...except for the effects of the matter described..., the financial statements present fairly..." | Basis for Qualified Opinion |
| Adverse | Adverse Opinion | "...the financial statements do not present fairly..." | Basis for Adverse Opinion |
| Disclaimer | Disclaimer of Opinion | "We were engaged to audit... we do not express an opinion..." | Basis for Disclaimer of Opinion |

A disclaimer changes more than the opinion. The report states that the auditor was **engaged to audit** the statements rather than that the auditor audited them, omits the statement that the evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate, and revises the auditor's responsibilities section to explain that the auditor could not obtain sufficient appropriate evidence. Key audit matters are not reported. PCAOB reports follow the same logic under AS 3105, with the reasons for a modification set out in the report.

## Uncertainties

An uncertainty, such as pending litigation, does not by itself lead to a modified opinion. If management has accounted for and disclosed the matter in accordance with the framework, the opinion is unmodified, and the auditor may add an **emphasis-of-matter paragraph** (an emphasis paragraph for issuers) to direct readers to the disclosure. If the accounting or disclosure departs from the framework and the effect is material, the opinion is qualified or adverse.

**Example.** Sloth Security is the defendant in a major lawsuit. Management has accrued its best estimate of the probable loss and disclosed the case fully. The auditor issues an unmodified opinion and decides to add an emphasis-of-matter paragraph pointing to the litigation note.

:::tip[Exam Strategy]

When a question asks which opinion to issue, ask two questions in order. First, is this a **misstatement** problem or an **evidence** problem? Second, is the effect **material** or **material and pervasive**? The answers place you in one cell of the table above.

:::
