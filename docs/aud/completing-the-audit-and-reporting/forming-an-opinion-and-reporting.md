# Forming an Opinion and Reporting

Everything in the audit, from planning to the last confirmation reply, leads to one decision: do the financial statements present fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework? This chapter explains how the auditor makes that decision. It covers the two sets of standards, what an audit can and cannot promise, the general requirements that apply to every audit, the factors the auditor weighs in forming the opinion, and two reporting situations that call for special care: different opinions on different statements, and group audits that involve other auditors. The wording of the report itself is covered in [Audit Reports: Unmodified and Modified Opinions](./audit-reports-unmodified-and-modified-opinions.md).

---

## Two Sets of Standards

| Client | Standard setter | Standards |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonissuers** (private companies, not-for-profits, governments) | AICPA Auditing Standards Board | Statements on Auditing Standards (SASs), codified as AU-C sections |
| **Issuers** (companies with securities registered with the SEC) | Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) | PCAOB auditing standards (AS sections), approved by the SEC |

The SASs are largely converged with the International Standards on Auditing. The PCAOB also registers and inspects the firms that audit issuers. Most concepts are shared, but report format, critical audit matters, and several independence and documentation rules differ, so exam questions state whether the entity is an issuer or a nonissuer.

**Example.** An audit of Bear Co., a private company, follows AICPA standards. An audit of Grizzly Industries, whose shares trade on a national exchange, follows PCAOB standards.

---

## Purpose and Limits of an Audit

The purpose of an audit is to **increase the confidence** that intended users can place in the financial statements, by expressing an opinion on whether they are fairly presented. The opinion addresses fair presentation, not perfect accuracy. It does not promise that the entity will survive or that management is running the business well.

The auditor provides **reasonable assurance**, a high but not absolute level of assurance, because every audit has inherent limitations:

- **Financial reporting involves judgment.** Estimates such as useful lives, credit losses, and fair values cannot be measured precisely, so even properly prepared statements carry some imprecision.
- **Audit procedures have limits.** The auditor tests on a sample basis, evidence is persuasive rather than conclusive, and fraud may be concealed through collusion, forgery, or management override.
- **An audit must be timely and cost-effective.** The auditor focuses effort on the areas of highest risk rather than examining everything.

Because of these limitations, an audit performed properly can still miss a material misstatement. What the standards demand is that the auditor plan and perform the audit to obtain reasonable assurance that the statements as a whole are free of material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error.

## General Requirements of Every Audit

AU-C 200 sets out requirements that apply throughout every GAAS audit. The PCAOB's general standard, AS 1000, sets similar requirements for issuer audits. A convenient memory aid is **SEJEC**:

| | Requirement | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **S** | Professional **skepticism** | A questioning mind and a critical assessment of evidence, including evidence that contradicts management |
| **E** | **Ethics** | Compliance with relevant ethical requirements, including independence |
| **J** | Professional **judgment** | Applying training, knowledge, and experience to decisions about materiality, risk, procedures, and conclusions |
| **E** | Sufficient appropriate **evidence** | Enough evidence (sufficiency) of good enough quality (relevance and reliability) to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level |
| **C** | **Compliance** with the standards | Following every AU-C section (or PCAOB standard) relevant to the engagement |

The [professional skepticism and judgment](../ethics-professional-responsibilities-and-general-principles/professional-skepticism-and-professional-judgment.md) chapter covers the first and third items in depth.

## Factors the Auditor Considers in Forming the Opinion

Near the end of the audit, the auditor steps back and asks whether the work done supports a conclusion. The main factors are:

- **Sufficiency and appropriateness of the evidence.** Did the procedures performed respond to the assessed risks, and does the evidence, taken together, support the conclusion? Evidence that contradicts management's assertions counts as much as evidence that supports them.
- **Uncorrected misstatements.** Are the misstatements management declined to correct material, individually or in the aggregate? (See [Misstatements and Internal Control Deficiencies](./misstatements-and-internal-control-deficiencies.md).)
- **Qualitative aspects of the entity's accounting practices**, including indicators of possible **bias** in management's judgments. A series of estimates that all lean in management's favor can make the statements misleading even if each estimate is individually reasonable.
- **Compliance with the framework.** Are the significant accounting policies appropriate and adequately disclosed? Are the estimates reasonable? Is the information presented relevant, reliable, comparable, and understandable? Do the disclosures let users understand the effect of material transactions and events?
- **Fair presentation as a whole.** Do the overall presentation, structure, and content of the statements, including the notes, represent the underlying transactions and events in a manner that achieves fair presentation?
- **Overall analytical review.** The auditor performs analytical procedures near the end of the audit to confirm that the statements are consistent with the auditor's understanding of the entity. A fluctuation no one can explain at this stage sends the auditor back to work.
- **Completion matters**, such as the review of subsequent events, the evaluation of going concern, and the receipt of management's written representations.

If the auditor concludes that the statements are fairly presented and the evidence is sufficient, the opinion is unmodified. If not, the auditor decides between a qualified opinion, an adverse opinion, and a disclaimer, as described in the next chapter. For an integrated audit of an issuer, the auditor also forms a separate opinion on internal control over financial reporting (see [Reporting on Internal Control](./reporting-on-internal-control.md)).

## Different Opinions on Different Statements

The auditor's report covers each statement presented, and the opinion need not be the same for all of them. The most common case arises in a first-year audit. If the auditor cannot obtain sufficient evidence about **opening inventory**, the ending balance sheet may still be fully supported, but cost of sales, net income, and cash flows from operations depend on the unverified opening amount. The auditor may then issue an unmodified opinion on the balance sheet and a qualified opinion or disclaimer on the statements of income, retained earnings, and cash flows.

The reverse is not allowed. When the auditor expresses an adverse opinion or disclaims an opinion on the financial statements as a whole, the same report may not include an unmodified opinion on a single statement or on specific elements of the statements. Such a **piecemeal opinion** would contradict, and appear to outweigh, the overall conclusion.

## Group Audits and Other Auditors

Many entities prepare **group financial statements** that combine a parent with subsidiaries, divisions, or other **components**, and some components are audited by other firms. The standards for nonissuers changed with SAS 149, which revised AU-C 600 for audits of group financial statements for periods ending on or after December 15, 2026.

:::note[2026 versus 2027]

SAS 149 is eligible for testing starting January 1, 2027. Candidates testing in 2026 are tested on the previous AU-C 600, which identified "significant components," used "component auditor" for any other auditor whether or not on the engagement team, and called the no-reference approach "assuming responsibility."

:::

### Nonissuers Under SAS 149

The **group engagement partner** is responsible for the group audit and for the auditor's report on the group financial statements. SAS 149 takes a **risk-based approach**: instead of classifying components as "significant," the group auditor identifies and assesses the risks of material misstatement of the group financial statements and decides where audit work is needed to respond to them. To address **aggregation risk** (small misstatements at several components adding up to a material amount for the group), the group auditor sets **component performance materiality** lower than group performance materiality.

The group auditor then decides how to treat the work of another firm's auditors.

**Being involved in the work of component auditors.** A **component auditor** performs audit work on a component for the group audit and, under SAS 149, is a **member of the engagement team**. The group engagement partner directs, supervises, and reviews the component auditor's work, just as for the firm's own staff, under the quality management standard for engagements (AU-C 220). The group auditor evaluates the component auditor's competence and independence, communicates the work to be done and the materiality to use, and receives two-way communications about significant matters, including fraud, related parties, and going concern. The group auditor's report does not mention the component auditor, because the group engagement partner takes responsibility for all of the work.

**Making reference to a referred-to auditor.** Alternatively, the group auditor may rely on another firm's own audit report on a component and **make reference** to it in the group report. That firm is a **referred-to auditor** and is **not** part of the engagement team. The group report states that the component's financial statements were audited by other auditors and indicates the portion of the group they represent (for example, a percentage of total assets and revenues). The referred-to auditor is not named unless that firm agrees and its report is presented with the group auditor's. Making reference divides responsibility; it is **not** a modification of the opinion. Before making reference, the group auditor confirms that the referred-to auditor is independent and competent, that its report is not restricted as to use, and that its audit was performed under standards that permit reference, such as GAAS or PCAOB standards.

**Example.** Grizzly Industries owns Sloth Security, which another firm audits. If the group auditor involves Sloth's auditors in the group audit, directing their work, setting component performance materiality, and reviewing their documentation, the group report makes no mention of them. If the group auditor instead relies on the other firm's separate audit report on Sloth, the group report refers to that audit and states the share of consolidated total assets and revenues that Sloth represents.

### Issuers

For issuer audits, PCAOB AS 1201 governs how the **lead auditor** plans and supervises the work of other auditors whose work it uses without making reference. AS 1206, effective for fiscal years ending on or after December 15, 2024, governs **dividing responsibility** with a referred-to auditor: the lead auditor's report states the division of responsibility and the magnitude of the portion audited by the referred-to auditor.
