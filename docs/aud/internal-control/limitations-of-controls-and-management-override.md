# Limitations of Controls and Management Override

Even an excellent system of internal control can fail. People make mistakes, employees can work together to get around a control, and the managers who designed the controls can simply decide not to follow them. That last limitation, **management override**, is serious enough that auditing standards treat it as a risk of material misstatement due to fraud on every audit, with specific procedures the auditor must always perform. This chapter explains the inherent limitations of internal control and how they affect the risk of material misstatement, then turns to management override: why it is always presumed, how it typically happens, the required audit procedures, and how it shapes the audit strategy and documentation.

The governing standards are **AU-C 240** for nonissuers and **AS 2401** for issuers. The Auditing Standards Board has approved **SAS 151**, which will replace AU-C 240 for audits of periods ending on or after December 15, 2028. It is not eligible for testing before January 1, 2029, so study the current AU-C 240.

---

## Inherent Limitations of Internal Control

Internal control can provide only **reasonable assurance**, because every system has limitations that no amount of design can remove:

- **Faulty judgment.** People decide how controls are designed and how to respond to what they find, and their decisions can be wrong, especially under time pressure or with incomplete information.
- **Human error.** A reviewer misreads a report, an employee misunderstands an instruction, or someone is simply tired.
- **Collusion.** Two or more people acting together can defeat controls that depend on separating their duties.
- **Management override.** Managers can direct others to bypass controls that apply to everyone else.
- **Cost versus benefit.** An entity will not spend more on a control than the risk is worth, so some risks are accepted.

Two practical constraints make these limitations worse. **Small entities** often cannot separate duties fully because they have too few people. And controls designed for yesterday's business can become ineffective as **conditions change**, which is why monitoring matters.

**Example:** Panda Records, a small owner-managed company, has three people in its accounting department, too few to separate authorization, recordkeeping, and custody completely. To compensate, the owner reviews every bank reconciliation and approves all disbursements over \$500. That review reduces the risk of employee error and theft, but it cannot catch everything, and it introduces a different risk: because the owner is involved in so much, the owner is also in a position to override the controls.

## Effect on the Risk of Material Misstatement

Limitations in internal control raise the **risk of material misstatement**, and the auditor considers that effect at two levels.

| Level | How limitations affect risk |
|---|---|
| **Financial statement level** | Pervasive weaknesses, such as management override or a weak tone at the top, can affect many accounts and assertions at once |
| **Assertion level** | Specific weaknesses, such as inadequate segregation of duties in the revenue cycle, raise the risk for particular assertions, such as occurrence of revenue |

In assessing the effect, the auditor considers whether compensating controls exist, how significant the weakness is to the financial statements, how likely it is to lead to a misstatement, and whether it creates an **opportunity for fraud**. When the control environment itself is weak (for example, when management shows disregard for controls or for ethics), the auditor treats it as a pervasive risk, because a poor tone at the top undermines every other component.

**Example:** Bear Co.'s CFO alone selects the method, chooses the assumptions, and approves the final amount of the warranty liability, with no review by anyone else. The absence of oversight creates an opportunity for management to bias the estimate, so the auditor assesses a higher risk of material misstatement for the **valuation** of the warranty liability.

## Management Override: A Risk in Every Audit

**Management override** is management's ability to manipulate accounting records and prepare fraudulent financial statements by overriding controls that otherwise appear to be operating effectively. It cannot be prevented by the control system, because management sits above the system.

AU-C 240 and AS 2401 recognize that the risk of management override is present in **every entity**. Although its level varies from entity to entity, the risk is a risk of material misstatement due to fraud and therefore a **significant risk**. Unlike the presumed fraud risk in revenue recognition, which the auditor may conclude does not apply in particular circumstances, the risk of management override cannot be set aside. Several reasons explain why the standards take this position: management controls the system and knows its weaknesses, override can happen at any time and in unpredictable ways, and the major frauds of recent decades have almost always involved it.

Management override is different from a control deficiency. A deficiency means a control is poorly designed or not operating. Override means the control works for everyone else, and management deliberately goes around it.

**Example:** Honey Entertainment's CEO tells the controller to record a fictitious sale at year-end so the company meets analysts' expectations. The controller records it even though there is no contract, invoice, or shipment. Revenue controls work properly for every other transaction; management has simply bypassed them for this one.

## How Management Override Happens

The standards identify three common methods, and each corresponds to one of the required procedures described in the next section.

### Inappropriate Journal Entries

Management may record fictitious or unsupported journal entries, often near the end of a reporting period, to inflate revenue, capitalize costs that should be expenses, or understate liabilities. Such entries are often made outside the normal process, by people who do not usually make entries, or with little explanation.

**Example:** At the CFO's direction, a Grizzly Industries accountant records a \$2.4 million entry on December 31, debiting accounts receivable and crediting revenue. The entry has no invoice, customer order, or shipping record behind it. Its only purpose is to reach the annual revenue target.

### Biased Accounting Estimates

Estimates require judgment, and judgment can be bent toward a desired result.

| Estimate | How bias may appear |
|---|---|
| **Allowance for credit losses** | Understating the allowance to overstate receivables and income |
| **Warranty liability** | Understating expected claims |
| **Useful lives of assets** | Choosing long lives to reduce depreciation |
| **Fair value measurements** | Selecting favorable assumptions or models |
| **Inventory obsolescence** | Delaying write-downs of slow-moving goods |

Bias often shows up as a pattern rather than in a single estimate. Each estimate may fall within a reasonable range, yet together they all lean the same way.

**Example:** For three years, Polar Inc.'s controller has chosen useful lives at the top of the acceptable range for every new asset class. Each choice is defensible on its own, but the consistent direction, always toward lower expense, suggests possible management bias.

### Significant Unusual Transactions

Management may enter into transactions outside the normal course of business, or otherwise unusual, that achieve a reporting result or hide the substance of what happened. Warning signs include an overly complex structure, little discussion with the board, more attention to the accounting result than to the economics, and counterparties that are related parties or have little substance.

**Example:** In December, Ursa Security sells its headquarters building to an entity controlled by the CEO's brother-in-law, at a price well above market value. The sale produces a large gain that offsets the year's operating losses, and there is no apparent business reason for it.

## Required Audit Procedures

Because the risk of management override exists on every audit, AU-C 240 and AS 2401 require the auditor to perform three procedures on every engagement, whatever the auditor's assessment of other fraud risks.

### 1. Test the Appropriateness of Journal Entries and Other Adjustments

The auditor:

- obtains an understanding of the financial reporting process and the controls over journal entries and other adjustments;
- asks people involved in the financial reporting process about inappropriate or unusual activity in processing entries;
- selects journal entries and other adjustments made **at the end of the reporting period** for testing; and
- considers the need to test entries and adjustments made **throughout the period**.

The auditor looks for characteristics associated with fraudulent entries: entries to unusual or seldom-used accounts, entries made by people who do not normally make them, entries recorded at the end of the period or after closing with little or no explanation, entries with round numbers or consistent ending digits, and entries with no account number.

**Example:** Bear Co.'s auditor obtains the complete population of the year's journal entries and uses data analytics to flag entries posted on weekends, entries posted directly by the CFO, entries to revenue in the last week of December, and round-dollar entries over \$100,000. The team traces each flagged entry to supporting documentation and asks the people who made them to explain their purpose.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

Journal entry testing works best when management cannot predict it. Varying the selection criteria from year to year, or adding tests management would not expect, is part of incorporating **unpredictability** into the audit.

:::

### 2. Review Accounting Estimates for Bias

The auditor evaluates whether the judgments and decisions management made in developing significant estimates indicate possible bias, even if each estimate is individually reasonable. If they do, the auditor reevaluates the estimates taken as a whole. The auditor also performs a **retrospective review**, comparing the judgments and assumptions behind significant estimates in the prior year's financial statements with how things actually turned out.

**Example:** Grizzly Industries' auditor reviews the warranty liability for the past four years. Actual warranty costs exceeded management's estimate every year, by an average of 18%. The consistent shortfall suggests a bias toward understating the liability and overstating income, so the auditor raises the assessed risk for the valuation of the warranty liability and expands substantive testing.

### 3. Evaluate the Business Rationale for Significant Unusual Transactions

For significant transactions outside the normal course of business or that otherwise seem unusual, the auditor evaluates whether the business rationale (or its absence) suggests that the transactions were entered into to engage in fraudulent financial reporting or to conceal misappropriation of assets. The auditor considers whether the terms are consistent with arm's-length dealing and whether related parties or entities with little substance are involved.

**Example:** In the last month of the year, Honey Entertainment records \$5 million of revenue from a consulting contract with a newly formed customer. The customer has no employees and shares an office address with Honey Entertainment's chief operating officer. The auditor concludes that the transaction lacks economic substance and appears designed to inflate revenue, evaluates the effect on the financial statements, and communicates the matter to those charged with governance.

:::warning

These three procedures are **required on every audit**. The auditor cannot skip them because management has always seemed trustworthy or because other fraud risks were assessed as low.

:::

## Effect on the Overall Audit Strategy

The three required procedures are a floor, not a ceiling. When the risk of override is higher than usual, for example because of new management, intense pressure to meet targets, or weak oversight by the board, the auditor adjusts the **nature, timing, and extent** of procedures.

The auditor changes the **nature** of procedures by relying more on evidence from independent outside sources, such as confirmations, and by building in unpredictability, for example by testing locations or accounts management would not expect. The auditor changes the **timing** by performing more work at or near year-end, when override is most likely, and by performing some procedures without advance notice. And the auditor changes the **extent** by testing larger samples and widening journal entry testing to more accounts, locations, or periods. If controls are vulnerable to override, the auditor may also rely less on them and take a more substantive approach.

**Example:** Bear Co. hired a new CEO and CFO this year, and the board has set aggressive growth targets. The auditor judges the risk of override to be elevated. The team increases journal entry testing from 25 entries to 60, moves substantive testing of receivables from interim to year-end, sends confirmations to more customers, and develops independent estimates for every significant estimate instead of only the riskiest ones.

## Documenting the Response

The auditor documents the fraud risk assessment and the response to it, including:

- the significant decisions reached in the engagement team's discussion of fraud risks;
- the identified and assessed risks of material misstatement due to fraud, including management override, at the financial statement and assertion levels;
- the overall responses and the specific procedures performed, linked to the assessed risks;
- the results of those procedures, including the journal entry selection criteria, the estimates reviewed for bias, and the unusual transactions evaluated; and
- communications about fraud with management, those charged with governance, and others.

**Example:** Bear Co.'s audit file includes a memo describing the risk of management override and the team's response, the data analytics report showing the journal entry selection criteria and results, a schedule comparing last year's five largest estimates with actual outcomes, and an evaluation of the three significant unusual transactions identified during the year.

## Summary

| Topic | Key takeaway |
|---|---|
| **Inherent limitations** | Faulty judgment, human error, collusion, management override, and cost versus benefit |
| **Effect on risk** | Limitations raise the risk of material misstatement at the financial statement level (pervasive weaknesses) and the assertion level (specific weaknesses) |
| **Management override** | Present in every entity; a fraud risk and a significant risk on every audit; cannot be set aside |
| **Methods of override** | Inappropriate journal entries, biased estimates, and significant unusual transactions |
| **Required procedures** | Test journal entries and adjustments, review estimates for bias (including a retrospective review), and evaluate the business rationale for significant unusual transactions |
| **Strategy** | Heightened override risk changes the nature, timing, and extent of procedures |
| **Coming change** | SAS 151 replaces AU-C 240 for periods ending on or after December 15, 2028; not tested before 2029 |
