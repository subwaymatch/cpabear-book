# Planning the Audit

A well-planned audit finds the material misstatements, puts experienced people on the hard areas, and finishes on time. Planning is not a phase that ends when fieldwork begins. It is **iterative**: the plan is revised whenever new information changes the auditor's view of the risks. This chapter follows the planning process in order: the preliminary engagement activities, the overall audit strategy and the detailed audit plan, the working materials that support the plan, the engagement partner's responsibilities, the factors that shape planning, the basics of audit documentation, and the financial statement assertions that link every procedure to the statements. The AICPA standard is AU-C 300; the PCAOB standard is AS 2101.

---

## Preliminary Engagement Activities

Before planning in earnest, the auditor performs three preliminary activities at the start of each engagement:

1. Performs procedures on the **acceptance or continuance** of the client relationship and the engagement
2. Evaluates compliance with **ethical requirements**, including independence
3. Establishes an **understanding of the terms of the engagement**, documented in an engagement letter

These steps make sure there are no issues, such as a threat to independence or doubts about management's integrity, that would make the auditor unwilling to proceed. The [Engagement Acceptance and Terms](../ethics-professional-responsibilities-and-general-principles/engagement-acceptance-and-terms.md) chapter covers them in detail.

## Overall Audit Strategy and Audit Plan

The two terms are sometimes used loosely, but they describe different things. The **overall audit strategy** sets the scope, timing, and direction of the audit. The **audit plan** turns the strategy into specific procedures.

### Overall Audit Strategy

In establishing the strategy, the auditor considers:

- **Scope**: the financial reporting framework, industry-specific reporting requirements, and the locations or components to be covered
- **Reporting objectives**: the reporting deadlines and the key dates for communications with management and those charged with governance
- **Significant factors**: materiality, areas of higher risk, and significant changes in the entity, its industry, or its reporting
- **Preliminary activities and prior knowledge**: the results of the preliminary engagement activities and knowledge from other work for the entity
- **Resources**: which team members and specialists to assign, when the work will be done, how much time each area needs, and how the team will be directed, supervised, and reviewed

### Audit Plan

The audit plan is more detailed. It describes:

- The **nature, timing, and extent** of planned risk assessment procedures
- The **nature, timing, and extent** of planned further audit procedures (tests of controls and substantive procedures) at the assertion level
- Any **other procedures** required by the standards

:::tip[Exam Tip]

Think of the strategy as the "what and why" and the plan as the "how." The strategy is set first and guides the plan, but both are documented and both are updated as the audit progresses.

:::

**Example:** For Bear Co., the engagement partner sets the strategy: all five operating segments are in scope, interim fieldwork begins October 15 and year-end work finishes in February, and two staff members with industry experience are assigned because the revenue arrangements are complex. The audit plan then lists the specific procedures for each segment, such as revenue cutoff testing, receivable confirmations, and inventory observations.

## Planning Materials: Starting From Last Year

Most audits are recurring engagements, so the starting point is usually the **prior-year audit plan** or the firm's template. The danger is simply rolling last year's work forward. The auditor updates the plan for what has changed: new accounting standards, new systems, acquisitions or disposals, changes in key personnel, and the misstatements and control deficiencies found last year.

Two supporting documents keep the plan practical:

- **Client assistance request list** (often called a PBC, or "prepared by client," list): the schedules, reconciliations, and documents the client will provide, with an owner and due date for each item
- **Time budget**: the hours planned for each area and each staff level, built from last year's actual hours and adjusted for changes; actual hours are compared with the budget during the audit

**Example:** Polar Inc. replaced its accounting system in the middle of the year. The auditor adds IT audit work on the new system and the data conversion, increases the budgeted hours for cash and revenue, and adds to the client assistance request list a reconciliation of balances converted from the old system to the new one.

The auditor also communicates an overview of the **planned scope and timing** of the audit, including the significant risks identified, to those charged with governance (see [Communication With Management and Governance](../ethics-professional-responsibilities-and-general-principles/communication-with-management-and-governance.md)).

## Engagement Partner Responsibilities

The **engagement partner** is responsible for the audit and its quality. Under AU-C 220, as revised by **SAS 146** for engagements for periods beginning on or after December 15, 2025, the engagement partner takes **overall responsibility for managing and achieving quality** on the engagement and must be sufficiently and appropriately involved throughout it. PCAOB AS 1000 sets out the engagement partner's responsibilities for issuer audits in similar terms. The partner may assign tasks to others but cannot assign away that responsibility.

The partner's responsibilities include:

- Making sure the team collectively has the **competence and capabilities**, and the time, to perform the audit
- **Directing, supervising, and reviewing** the work of team members
- Making sure **consultation** takes place on difficult or contentious matters
- Being satisfied that **sufficient appropriate evidence** supports the conclusions and that the **auditor's report** is appropriate

**Example:** Reviewing the inventory valuation work at Panda Industries, the engagement partner sees that the sample was too small for the assessed risk and directs the team to expand it. The partner, not the senior who chose the sample, is responsible for the evidence being sufficient.

## Factors That Shape Planning

| Factor | Planning Implication |
|---|---|
| **Size and complexity** | Larger, more complex entities need more staff, more time, and often specialists |
| **Prior experience** | Recurring engagements build on prior knowledge; first-year audits need more planning, including procedures on opening balances |
| **Industry and regulation** | Highly regulated industries such as banking and health care bring specialized requirements |
| **Internal control** | Effective controls may support a reliance strategy; weak controls call for more substantive work |
| **Identified risks** | Higher-risk areas get more experienced staff and more persuasive evidence |
| **Information technology** | The IT environment shapes both the entity's controls and the auditor's approach, including data analytics |
| **Going concern indicators** | Conditions that suggest doubt call for additional procedures |
| **Group structure** | Multiple components require coordinating with component auditors |

**Example:** Cub Entertainment is a small private company with simple operations, so its audit plan is lean: a small team and a short timeline. Grizzly Inc. is a multinational manufacturer with foreign subsidiaries, complex supply chains, and significant estimates. Its planning is far more extensive, including component auditors and specialists.

## Audit Documentation Basics

**Audit documentation** (working papers) is the record of the procedures performed, the evidence obtained, and the conclusions reached. It must be sufficient for an **experienced auditor** with no previous connection to the engagement to understand the nature, timing, and extent of the procedures, the results and evidence, the significant matters and conclusions, and who performed and reviewed the work and when.

Documentation is traditionally organized into two files:

| File | Contents | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Current file** | Documentation for **this year's** audit | Trial balance, lead schedules, audit programs, confirmations, analytical procedures, adjusting entries, the representation letter |
| **Permanent file** | Information of **continuing significance** carried from year to year | Articles of incorporation, bylaws, organizational charts, long-term contracts and debt agreements, prior internal control documentation |

After the report is released, the auditor has a limited time to assemble the final file:

| | AICPA (Nonissuers) | PCAOB (Issuers) |
|---|---|---|
| **Documentation completion date** | No later than **60 days** after the report release date | No later than **14 days** after the report release date |
| **Retention** | At least **5 years** from the report release date | **7 years** from the report release date |

The PCAOB's 14-day period came with **AS 1000**, which replaced the former 45-day period for audits of fiscal years beginning on or after December 15, 2024 (one year later for firms that issued audit reports for 100 or fewer issuers in 2024). Older study materials may still show 45 days. After the completion date, nothing may be deleted or discarded before the end of the retention period. The [Engagement Documentation](../ethics-professional-responsibilities-and-general-principles/engagement-documentation.md) chapter covers these rules in depth.

## Financial Statement Assertions

**Assertions** are the representations, explicit or implicit, that management makes when it presents financial statements. A balance sheet line reading "Accounts receivable, \$4 million" asserts that the receivables exist, that all of them are included, that the entity owns them, and that they are stated at the right amount. The auditor uses assertions to decide what could go wrong and to design procedures that address each risk.

Under AU-C 315, as revised by SAS 145, the assertions fall into two groups, each of which includes the related disclosures:

| Assertion | Classes of Transactions and Events | Account Balances | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Occurrence** | Yes | | Recorded transactions and events actually happened and pertain to the entity |
| **Existence** | | Yes | Recorded assets, liabilities, and equity interests exist |
| **Rights and obligations** | | Yes | The entity holds the rights to its assets, and its liabilities are its obligations |
| **Completeness** | Yes | Yes | Everything that should be recorded or disclosed has been |
| **Accuracy** (transactions) / **Accuracy, valuation, and allocation** (balances) | Yes | Yes | Amounts are recorded correctly and balances are stated at appropriate amounts |
| **Cutoff** | Yes | | Transactions are recorded in the correct period |
| **Classification** | Yes | Yes | Items are recorded in the proper accounts |
| **Presentation** | Yes | Yes | Items are appropriately aggregated or disaggregated and clearly described, and disclosures are relevant and understandable |

PCAOB standards (AS 1105) group the assertions into five: **existence or occurrence**, **completeness**, **valuation or allocation**, **rights and obligations**, and **presentation and disclosure**. The ideas are the same; only the grouping differs.

**Example:** For Sloth Security's accounts receivable at year-end, the auditor asks:

- **Existence:** Do the recorded receivables represent amounts real customers owe?
- **Completeness:** Have all amounts customers owe been recorded?
- **Accuracy, valuation, and allocation:** Are receivables stated at the amount expected to be collected, after the allowance for credit losses?
- **Rights and obligations:** Does Sloth own the receivables, or were some sold or pledged?

:::tip[Exam Tip]

Questions often ask you to match a procedure to the assertion it addresses. Confirming receivables mainly tests **existence**. Searching for unrecorded liabilities after year-end mainly tests **completeness** of payables. Reviewing the aging and subsequent collections tests **valuation**.

:::

## Summary

| Topic | Key Takeaway |
|---|---|
| **Preliminary activities** | Acceptance or continuance, ethics and independence, terms of the engagement |
| **Overall audit strategy** | Scope, reporting objectives, significant factors, and resources |
| **Audit plan** | Nature, timing, and extent of risk assessment and further procedures |
| **Planning materials** | Update the prior-year plan for changes; client assistance request list; time budget |
| **Engagement partner** | Overall responsibility for managing and achieving quality (SAS 146; AS 1000) |
| **Documentation completion** | 60 days (AICPA) or 14 days (PCAOB) after the report release date; retention 5 or 7 years |
| **Assertions** | Transactions: occurrence, completeness, accuracy, cutoff, classification, presentation; balances: existence, rights and obligations, completeness, accuracy valuation and allocation, classification, presentation |
