# Risk Assessment Procedures

The modern audit is **risk-based**. Instead of testing everything equally, the auditor identifies where material misstatements are most likely and concentrates the work there. The previous chapter described what the auditor learns about the entity; this chapter explains how the auditor turns that understanding into an assessment of risk. It covers the risk assessment procedures themselves, the audit risk model, inherent risk and the inherent risk factors introduced by SAS 145, control risk, significant risks, the "stand-back" requirement, and how the assessed risk sets the level of detection risk and the substantive work that follows. The AICPA standard is AU-C 315, as revised by SAS 145; the PCAOB standard is AS 2110.

---

## The Procedures

**Risk assessment procedures** are the procedures the auditor performs to understand the entity and its environment, the applicable financial reporting framework, and its system of internal control, and to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement. They are required in every audit and consist of:

- **Inquiries** of management, those charged with governance, internal audit, and others within the entity
- **Analytical procedures**, usually on high-level data, to spot unusual amounts and relationships
- **Observation and inspection**, such as touring facilities and reading minutes, business plans, and policy manuals

The engagement team also **discusses** how the entity's financial statements could be materially misstated, including through fraud. Risk assessment procedures tell the auditor where to look. They do not, on their own, provide sufficient appropriate evidence for the opinion.

---

## The Audit Risk Model

**Audit risk** is the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated; in practice, the risk of giving a clean opinion on materially misstated statements. The auditor reduces audit risk to an acceptably low level.

$$\text{Audit Risk} = \text{RMM} \times \text{Detection Risk}$$

The **risk of material misstatement (RMM)** is the risk that the statements are materially misstated **before** the audit. It is the entity's risk, made up of inherent risk and control risk:

$$\text{RMM} = \text{Inherent Risk} \times \text{Control Risk}$$

**Detection risk** is the risk that the auditor's procedures will fail to detect a misstatement that exists and could be material. The auditor does not control inherent risk or control risk; it **assesses** them. Detection risk is the only component the auditor sets, by choosing the nature, timing, and extent of procedures.

---

## Inherent Risk and the Inherent Risk Factors

**Inherent risk** is the susceptibility of an assertion to a misstatement that could be material, **before considering any related controls**. SAS 145 asks the auditor to assess inherent risk by considering the **inherent risk factors**, the characteristics of events or conditions that affect how likely an assertion is to be misstated:

| Inherent Risk Factor | What It Captures | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Complexity** | Information that is hard to prepare or calculate | Derivatives, multiple-element revenue contracts, complex consolidations |
| **Subjectivity** | Limits on objectivity because judgment is needed | Fair value estimates, allowances, impairment tests |
| **Change** | Events or conditions that alter the business or its reporting | New accounting standards, new systems, acquisitions, new regulations |
| **Uncertainty** | Amounts that cannot be measured precisely | Litigation outcomes, long-range forecasts, warranty reserves |
| **Susceptibility to misstatement due to management bias or other fraud risk factors** | Pressure or opportunity to shade the numbers or misappropriate assets | Bonus targets tied to earnings, cash and easily sold inventory |

Inherent risk is assessed on a **spectrum**, from lower to higher, by considering the **likelihood** of a misstatement and its potential **magnitude** together. A risk that is both quite likely and potentially large sits near the top of the spectrum.

**Example:** Cub Entertainment produces feature films and must estimate the ultimate revenue from each film to amortize its capitalized costs. The estimate is highly subjective and uncertain, and management's bonuses depend on reported earnings. Three inherent risk factors point the same way, so inherent risk for the film cost asset is near the upper end of the spectrum.

---

## Control Risk

**Control risk** is the risk that a material misstatement will **not be prevented, or detected and corrected, on a timely basis** by the entity's internal control. SAS 145 requires the auditor to assess control risk **separately** from inherent risk. Control risk can be assessed below the maximum only if the auditor plans to **test** the operating effectiveness of the related controls. If the auditor does not plan to test them, control risk is at the **maximum**, and the risk of material misstatement equals inherent risk. Even effective controls cannot reduce control risk to zero, because all controls have inherent limitations.

**Example:** Sloth Security separates its cash disbursement duties: one person prepares payments, another approves them, and a third reconciles the bank account. The auditor tests these controls, finds them operating effectively, and assesses control risk for the disbursement assertions as low.

---

## Financial Statement Level and Assertion Level

The auditor identifies and assesses risks at two levels:

- **Financial statement level** risks are **pervasive**: they affect many assertions at once. A weak control environment, doubts about management's integrity, and going concern problems are examples. They call for **overall responses**, such as assigning more experienced staff, increasing supervision, and building unpredictability into the procedures.
- **Assertion level** risks relate to specific classes of transactions, account balances, and disclosures, such as the valuation of inventory. They call for **further audit procedures** aimed at those assertions.

An assertion with an identified risk of material misstatement is a **relevant assertion**, and an account, class of transactions, or disclosure with at least one relevant assertion is **significant**.

---

## Significant Risks

A **significant risk** is an identified risk of material misstatement for which the assessed inherent risk is **close to the upper end of the spectrum**, or one that another standard says must be treated as significant. The risks that must always be treated as significant include the **fraud risks** (the presumed risk in revenue recognition and the risk of management override) and significant related party transactions outside the normal course of business.

Significant risks carry extra requirements:

- The auditor evaluates the **design and implementation** of the controls that address them.
- The auditor performs substantive procedures that respond **specifically** to each one. If the response is substantive procedures only, it must include **tests of details**.
- If the auditor relies on controls over a significant risk, those controls must be tested in the **current period**.

---

## The Stand-Back Requirement

After identifying the significant classes of transactions, account balances, and disclosures, the auditor **stands back** and looks at the material items that were **not** identified as significant. For each one, the auditor evaluates whether that conclusion is still appropriate. This guards against an overlooked risk. Separately, the auditor must perform substantive procedures for every material class of transactions, account balance, and disclosure, whether or not it was assessed as significant.

---

## Detection Risk and the Auditor's Response

Detection risk moves **opposite** to the risk of material misstatement. The higher the assessed RMM, the lower the detection risk the auditor can accept, and the more persuasive the substantive evidence must be.

| Assessed RMM | Acceptable Detection Risk | Auditor's Response |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Low | More extensive, more persuasive substantive procedures |
| **Low** | Higher | Less extensive procedures may be enough |

The auditor lowers detection risk by changing the **nature**, **timing**, and **extent** of procedures:

| Dimension | Lower Detection Risk (More Work) | Higher Detection Risk (Less Work) |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | More reliable procedures, such as external confirmations and tests of details | Analytical procedures or less rigorous tests may suffice |
| **Timing** | At or near the balance sheet date | Interim testing may be acceptable |
| **Extent** | Larger samples, more items tested | Smaller samples |

Detection risk can never be zero. Sampling, the choice of procedures, and human error in performing or evaluating them all leave some risk.

**Example:** Panda Industries licenses its products under contracts with variable consideration. Inherent risk for revenue is high (complexity and subjectivity), and the auditor does not plan to rely on controls, so control risk is at the maximum. With RMM high, the auditor sets detection risk low: it reads and recalculates revenue contract by contract (nature), concentrates the work at year-end (timing), and tests a larger sample of contracts (extent).

:::tip[Exam Tip]

If a question describes higher inherent risk (complex estimates, fraud risk factors, unusual transactions) or ineffective controls, the right answer involves **more persuasive** substantive work: more reliable procedures, testing closer to year-end, and larger samples.

:::

Once procedures are performed, the misstatements found are classified as factual, judgmental, or projected and evaluated against materiality, as described in [Misstatements and Internal Control Deficiencies](../completing-the-audit-and-reporting/misstatements-and-internal-control-deficiencies.md).

## Summary

| Concept | Key Point |
|---|---|
| **Risk assessment procedures** | Inquiry, analytical procedures, observation and inspection, plus the team discussion; required in every audit; not sufficient alone |
| **Audit risk model** | AR = RMM × DR, where RMM = IR × CR |
| **Inherent risk factors** | Complexity, subjectivity, change, uncertainty, and susceptibility to management bias or other fraud risk factors |
| **Spectrum of inherent risk** | Likelihood and magnitude assessed together |
| **Control risk** | Assessed separately; maximum unless controls will be tested, so RMM then equals inherent risk |
| **Significant risks** | Near the upper end of the spectrum, plus fraud risks; specific responses and tests of details if substantive only |
| **Stand-back** | Re-evaluate material items not identified as significant |
| **Detection risk** | Set by the auditor; inversely related to RMM; never zero |
