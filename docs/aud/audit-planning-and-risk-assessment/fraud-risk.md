# Fraud Risk

Fraud is what separates a misstatement that someone intended from one that happened by mistake, and that difference changes how the auditor must work. Management and those charged with governance are primarily responsible for preventing and detecting fraud. The auditor's responsibility is to plan and perform the audit to obtain **reasonable assurance** that the financial statements are free of material misstatement, **whether caused by fraud or error**. This chapter defines the two kinds of fraud that matter to an audit, explains the fraud triangle, walks through the auditor's required steps, lists common fraud risk factors, and covers the two fraud risks presumed in every audit: revenue recognition and management override of controls. The AICPA standard is AU-C 240; the PCAOB standard is AS 2401.

---

## What Fraud Is

**Fraud** is an intentional act by one or more individuals among management, those charged with governance, employees, or third parties, involving deception to obtain an unjust or illegal advantage. **Intent** is what distinguishes fraud from **error**. The auditor does not make legal determinations of whether fraud occurred; the auditor's concern is fraud that causes a material misstatement.

Fraud is harder to detect than error because it is concealed, sometimes through forged documents or collusion among several people. Fraud by management is harder to detect than fraud by employees, because management can override controls and direct others. That is why even a well-planned audit carries a higher risk of missing a material fraud than a material error, and why the standards require a specific, skeptical approach.

---

## Two Types of Fraud

### Fraudulent Financial Reporting

**Fraudulent financial reporting** is an intentional misstatement or omission designed to deceive users of the financial statements. It is usually committed by **management** and may involve:

- Manipulating, falsifying, or altering accounting records or supporting documents
- Misrepresenting or intentionally omitting events, transactions, or other significant information
- Intentionally misapplying accounting principles for amounts, classification, presentation, or disclosure

**Example:** Polar Inc.'s CFO tells the accounting staff to record fictitious sales in the last week of the year so that earnings meet analysts' expectations.

### Misappropriation of Assets

**Misappropriation of assets** is theft or misuse of the entity's assets. It can be committed by **employees at any level** and is often accompanied by false records to hide the loss. Examples include embezzling cash receipts, stealing inventory, paying fictitious vendors, and using company assets for personal purposes.

**Example:** A warehouse supervisor at Grizzly Inc. diverts finished goods to a personal storage unit, sells them, and adjusts the inventory records to hide the shortages.

Fraudulent financial reporting usually involves management and misstates the statements directly, typically by overstating revenue or assets or understating liabilities and expenses. Misappropriation is usually committed by employees and is often immaterial, though it can be material when amounts are large or the concealment distorts the records.

## The Fraud Triangle

The **fraud triangle** describes three conditions that are generally present when fraud occurs. AU-C 240 organizes fraud risk factors around them.

| Condition | What It Means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Incentive or pressure** | A reason to commit fraud | Earnings targets, bonuses tied to results, debt covenants, analyst expectations, personal debts |
| **Opportunity** | Circumstances that allow fraud to be committed | Weak or absent controls, poor segregation of duties, ineffective oversight, complex structures, management able to override controls |
| **Attitude or rationalization** | A mindset that lets the person justify the act | "I'm only borrowing it." "The company owes me." "Everyone does it." |

**Example:** An accounts payable clerk at Sloth Security sets up a fictitious vendor and pays its invoices. The **opportunity** exists because the same person can create vendors and approve payments. The **pressure** is mounting personal credit card debt. The **rationalization** is "I've been underpaid for years, so this is what the company owes me."

Internal controls act mainly on **opportunity**. Strong controls reduce it, but they cannot remove pressure or rationalization, and management can override controls. That is why management override is treated as a fraud risk in every audit.

## The Auditor's Required Steps

### 1. Maintain Professional Skepticism

The auditor approaches the audit with a questioning mind, recognizing that a material misstatement due to fraud could exist **regardless of past experience** with the honesty and integrity of management. Records and documents are generally accepted as genuine, but if conditions suggest a document is not authentic or has been altered, the auditor investigates further.

### 2. Discuss Fraud Risks as a Team

The engagement partner and key members of the engagement team hold a **discussion** (often called brainstorming) about how and where the entity's financial statements could be materially misstated due to fraud, including how management could commit and conceal fraudulent financial reporting and how assets could be misappropriated. The discussion sets aside any belief that management is honest.

### 3. Gather Information to Identify Fraud Risks

As part of risk assessment, the auditor:

- **Inquires of management** about its assessment of fraud risk, its process for identifying and responding to fraud risks, and any known, suspected, or alleged fraud
- **Inquires of those charged with governance** about their oversight of that process and whether they know of any fraud, and also inquires of internal audit and others within the entity
- Evaluates **unusual or unexpected relationships** found in analytical procedures, especially those involving revenue
- Considers **fraud risk factors** and other information obtained during the audit

### 4. Identify and Assess Fraud Risks

The auditor identifies the risks of material misstatement due to fraud at the financial statement level and the assertion level. Assessed fraud risks are treated as **significant risks**, so the auditor must evaluate the design and implementation of the entity's related controls.

### 5. Respond to the Assessed Risks

Responses work at two levels:

- **Overall responses**: assigning more experienced staff and closer supervision, evaluating whether management's choice of accounting policies (especially for subjective measurements and complex transactions) suggests fraudulent reporting, and building **unpredictability** into the audit (testing accounts or locations not normally tested, changing the timing, using different sampling methods)
- **Assertion-level responses**: changing the nature, timing, and extent of procedures, for example confirming contract terms with customers in addition to balances, or counting inventory at year-end at locations chosen without notice

### 6. Evaluate the Evidence

The auditor considers whether analytical procedures performed near the end of the audit indicate a previously unrecognized fraud risk. When a misstatement is found, the auditor considers whether it may be **intentional**. If it may be, the auditor evaluates the implications, especially for management's integrity and the reliability of its representations, because a fraud is unlikely to be an isolated event.

### 7. Communicate

If the auditor has identified fraud, or has information indicating that fraud may exist, the auditor brings it to the attention of the **appropriate level of management** on a timely basis. Fraud involving senior management, employees with significant roles in internal control, or anyone else when the fraud causes a material misstatement is communicated to **those charged with governance**. Reporting outside the entity is ordinarily prevented by confidentiality, unless a legal or regulatory requirement, a subpoena, a successor auditor's inquiries, or government audit requirements call for it.

## Fraud Risk Factors

**Fraud risk factors** are events or conditions that indicate an incentive or pressure to commit fraud, provide an opportunity to commit it, or suggest an attitude or rationalization that justifies it. A risk factor does not mean fraud has occurred, but the auditor must consider each one.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Financial pressure** | Declining profitability or cash flow; compensation heavily tied to aggressive targets; pressure to meet debt covenants |
| **Complex or unusual transactions** | Significant transactions near year-end; significant related party transactions outside the normal course of business |
| **Governance weaknesses** | Management dominated by one person or a small group; ineffective board or audit committee oversight |
| **Accounting warning signs** | Large or unusual journal entries at period end; recurring significant adjustments; aggressive estimates |
| **Attitude** | Excessive interest in the stock price; disregard for controls or regulators; frequent disputes with the auditor |
| **Employee behavior** | Living beyond one's means; refusing to take vacation; guarding duties from others |
| **Documentation** | Missing or altered documents; unexplained reconciling items; excessive credits or voids |

**Example:** At Bear Co., the CEO personally approves all journal entries over \$10,000 with no one reviewing his approvals, and several large nonroutine entries were recorded in the last week of the year. The concentration of authority and the unusual year-end entries raise the assessed risk of fraudulent financial reporting.

## Revenue Recognition: A Presumed Fraud Risk

The auditor must **presume** that there are risks of fraud in revenue recognition and evaluate which types of revenue, revenue transactions, or assertions give rise to them. Revenue earns this presumption because it is usually the largest line item, it often involves judgments about timing and measurement, management is under pressure to meet revenue targets, and it is easy to manipulate through:

- Fictitious sales
- Premature recognition, before the performance obligation is satisfied
- Channel stuffing (pushing customers to take goods early)
- Improper cutoff (recording next period's sales this period)
- Bill-and-hold arrangements without substance
- Side agreements that change the terms of a sale

The presumption is **rebuttable**, but only when the auditor concludes it does not apply to the engagement (for example, a single type of simple rental revenue). The auditor must document the reasons.

**Example:** Cub Entertainment earns revenue from film licensing, theatrical distribution, and merchandise, each with different recognition patterns. The auditor identifies fraud risks in the timing of licensing revenue and in year-end merchandise cutoff. The responses include reading license agreements to check when control passes, testing merchandise sales on either side of year-end, and comparing revenue by stream with industry trends.

---

## Management Override: A Fraud Risk in Every Audit

Management can override controls that otherwise appear effective. Because that risk exists in **every** entity, it is a significant risk that **cannot be rebutted**. Whatever the auditor's assessment of other risks, the auditor must:

1. **Test the appropriateness of journal entries** recorded in the general ledger and other adjustments made in preparing the statements, with a focus on entries made at the end of the period
2. **Review accounting estimates for bias**, including a retrospective review of the prior year's significant estimates to see how they turned out
3. **Evaluate the business rationale** for significant transactions that are outside the normal course of business or otherwise unusual

:::danger[Common Exam Trap]

Revenue recognition is a **rebuttable** presumption; management override is **not**. The three override procedures (journal entries, estimates, and unusual transactions) are required in every audit, regardless of the assessed risk.

:::

## Looking Ahead: SAS 151

The AICPA's Auditing Standards Board has approved **SAS 151**, *The Auditor's Responsibilities Relating to Fraud in an Audit of Financial Statements*, which will supersede AU-C 240. It adds a stronger fraud focus to risk assessment, requires the auditor to understand the entity's whistleblower or fraud-reporting program, and expands the responses and communications required when fraud is identified or suspected, while keeping the revenue recognition presumption. It is effective for periods ending on or after December 15, 2028, so it is not expected to be tested before 2029; the exam continues to test AU-C 240 as described in this chapter.

## Summary

| Topic | Key Takeaway |
|---|---|
| **Fraud vs. error** | Intent is the difference; fraud is harder to detect because it is concealed |
| **Fraudulent financial reporting** | Intentional misstatement, usually by management |
| **Misappropriation of assets** | Theft or misuse of assets, by employees at any level |
| **Fraud triangle** | Incentive or pressure, opportunity, attitude or rationalization |
| **Auditor's responsibility** | Reasonable (not absolute) assurance; skepticism; team discussion; fraud risks are significant risks |
| **Revenue recognition** | Presumed fraud risk; rebuttable with documentation |
| **Management override** | Present in every audit; not rebuttable; journal entries, estimates, unusual transactions |
