# Auditing Inventory and Cost of Sales

Inventory is often one of the largest assets on the balance sheet, and it is hard to audit from the records alone. Quantities can be inflated, goods can be counted that belong to someone else, and items that will never sell can sit on the books at full cost. Because ending inventory flows straight into cost of sales, an error in one is an error in the other. This chapter follows the inventory audit in the order the auditor performs it: observing the physical count, dealing with inventory held by others, testing valuation, testing cutoff, and using analytical procedures on cost of sales.

---

## Observing the Physical Count

When inventory is material, AU-C 501 (and PCAOB AS 2510 for issuers) requires the auditor to obtain evidence about its **existence and condition** by **attending the physical count**, unless that is impracticable, and by performing procedures on the final inventory records to confirm that they reflect the count results.

Management takes the count; the auditor observes it. The auditor's job is to evaluate whether management's counting procedures are adequate and are being followed, and to make enough test counts to corroborate the results.

The work falls into three stages:

1. **Before the count**, the auditor reviews management's written count instructions to confirm they provide for an orderly, complete count: prenumbered tags or count sheets, two-person teams, a plan for identifying damaged and obsolete goods, and a halt to shipping and receiving during the count.
2. **During the count**, the auditor tours the facility, observes whether count teams follow the instructions, and makes **test counts**. The auditor records the last receiving report and last shipping document used before the count, for later cutoff testing, and notes goods that look damaged, obsolete, or slow-moving. Goods held for others (such as goods held on consignment for a supplier) should be segregated and excluded.
3. **After the count**, the auditor traces the test counts to the final inventory listing, accounts for all prenumbered tags (used, unused, and voided), and tests the listing's accuracy by recalculating quantity times price and footing the totals.

:::tip[Dual-Direction Testing]

Test counts run in two directions. **From the floor to the count records** tests **completeness**: is every item on the shelves recorded? **From the count records to the floor** tests **existence**: does every recorded item actually exist?

:::

Not every count happens on the balance sheet date. If the count takes place before year-end, the auditor tests the transactions between the count date and year-end (a rollforward). If the entity keeps reliable perpetual records and counts sections of inventory throughout the year (cycle counts), the auditor observes some of those counts and evaluates the counting program. If attending the count is impracticable, for example because the auditor was engaged after year-end, the auditor performs alternative procedures, such as examining documentation of later sales of items on hand at the count date. If no alternative works, the auditor has a scope limitation.

**Example.** Grizzly Industries manufactures electronic components. At the year-end count, the auditor confirms that shipping and receiving are shut down, observes two-person teams at work, and makes 40 test counts in both directions. The auditor notes a bin of circuit boards for a discontinued product line and later traces all 40 test counts to the final listing. The obsolete boards become a valuation question for later in the audit.

## Inventory Held by Others

Some inventory sits outside the entity's own facilities: in a public warehouse, with a processor, or on consignment with a retailer. When that inventory is material, the auditor obtains evidence about its existence and condition by one or both of the following:

- **Requesting confirmation** from the third party of the quantities and condition of goods held for the entity.
- **Performing inspection or other procedures**, such as attending the third party's count, reviewing a SOC 1 report or another auditor's report on the warehouse's controls, or examining warehouse receipts.

Confirmation is strongest when the third party is independent and reputable. If the custodian is related to the entity, or the inventory is a large part of total assets, the auditor usually adds procedures beyond the confirmation. If the warehouse receipts have been pledged as collateral, the auditor may also confirm with the lender.

**Example.** Panda Industries stores \$1.8 million of finished goods in an independent public warehouse. The auditor sends a confirmation request directly to the warehouse, which replies with quantities that agree with Panda's records. Because the goods are material, the auditor also reviews the warehouse operator's SOC 1 Type 2 report on its inventory controls.

Ownership runs the other way, too. Goods the entity has shipped **out** on consignment still belong to the entity and belong in its inventory. Goods it holds **for** a consignor do not.

## Valuation

Inventory is measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value (lower of cost or market for entities using LIFO or the retail method). The auditor tests both halves:

- **Cost.** For purchased goods, the auditor vouches unit costs to vendor invoices and checks that the costing method (FIFO, weighted average) is applied correctly. For manufactured goods, the auditor tests how material, labor, and overhead are accumulated and how overhead is allocated.
- **Net realizable value.** The auditor compares carrying amounts to selling prices after year-end, less costs to complete and sell. An item that sold in January for less than its cost needs a write-down.

Obsolete and slow-moving goods are the classic valuation risk. Observation at the count helps, but it is not enough, because management knows things a walk-through will not reveal. The auditor **inquires** of management, production, and sales personnel about obsolete, damaged, or excess goods, and analyzes aging and turnover reports, often using data analytics to flag items with no sales in the last year.

**Example.** Cub Entertainment holds an inventory of DVDs and other physical media. When the auditor asks about obsolescence, management acknowledges that sales of physical media have fallen sharply as customers moved to streaming. The aging report shows that 30% of the titles have not sold in over 18 months. The auditor evaluates management's obsolescence reserve against these facts and proposes a larger write-down to net realizable value.

## Cutoff and Ownership

Cutoff testing makes sure inventory transactions near year-end land in the right period, using the receiving and shipping document numbers the auditor recorded at the count.

- **Purchases.** Goods received before year-end should be in inventory with the related payable recorded; goods received after year-end should be excluded from both. Goods in transit at year-end belong to the buyer if they were shipped **FOB shipping point**, even though they have not arrived.
- **Sales.** Goods shipped before year-end under FOB shipping point terms are excluded from inventory and recorded in cost of sales. Goods shipped under **FOB destination** terms remain the seller's inventory until they are delivered.

**Example.** Kodiak Partners' last receiving report before December 31 is #2945. The auditor confirms that the goods on #2945 were counted and that the related payable was recorded. Report #2946, dated January 2, covers goods that are properly excluded from both year-end inventory and accounts payable.

An inventory cutoff error always has a partner: a purchase recorded in the wrong period also misstates payables, and a sale recorded in the wrong period also misstates revenue and receivables. The related procedures appear in [Auditing Payables and Expenses](./auditing-payables-and-expenses.md) and [Auditing Receivables and Revenue](./auditing-receivables-and-revenue.md).

## Cost of Sales

Because cost of sales equals beginning inventory plus purchases minus ending inventory, much of the evidence for cost of sales comes from the inventory and purchases testing above. The auditor adds **analytical procedures**: comparing gross margin by product line and by month to the prior year, budgets, and industry data, and computing inventory turnover. A gross margin that jumps in the last month of the year can point to overstated ending inventory, since every dollar of overstated inventory is a dollar of understated cost of sales.

---

## Presentation and Disclosure

Finally, the auditor checks that the notes disclose the costing method, the major categories of inventory (raw materials, work in process, finished goods), significant write-downs, and any inventory **pledged as collateral**. Bear Co., for example, uses FIFO and reports \$2.1 million of raw materials, \$0.8 million of work in process, and \$3.5 million of finished goods. The auditor agrees these amounts to the final inventory listing and confirms that the note discloses the \$1.2 million of finished goods pledged under the company's revolving credit facility, which the bank confirmation also reported.

:::warning[Common Exam Trap]

The auditor **observes** the client's count and makes **test counts**; the auditor does not take the inventory. Responsibility for the count and the inventory records belongs to management.

:::
