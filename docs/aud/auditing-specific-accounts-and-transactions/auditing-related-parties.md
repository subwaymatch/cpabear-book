# Auditing Related Parties

A related party transaction is a deal between an entity and someone who can influence it, such as an owner, an officer, or a company they control. Such deals are legal and often ordinary, but they may not be negotiated at arm's length, and they can be used to dress up results: a sale to a company owned by the CEO looks like revenue even if it will never be collected. The auditing difficulty is that management knows its related parties far better than the auditor does and may not volunteer them. This chapter follows AU-C 550 (nonissuers) and PCAOB AS 2410 (issuers). It covers who counts as a related party, how the auditor identifies relationships and transactions, how the auditor responds to the transactions found, and the reporting and representation issues that follow.

---

## Who Is a Related Party

Under ASC 850, related parties include the entity's affiliates and equity method investees, trusts for the benefit of employees (such as pension plans managed by management), **principal owners** (owners of more than 10% of the voting interests) and their immediate families, **management** and their immediate families, and any other party that can significantly influence the entity's policies, or be significantly influenced by it, so that one party might be prevented from fully pursuing its own interests.

The financial statements must disclose material related party transactions: the nature of the relationship, a description of the transactions, the dollar amounts, and the balances owed at year-end. A disclosure may say that a transaction was made on terms equivalent to an arm's-length transaction only if management can substantiate that claim.

---

## Identifying Related Parties and Their Transactions

The auditor's work starts in risk assessment. The engagement team discusses how the entity could use related party transactions to misstate its financial statements or commit fraud. The auditor then asks management to identify its related parties, the nature of each relationship, and the transactions during the year, and obtains an understanding of the controls management uses to identify, authorize, approve, and record related party transactions.

Inquiry alone is not enough, because the risk is precisely that management will leave something out. Throughout the audit, the auditor stays alert for evidence of relationships management did not mention.

| Source | What it may reveal |
|---|---|
| **Board and committee minutes** | Approval of transactions with officers, directors, or affiliates |
| **Contracts, loan agreements, and guarantees** | Guarantees of an officer's personal debt, leases with entities owned by management |
| **Bank and legal confirmations** | Guarantees, pledged assets, or litigation involving related parties |
| **Conflict-of-interest questionnaires** | Officers' and directors' outside business interests |
| **Proxy statements and other SEC filings** (issuers) | Beneficial ownership and transactions with directors and officers |
| **Tax returns and ownership records** | Affiliated entities and owners |
| **Prior-year audit files and the predecessor auditor** | Related parties identified in earlier years |

Two kinds of transactions deserve special attention. **Significant unusual transactions** are those outside the normal course of business or otherwise unusual because of their timing, size, or nature, such as a large sale in the last week of the year with extended payment terms. The auditor evaluates their business purpose, because a transaction with no sensible business rationale may involve an undisclosed related party or be designed to misstate the financial statements. Under PCAOB standards, the auditor must also obtain an understanding of the company's financial relationships and transactions with its **executive officers**, such as employment and bonus arrangements, loans, and special perquisites, by reading employment contracts and the proxy statement, for example.

Other warning signs include prices or terms far from market (a loan to an officer at no interest, a purchase at twice the going rate), complex structures or entities with no apparent economic substance, and sales to or purchases from parties that share an address, owner, or officer with the entity.

**Example.** While auditing Grizzly Industries, the auditor learns from inquiry that the CEO's brother owns Panda Industries, which supplies promotional materials. The proxy statement shows that a director owns 15% of Cub Entertainment, a major customer. The prior-year file notes purchases from Kodiak Partners, a company co-owned by Grizzly's CFO. The auditor obtains a list of all transactions and year-end balances with all three companies and tests them as described below.

## Responding to Related Party Transactions

For each significant related party transaction identified, the auditor:

- **Reads the underlying agreements** and evaluates whether the transaction has a business purpose (or lacks one), whether its terms match management's explanation, and whether it was properly authorized and approved.
- **Evaluates the accounting and disclosure**, including whether the amounts and balances in the notes are complete and accurate.
- **Considers confirming** the amounts and terms with the related party, or with intermediaries such as banks or guarantors, when the evidence from the entity alone is not enough.
- For a large balance owed by a related party, **considers the related party's ability to pay**, for example by obtaining its financial statements.

A related party that management never disclosed is a serious finding. The auditor shares the information with the engagement team promptly, asks management to identify every transaction with that party, asks why the entity's controls failed to identify it, and performs substantive procedures on the transactions. The auditor also reconsiders whether other undisclosed related parties may exist. If the nondisclosure appears intentional, the auditor evaluates the implications for fraud and for the reliability of management's other representations.

:::warning[Arm's-Length Claims]

The auditor generally cannot confirm that a related party transaction was on the same terms as one with an unrelated party. If management's disclosure claims arm's-length terms, the auditor needs evidence to support that claim. If the evidence is not available and management will not remove or revise the statement, the auditor evaluates the effect on the opinion, which may require a modified opinion.

:::

## Representations and Communications

Management's written representations include a statement that management has disclosed to the auditor the identity of all related parties and all related party relationships and transactions of which it is aware, and that those relationships and transactions have been appropriately accounted for and disclosed.

The auditor communicates significant matters about related parties to those charged with governance. For issuers, PCAOB standards require the auditor to communicate its evaluation of the company's identification of, accounting for, and disclosure of related party relationships and transactions, along with other significant matters such as related parties that management did not disclose and significant unusual transactions.
