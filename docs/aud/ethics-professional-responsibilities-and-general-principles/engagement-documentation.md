# Engagement Documentation

If it is not documented, it was not done. That old saying is close to how the standards treat **engagement documentation** (the audit file, or workpapers). Documentation is the record of the procedures performed, the evidence obtained, and the conclusions reached, and it is the main support for the auditor's report. It is also what reviewers, inspectors, and courts look at later to decide whether the audit met the standards. This chapter explains what sufficient documentation looks like, the rules for assembling and retaining the file, what may change after the file is closed, and the differences between AICPA and PCAOB requirements, which the exam tests often.

For nonissuers, the standard is **AU-C 230**. For issuers, it is **AS 1215**. Documentation can be on paper or electronic; the requirements are the same either way.

---

## The Experienced Auditor Standard

The test for documentation is whether it would enable an **experienced auditor, having no previous connection with the engagement**, to understand:

1. the **nature, timing, and extent** of the procedures performed;
2. the **results** of those procedures and the **evidence** obtained;
3. the **significant findings or issues** that arose, the conclusions reached on them, and the **significant professional judgments** made in reaching those conclusions.

An experienced auditor here means someone who understands audit processes, the standards, the entity's business environment, and the accounting and auditing issues relevant to its industry. The standard is demanding on purpose. Oral explanations by the engagement team do not, on their own, support work that is not documented; they can only clarify what the file already shows.

Beyond supporting the opinion, documentation helps the team plan and supervise the work, lets reviewers and engagement quality reviewers evaluate it, provides a record for next year's audit, and supports external inspections and peer reviews.

---

## What Documentation Must Show

Documentation takes many forms: audit programs, planning and conclusion memos, lead schedules and reconciliations, confirmation responses, copies or abstracts of key contracts and minutes, checklists, emails, data analytics outputs, and screenshots from the client's systems. Whatever the form, the auditor records:

- the **identifying characteristics** of the specific items tested (for example, invoice numbers or transaction dates), so another auditor could find the same items;
- **who performed** the work and the date it was completed;
- **who reviewed** the work, and the date and extent of the review; and
- the **conclusions** reached.

The auditor also documents discussions of significant findings or issues with management, those charged with governance, and others, including what was discussed, when, and with whom. If the auditor identifies information that is inconsistent with the auditor's final conclusion on a significant matter, the file must show how the inconsistency was addressed. And if the auditor departs from a presumptively mandatory requirement, the file must give the justification and explain how alternative procedures achieved the requirement's intent.

**Example:** For Bear Co.'s accounts receivable, the team confirms 40 customer balances. The file shows how the sample was selected, lists each customer and balance, includes the confirmations sent and received, describes the alternative procedures for nonresponses, and concludes on whether receivables are materially misstated. The staff accountant who did the work and the senior who reviewed it each sign off with the date.

### Significant Matters and Judgments

The most important part of the file is the record of the hard calls. Significant findings and issues include matters involving significant risks, results indicating that the financial statements could be materially misstated or that the risk assessment needs revision, circumstances that caused significant difficulty in applying procedures, and findings that could lead to a modified report. For each, the auditor documents the basis for the conclusion, the alternatives considered, and any consultation with others.

**Example:** Honey Entertainment's goodwill impairment test uses a revenue growth rate well above industry forecasts. The workpapers document the team's evaluation of the rate, the alternative scenarios it ran, its discussion with the engagement partner and a valuation specialist, and its conclusion that an additional \$2.3 million impairment was needed.

## Assembly and Retention

After the auditor's report is released, the team has a limited window to assemble the final audit file. The **report release date** is the date the auditor grants the entity permission to use the report. The deadline for completing the final file is the **documentation completion date**.

| | AICPA (AU-C 230), nonissuers | PCAOB (AS 1215), issuers |
|---|---|---|
| **Documentation completion date** | No later than **60 days** after the report release date | No later than **14 days** after the report release date |
| **Minimum retention period** | **5 years** from the report release date | **7 years** from the report release date |

The PCAOB window used to be 45 days. **AS 1000**, adopted in 2024, shortened it to 14 days for audits of fiscal years beginning on or after December 15, 2024. Firms that issued audit reports for 100 or fewer issuers in 2024 received one more year, so the 14-day rule applies to them for fiscal years beginning on or after December 15, 2025.

During the assembly period, the team performs administrative work: organizing and cross-referencing the file, signing off on completion checklists, and discarding superseded drafts. Assembly is not a time to perform new procedures or reach new conclusions. Firms may keep files longer than the minimums, and laws or regulators may require them to.

**Example:** Panda Industries, a private company, receives its audit report on March 15. Under AU-C 230, the team must complete the final file by May 14, 60 days later. If Polar Inc., an issuer, receives its report on the same day, its file must be complete by March 29.

:::caution

Watch the entity type. Nonissuer: **60 days and 5 years**. Issuer: **14 days and 7 years**. Older study materials still show 45 days for the PCAOB, which is no longer correct.

:::

---

## Changes After the Documentation Completion Date

Once the documentation completion date passes, the file is locked. The auditor **must not delete or discard** any documentation before the end of the retention period. If the auditor later needs to add to or modify the file, the addition must show **when** it was made, **who** made and reviewed it, and the **specific reasons** for it. The original documentation stays in the file.

Additions typically arise when the auditor learns of a fact that existed at the report date (a subsequently discovered fact), or when an inspection or peer review requires the auditor to explain work that was performed but not clearly documented.

**Example:** Six months after the documentation completion date for Ursa Security's audit, the firm learns that a large receivable it confirmed was fictitious. The auditor adds a dated memorandum describing the discovery, the additional procedures performed, and the effect on the report. The original confirmation workpaper is not removed; it stays in the file alongside the new memo.

## PCAOB Requirements Beyond AU-C 230

The two standards share the experienced auditor test and most of the content requirements. The PCAOB adds several features worth knowing:

- **The 14-day and 7-year rules** described above.
- **A presumption about undocumented work.** If the documentation does not show that a procedure was performed, evidence was obtained, or a conclusion was reached, the auditor must demonstrate with persuasive other evidence that it was. Oral explanation alone is not persuasive other evidence.
- **An engagement completion document.** The auditor must identify all significant findings or issues in an engagement completion document, with cross-references to the supporting workpapers.
- **Work of other auditors.** The office issuing the report must obtain and retain specified documentation of work performed by other auditors, or have access to it.

**Example:** In Polar Inc.'s audit, the team tests journal entries for management override. The file lists each entry tested by entry number, date, and amount, along with the selection criteria and results. A note saying only "tested a sample of journal entries; no exceptions" would not meet either standard, because another auditor could not identify what was tested.

## Summary

| Topic | Key takeaway |
|---|---|
| **Standard of sufficiency** | An experienced auditor with no previous connection could understand the procedures, results, significant findings, and significant judgments |
| **Required elements** | Identifying characteristics of items tested, who performed and when, who reviewed and when, conclusions |
| **Documentation completion date** | 60 days after report release (AICPA); 14 days (PCAOB, under AS 1000) |
| **Retention** | 5 years (AICPA); 7 years (PCAOB), from the report release date |
| **After completion** | Nothing deleted; additions show when, who, and why |
| **PCAOB extras** | Presumption that undocumented work was not done; engagement completion document; documentation of other auditors' work |
