# Code of Professional Conduct

An audit opinion is only worth something if the person giving it is honest, competent, and independent of the client. The **AICPA Code of Professional Conduct** is where those expectations are written down, and every AICPA member must follow it. On the AUD exam you will be asked to recognize what the Code requires and, more often, to apply it to a situation: Is this relationship a threat? Does a specific rule prohibit it? Can safeguards fix it? This chapter starts with how the Code is organized and the principles behind it, then explains the conceptual framework that ties the rules together, and then works through the rules the exam tests most: independence, integrity and objectivity, confidentiality, and fees.

---

## How the Code Is Organized

The Code is divided into three parts according to the member's role: **Part 1** for members in public practice, **Part 2** for members in business, and **Part 3** for other members (for example, retired or unemployed members). Each part contains three kinds of guidance:

| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Principles** | Broad statements of the profession's ideals. They guide the rules but are not enforceable on their own. |
| **Rules** | Enforceable requirements. A violation can lead to discipline by the AICPA or a state board. |
| **Interpretations** | Explanations of how a rule applies in specific circumstances, issued by the Professional Ethics Executive Committee. A member who departs from an interpretation must be able to justify the departure. |

For an auditor, Part 1 matters most. Its main rules are summarized below. Independence takes up the largest share of the Code and of the exam, so it gets its own section later in this chapter.

| Rule | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **Integrity and Objectivity** | Be objective and honest, avoid conflicts of interest, do not knowingly misrepresent facts, and do not subordinate your judgment to others |
| **Independence** | Be independent when performing services that professional standards require independence for, such as audits, reviews, and examinations |
| **General Standards** | Undertake only work you can complete with professional competence; exercise due professional care; plan and supervise adequately; obtain sufficient relevant data |
| **Compliance With Standards** | Follow the standards issued by bodies the AICPA has designated, such as the Auditing Standards Board, the Accounting and Review Services Committee, and the PCAOB |
| **Accounting Principles** | Do not state that financial statements follow GAAP if they contain a material departure, unless unusual circumstances would make following GAAP misleading (then disclose the departure, its effects, and the reason) |
| **Acts Discreditable** | Do not commit acts that discredit the profession, such as keeping client records after the client asks for them back |
| **Contingent Fees** | Do not charge a contingent fee to a client for whom the firm performs an audit, review, examination of prospective financial information, or a compilation that third parties will use without an independence disclosure; never charge one for preparing an original or amended tax return |
| **Commissions and Referral Fees** | Do not accept a commission involving a client for whom the firm performs an audit, review, or similar attest service; disclose any permitted commission or referral fee to the client |
| **Advertising and Solicitation** | Do not advertise or solicit in a false, misleading, or deceptive way |
| **Confidential Client Information** | Do not disclose confidential client information without the client's specific consent, except as the rule permits |
| **Form of Organization and Name** | Practice only in a permitted form of organization, under a name that is not misleading |

## The Six Principles

The principles describe the kind of professional the rules are trying to produce. Members should exercise sensitive professional and moral judgment in all their activities (**Responsibilities**) and act in a way that serves the public, which includes clients, investors, creditors, employers, and governments (**The Public Interest**). They should perform their work with **Integrity**, which means being honest and candid within the limits of client confidentiality and never putting personal gain ahead of the public trust. They should be objective and free of conflicts of interest and, in public practice, independent when providing attest services (**Objectivity and Independence**). They should follow technical and ethical standards and keep improving their competence (**Due Care**). Finally, members in public practice should consider all of these principles when deciding which services to offer a client (**Scope and Nature of Services**).

Independence has two sides, and the exam tests both. **Independence of mind** (sometimes called independence in fact) is the auditor's actual state of mind: free of influences that would compromise judgment. **Independence in appearance** means avoiding circumstances that would lead a reasonable, informed third party to conclude that the auditor's objectivity has been compromised. An auditor who is perfectly objective but owns stock in the client still lacks independence in appearance.

## The Conceptual Framework Approach

No list of rules can anticipate every situation. The Code therefore includes **conceptual frameworks**: one for members in public practice, one for members in business, and one specifically for independence. They all work the same way. When a relationship or circumstance is not addressed by a specific rule or interpretation, the member:

1. **Identifies threats** to compliance with the rules (for independence, threats to the auditor's independence).
2. **Evaluates the significance** of each threat. A threat is at an acceptable level if a reasonable and informed third party, weighing all the facts, would likely conclude that compliance with the rules is not compromised.
3. **Identifies and applies safeguards** to eliminate the threat or reduce it to an acceptable level. Safeguards can be created by the profession or by law, applied by the client, or applied by the firm (for example, an additional partner review).
4. **Declines or ends the service** if no safeguard reduces the threat to an acceptable level. For an attest client, that means independence is impaired.

The order matters. If a specific rule or interpretation addresses the situation, the member follows it, and safeguards cannot rescue a relationship that a rule prohibits. The framework fills the gaps between the rules.

**Example:** Bear Co. asks its audit firm to train its staff on a new accounting system. No interpretation addresses this exact service. The auditor identifies a possible management participation threat (the firm could end up running the system), judges it modest if the training is general, and applies safeguards: Bear Co. management makes every decision about how the system is configured and used. With those safeguards, the threat is at an acceptable level and the firm can provide the training.

## The Seven Threats

The Code names seven categories of threats. Most exam questions in this area describe a situation and ask which threat it creates, so it helps to attach each threat to a simple picture.

| Threat | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Adverse interest** | The member's interests are opposed to the client's | The firm and Kodiak Partners, an audit client, are suing each other |
| **Advocacy** | The member promotes the client's position so far that objectivity suffers | The firm promotes Grizzly Industries' shares to prospective investors |
| **Familiarity** | A long or close relationship makes the member too sympathetic to the client | The engagement partner has audited Honey Entertainment for 15 years and vacations with its CFO |
| **Management participation** | The member takes on management responsibilities | The auditor hires staff and approves vendor payments for Panda Records |
| **Self-interest** | The member could benefit from an interest in, or relationship with, the client | The engagement partner owns shares of Bear Co., or the firm depends on Bear Co. for a large share of its fees |
| **Self-review** | The member evaluates the results of the firm's own earlier work | The firm prepared the depreciation schedules that support Polar Inc.'s fixed asset balances and now audits those balances |
| **Undue influence** | Pressure from the client or others causes the member to subordinate judgment | Ursa Security's CEO threatens to replace the firm unless the auditor accepts an aggressive accounting position |

## Independence

### Who Must Be Independent

Independence rules apply to **covered members**. A covered member is:

- an individual on the attest engagement team;
- an individual in a position to influence the attest engagement, such as a supervisor of the engagement partner;
- a partner or manager who provides nonattest services to the attest client (once those services reach 10 hours in a fiscal year);
- a partner in the office where the lead attest engagement partner primarily practices;
- the firm itself, including its employee benefit plans; and
- an entity whose policies any of the above can control.

A covered member's **immediate family** (spouse, spousal equivalent, and dependents) is generally subject to the same rules as the covered member. **Close relatives** (parents, siblings, and nondependent children) are treated more leniently, as described below.

A revised definition of the **attest engagement team** takes effect for attest engagements beginning on or after December 15, 2026. It states explicitly that internal auditors, the auditor's external specialists, and referred-to auditors and practitioners are not part of the team. Under the AICPA's policy for new pronouncements, the revised definition should become eligible for testing in the second quarter of 2027.

### Financial Interests

A **direct financial interest** in an attest client, such as stock, bonds, or a partnership interest held by a covered member or the member's immediate family, impairs independence no matter how small. An **indirect financial interest**, held through an intermediary like a diversified mutual fund, impairs independence only if it is material to the covered member.

**Example:** An audit partner on the Grizzly Industries engagement inherits 10 shares of Grizzly Industries stock. A direct interest of any size would normally impair independence. Because the shares were unsolicited (an inheritance), independence is not impaired if the partner disposes of them as soon as practicable, and no later than 30 days after learning of the shares and gaining the right to sell them. If the partner instead holds a diversified mutual fund that owns some Grizzly Industries stock, that is an indirect interest, and independence is impaired only if the partner's interest is material to the partner.

**Loans** between a covered member and an attest client also impair independence, with narrow exceptions for loans from a financial institution client made under its normal lending procedures. Home mortgages and other loans obtained before the lender became an attest client may be kept ("grandfathered") if they stay current under their original terms. A covered member may also obtain certain low-risk loans at any time, such as auto loans collateralized by the vehicle, loans secured by the cash surrender value of an insurance policy or by cash deposits, and credit cards with balances paid down to \$10,000 or less by the payment due date.

### Employment and Family Relationships

Independence is impaired if a covered member's **immediate family member** holds a **key position** at an attest client. A key position is one with primary responsibility for significant accounting functions or the preparation of the financial statements, or the ability to influence their contents, such as CEO, CFO, controller, or chief accounting officer.

For **close relatives**, the rule is narrower. Independence is impaired if an individual on the attest engagement team has a close relative who holds a key position at the client, or if a team member (or certain other covered members, such as partners in the lead partner's office) knows that a close relative has a financial interest in the client that is material to the relative. Close relationships that the rule does not specifically address are evaluated under the conceptual framework.

The Code also limits working for an attest client while working for the firm. Under a revised interpretation effective September 15, 2026, covered members may not be simultaneously employed by or associated with an attest client, and partners and professional employees of the firm generally may not hold a key position at one; the exceptions are narrow, such as certain independent contractor arrangements. A team member who is considering an offer of employment from an attest client must report it to the firm and step off the engagement until the offer is rejected or employment is no longer being sought.

When a **former partner or professional employee** joins an attest client in a key position or as a board member, the firm's independence is impaired unless the individual has truly separated from the firm. The amounts owed to the individual (capital balances and retirement benefits) must be immaterial to the firm and fixed as to amount and payment dates, and the individual may not participate, or appear to participate, in the firm's business or be in a position to influence it. The firm also considers whether the familiarity threat requires safeguards, such as changing the engagement team.

**Example:** A senior manager who worked on the Honey Entertainment audit leaves the firm to become Honey Entertainment's controller. If her retirement balance with the firm will vary with the firm's future profits, the amount is not fixed, and independence is impaired. If the balance is fixed, immaterial, and paid on a set schedule, and she has no further involvement with the firm, independence can be preserved, although the firm should consider modifying the audit plan because she knows the firm's approach.

### Nonattest Services

A firm can provide nonattest services, such as tax preparation, bookkeeping, or consulting, to an attest client without impairing independence, but only if the client stays in charge. The general requirements are:

1. The client agrees to perform all **management responsibilities** related to the service.
2. The client designates an individual with **suitable skill, knowledge, or experience** (preferably within senior management) to oversee the service, evaluate its adequacy and results, and accept responsibility for it.
3. The member does **not assume management responsibilities**, such as making decisions, authorizing transactions, or having custody of client assets.

Before performing the service, the firm should document in writing its understanding with the client about the service and each party's responsibilities.

**Example:** Kodiak Partners engages its audit firm to help with bookkeeping. Independence is maintained if Kodiak Partners' controller, who has the needed accounting knowledge, reviews and approves every proposed journal entry and accepts responsibility for the records. If the firm starts approving transactions on its own, it has taken over a management responsibility and independence is impaired.

Some services are addressed by specific interpretations. The **tax services** interpretation was revised (and released in July 2026) to give a principles-based framework for evaluating the self-review and advocacy threats that tax services to attest clients can create. It takes effect January 15, 2027 and, under the AICPA's policy, should become eligible for testing April 1, 2027.

### Fees

Fees can create self-interest threats, and several rules address them.

- **Unpaid fees.** Independence is impaired if fees for professional services rendered more than one year before the date of the current-year report are still unpaid when that report is issued. Long-overdue fees start to look like a loan to the client.
- **Determining the attest fee.** Since January 1, 2025, the Code has addressed the threat that arises when the fee for an attest engagement is influenced by other services the firm provides to the client. The attest fee should be set on its own merits.
- **Fee dependency.** When fees from one attest client are significant to the firm, the firm evaluates the resulting self-interest and undue influence threats. If the dependency continues for five years, safeguards are required.

**Example:** Panda Records' auditor plans to issue its report on the 20X5 financial statements on March 15, 20X6. Panda Records still owes \$85,000 for tax work the firm performed in January 20X5, more than one year before the report date. Unless Panda Records pays before the report is issued, independence is impaired.

### Gifts and Entertainment

A covered member who accepts a **gift** from an attest client impairs independence unless the value is **trivial and inconsequential** to the recipient. **Entertainment**, such as a dinner or a sporting event attended with client personnel, does not impair independence as long as it is reasonable in the circumstances. The member considers its nature, cost, frequency, and the occasion, and whether client representatives attend.

## Integrity and Objectivity: Responding to Noncompliance With Laws and Regulations

The Integrity and Objectivity Rule also governs what a member does on discovering that a client may be breaking the law. Under the **noncompliance with laws and regulations (NOCLAR)** interpretations, which took effect June 30, 2023, a member in public practice who becomes aware of actual or suspected NOCLAR while providing professional services should:

1. obtain an understanding of the matter;
2. discuss it with the appropriate level of management and communicate it, as appropriate, to those charged with governance; and
3. decide whether further action is needed, such as disclosing the matter to an appropriate authority when law or regulation requires it, or withdrawing from the engagement.

Clearly inconsequential matters are excluded. The interpretations do not override the Confidential Client Information Rule, so disclosure outside the client still requires a legal basis or the client's consent.

The same rule covers **conflicts of interest**, for example advising two clients on opposite sides of a transaction. The member identifies and evaluates the conflict. If the threat is significant, the member discloses it to the affected clients and obtains their consent; if safeguards cannot reduce it to an acceptable level, the member declines or ends the service.

## Confidential Client Information, Including AI and Other Tools

A member in public practice may not disclose confidential client information without the client's specific consent. The rule does not prevent a member from:

- complying with professional standards (for example, the Accounting Principles Rule);
- complying with a validly issued and enforceable subpoena or summons, or with applicable laws and regulations;
- having the work reviewed in a peer review or similar quality review; or
- responding to an inquiry by, or filing a complaint with, the AICPA, a state society, or a board of accountancy.

The blueprint specifically tests confidentiality when a member uses **automated or third-party tools, including artificial intelligence**. The Code's existing rules answer the question. Before using a third-party service provider that will have access to confidential client information, the member should inform the client. Before disclosing confidential information to the provider, the member should either have a contract requiring the provider to keep the information confidential (and be reasonably assured the provider has procedures to do so) or obtain the client's specific consent. A cloud-based or generative AI tool whose provider can retain, view, or train on the data entered into it is a third party for this purpose.

**Example:** A staff accountant wants to paste Polar Inc.'s customer aging report into a free public AI chatbot to summarize it. The chatbot's terms allow the provider to keep and use what users enter. Doing so would disclose confidential client information to a third party without a confidentiality agreement or Polar Inc.'s consent, which violates the rule. The firm's approved AI tool, licensed under a contract that bars the provider from retaining or using client data, can be used instead. Either way, the member remains responsible for checking the tool's output, because due care and supervision apply to work produced with technology just as they do to work produced by people.

## Summary

| Area | Rule |
|---|---|
| Conceptual framework | Identify threats, evaluate significance, apply safeguards; if threats remain, decline or end the service. A specific rule overrides the framework. |
| Direct financial interest | Always impairs independence (unsolicited interests must be disposed of within 30 days) |
| Indirect financial interest | Impairs independence only if material to the covered member |
| Immediate family in key position | Impairs independence |
| Close relative of a team member | Key position, or a known material financial interest, impairs independence |
| Former employee joins client | Amounts owed must be immaterial and fixed; no continued participation in the firm |
| Nonattest services | Permitted if the client assumes management responsibilities and an individual with suitable skill, knowledge, or experience oversees the service |
| Unpaid fees | Fees for services more than one year before the report date must be paid before the report is issued |
| Gifts and entertainment | Gifts must be trivial and inconsequential; entertainment must be reasonable |
| Loans | Generally prohibited, except grandfathered and permitted loans from financial institution clients |
| NOCLAR | Understand, discuss, communicate to governance, then decide on further action |
| Confidentiality | No disclosure without consent; third-party tools, including AI, require a confidentiality agreement or client consent |

:::tip[Exam Tip]

For any independence question, work in this order: (1) identify the person and decide whether they are a covered member, immediate family, or close relative; (2) check whether a specific rule or interpretation addresses the relationship; (3) only if none does, apply the conceptual framework by naming the threat, judging its significance, and deciding whether safeguards can reduce it to an acceptable level.

:::
