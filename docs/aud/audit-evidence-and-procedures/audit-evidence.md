# Audit Evidence

Every audit opinion rests on evidence. Because the auditor never examines everything, the real question in each area is whether the evidence is enough, and good enough, to support a conclusion. The standards put it as **sufficient appropriate audit evidence**. This chapter starts with where evidence comes from, including information generated by technology such as artificial intelligence, then explains what makes evidence sufficient and appropriate, the procedures auditors use to obtain it, and the direction of testing that links each procedure to an assertion. The AICPA standard is AU-C 500, *Audit Evidence* (as revised by SAS 142); the PCAOB standard is AS 1105.

---

## Evidence and the Flow of the Audit

The auditor first performs **risk assessment procedures** to understand the entity and identify where material misstatements could occur. Those procedures produce evidence, but they are not enough on their own to support the opinion. The auditor then performs **further audit procedures** designed in response to the assessed risks:

- **Tests of controls**, which provide evidence about whether controls operated effectively (see [Tests of Controls](./tests-of-controls.md))
- **Substantive procedures**, which provide evidence about whether amounts and disclosures are misstated: tests of details and substantive analytical procedures (see [Substantive Procedures](./substantive-procedures.md))

Evidence accumulates throughout the engagement, from planning through the subsequent events review and the representation letter. The auditor also considers evidence from earlier audits and from the firm's acceptance and continuance procedures.

## Sources of Audit Evidence

The 2027 blueprint asks candidates to determine the sources of information to be used as audit evidence and names several examples. Each source raises its own reliability questions.

| Source | Example | What the Auditor Evaluates |
|---|---|---|
| **Information produced by the entity** | An aged receivables listing, a fixed asset register, a sales report from the ERP system | Whether the information is accurate and complete, and precise and detailed enough for the auditor's purpose |
| **Management's specialist** | An actuary's pension valuation, an appraiser's report on real estate | The specialist's competence, capabilities, and objectivity; what the specialist did; whether the work is appropriate evidence for the assertion |
| **External sources** | Bank confirmations, published market prices, credit agency reports | The source's reputation and independence from the entity, and whether the information reached the auditor without alteration |
| **Developed by the audit team** from internal or external data | The auditor's own recalculation of depreciation, an independent expectation of interest expense, a data analysis of all sales transactions | The reliability of the underlying data and the soundness of the auditor's method |
| **Generated using technologies, including artificial intelligence** | A reconciliation produced by the entity's automation tool, a contract summary produced by an AI tool | How the output was produced, the data that went in, the controls over the tool, and whether the output agrees to its sources |

Information produced by the entity deserves special attention because so much evidence starts there. Before using an entity-prepared report, the auditor obtains evidence about its **accuracy and completeness** (for example, by testing the report's logic or agreeing its totals to the general ledger) and evaluates whether it is **precise and detailed enough** for the procedure. The [Reliability of Data and Information](./reliability-of-data-and-information.md) chapter covers these procedures in depth.

### Information Generated Using Technology and AI

Information is not reliable just because software produced it. Whether a schedule came from the entity's AI tool or from the audit team's own tool, the auditor asks the same questions:

- **Inputs:** What data did the tool use, and is that data complete and accurate?
- **Processing:** What does the tool do with the data? Can its results be explained, recalculated, or agreed to source documents?
- **Controls:** Who can change the tool or its settings, and are changes tested and approved?
- **Output risks:** Could the tool produce plausible but wrong results (for example, an AI summary that invents a contract term) or results biased toward a desired answer?

When the audit team uses its own technology, the auditor remains fully responsible for the evidence, so the team must understand the tool, supervise its use, and review its output like any other work. The phrase "generated using various technologies including artificial intelligence" is new to this blueprint task in 2027, so a 2026 candidate would not see it listed there.

## Sufficiency and Appropriateness

### Sufficiency: How Much Evidence

**Sufficiency** is the quantity of evidence. More evidence is needed when the **assessed risk of material misstatement is higher** and when the **quality of the evidence is lower**. Quantity cannot rescue poor quality, though: a stack of unreliable or irrelevant documents is still unreliable or irrelevant.

### Appropriateness: Relevance and Reliability

**Appropriateness** is the quality of evidence, and it has two parts: **relevance** and **reliability**.

Evidence is **relevant** when it bears on the assertion being tested. A procedure can give excellent evidence for one assertion and none for another. Confirming receivable balances with Kodiak Partners' customers is strong evidence that the recorded receivables **exist**, but it says nothing about **completeness**: a receivable that was never recorded will never appear on a confirmation request.

Evidence is **reliable** when it can be trusted. SAS 142 directs the auditor to consider attributes such as the information's **accuracy**, **completeness**, **authenticity**, and **susceptibility to management bias**, along with the credibility of its source. The standards also offer general guidance, each item subject to exceptions:

| Factor | More Reliable | Less Reliable |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | From an independent source outside the entity | Generated inside the entity |
| **Internal controls** | Generated internally when related controls are effective | Generated internally when controls are weak |
| **How obtained** | Obtained directly by the auditor | Obtained indirectly or by inference |
| **Form** | Documentary (paper or electronic) | Oral |
| **Original vs. copy** | Original documents | Copies, scans, or other transformed versions |

**Example:** A bank confirmation that Polar Inc.'s bank sends directly to the auditor is more reliable than a bank statement handed over by Polar's accounting staff. The confirmation comes from an independent source and reaches the auditor directly.

The auditor is not expected to be an expert in authenticating documents and is not required to authenticate them. But if conditions suggest a document may not be authentic, or that its terms were modified without disclosure, the auditor must investigate further.

### Evaluating Evidence With Skepticism

The auditor evaluates evidence **whether it corroborates or contradicts** management's assertions. It is easy to stop looking once evidence supports the expected answer; the standards require the auditor to consider contradictory evidence too. When evidence from one source is inconsistent with evidence from another, or the auditor doubts the reliability of information, the auditor determines what additional or modified procedures are needed to resolve the matter and considers the effect on the rest of the audit.

At the end of each area, the auditor concludes whether **sufficient appropriate evidence** has been obtained to meet the objectives of the planned procedures. If it has not, the auditor obtains more. If more cannot be obtained, the result is a scope limitation, which leads to a qualified opinion or a disclaimer.

## Audit Procedures for Obtaining Evidence

The standards describe seven types of audit procedures. Each can be used as a risk assessment procedure, a test of controls, or a substantive procedure, depending on its purpose.

| Procedure | What the Auditor Does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Inspection** | Examines records or documents, or physically examines tangible assets | Reading Bear Co.'s lease agreements; examining equipment on the factory floor |
| **Observation** | Watches a process or procedure being performed by others | Watching Panda Industries' staff count inventory |
| **External confirmation** | Obtains a direct response from a third party | Sending confirmation requests to Grizzly Inc.'s major customers |
| **Recalculation** | Checks the mathematical accuracy of documents or records, manually or with automated tools | Recomputing Bear Co.'s depreciation expense for every asset with a data analytics tool |
| **Reperformance** | Independently executes a procedure or control the entity performed | Redoing Sloth Security's month-end bank reconciliation |
| **Analytical procedures** | Evaluates financial information by studying plausible relationships among data | Comparing interest expense with average debt times the average rate |
| **Inquiry** | Seeks information from knowledgeable people inside or outside the entity | Asking Sloth Security's IT manager how access to the billing system is granted |

### Inquiry and Interviews

Inquiry is used throughout the audit, but inquiry alone rarely provides sufficient appropriate evidence; the auditor corroborates the answers with other procedures. Good inquiry is a skill. The auditor asks open questions, listens for answers that are vague, inconsistent, or rehearsed, and asks follow-up questions to understand the person's perspective and motives. Some of the most useful answers come from people **outside the accounting function**, such as sales staff, warehouse supervisors, or in-house lawyers, because they see the transactions before they become accounting entries.

**Example:** In a casual conversation, a Grizzly Inc. sales manager mentions that two large customers "can send back anything they don't sell." The controller has said all sales are final. The auditor follows up by asking which customers have such terms, how often goods come back, and who approved the arrangement, then inspects the customer contracts and post-year-end returns. The answers bear directly on whether revenue was recognized too early.

## Direction of Testing: Vouching and Tracing

The direction in which the auditor tests determines which assertion the test addresses, and the exam tests this often.

| Direction | Starting Point | Ending Point | Question Answered | Assertion Tested |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vouching** | Recorded entries (ledgers, journals) | Supporting documents | Is this recorded item real? | Existence or occurrence |
| **Tracing** | Source documents | Recorded entries | Was this real event recorded? | Completeness |

**Vouching example:** The auditor selects 25 sales from Polar Inc.'s sales journal and examines the related shipping documents and customer orders. This tests whether recorded sales actually **occurred**.

**Tracing example:** The auditor selects 25 shipping documents from Grizzly Inc.'s shipping log and traces each one forward to the sales journal and general ledger. This tests whether all shipments were recorded, the **completeness** assertion.

:::danger[Common Exam Trap]

The sample must be drawn from the right population. To test **existence or occurrence**, select from the **recorded** items and vouch them to support. To test **completeness**, select from an independent source of **what actually happened** (shipping documents, receiving reports) and trace forward to the records. Selecting from the ledger can never reveal an item that is missing from the ledger.

:::
