# Attestation Engagements

CPAs are asked to report on far more than historical financial statements: a company's compliance with a loan agreement, the controls at a service organization, a financial forecast, or the effects of an acquisition shown in pro forma statements. The AICPA's **Statements on Standards for Attestation Engagements (SSAEs)**, codified in the **AT-C sections**, give all of these engagements a common framework. This chapter first sets out the shared vocabulary, then covers the three engagement types (examination, review, and agreed-upon procedures), and finally the special subject matters the exam likes to test: prospective financial information, pro forma information, and compliance.

---

## The Building Blocks: Subject Matter, Criteria, and Assertions

Every attestation engagement measures a **subject matter** against **criteria**. The subject matter is the thing being reported on, such as an entity's cybersecurity program, its compliance with a contract, or a forecast. The criteria are the benchmarks used to measure or evaluate it, such as a published control framework or the terms of the contract.

The practitioner may accept an engagement only if the criteria are **suitable**, which means they are:

- **Relevant** to the subject matter
- **Objective** (free from bias)
- **Measurable**, so that measurements are reasonably consistent
- **Complete**, so that no relevant factors are omitted

The criteria must also be available to the intended users, either publicly or in the practitioner's report.

The **responsible party** is the party responsible for the subject matter, usually management. In many engagements the responsible party gives the practitioner a written **assertion** about the subject matter (for example, "Bear Co. complied with the covenants in Section 6 of its loan agreement"). The practitioner must be **independent** in every examination, review, and agreed-upon procedures engagement.

**Example:** Bear Co. engages a CPA to examine its cybersecurity risk management program. The subject matter is management's description of the program and the effectiveness of its controls. The criteria are the description criteria and trust services criteria the AICPA publishes for cybersecurity examinations. Because those criteria are suitable and publicly available, the engagement can proceed.

## Three Types of Attestation Engagements

| Type | Level of Assurance | Nature of Procedures | Report Expression |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Examination** | Reasonable (high) | Extensive procedures, similar to an audit | Opinion: "In our opinion, ... is presented, in all material respects, in accordance with ..." |
| **Review** | Limited | Primarily inquiry and analytical procedures | Conclusion: "We are not aware of any material modifications that should be made ..." |
| **Agreed-upon procedures (AUP)** | None | Specific procedures the engaging party agrees to | Procedures and findings only, with no opinion or conclusion |

The assurance ladder mirrors the one for financial statements: an examination is like an audit, a review is like a review of financial statements, and an AUP engagement gives only findings.

---

## Examination Engagements

An **examination** gives the highest level of assurance available under the SSAEs: **reasonable assurance**. The practitioner plans and performs procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence and then expresses an **opinion**. There are two kinds of examination:

- In an **assertion-based examination** (AT-C 205), the responsible party measures the subject matter against the criteria and provides a written assertion. The practitioner requests that assertion and may report on either the subject matter or the assertion.
- In a **direct examination** (AT-C 206), the practitioner does the measuring or evaluating. No assertion from the responsible party is needed, and the opinion conveys the results of the practitioner's own measurement or evaluation.

The examination report includes a title with the word **"independent,"** identifies the subject matter, the criteria, and the responsible party, describes the responsibilities of the responsible party and the practitioner, states that the examination was conducted under AICPA attestation standards and that the practitioner is independent, and gives the opinion, signature, city and state, and date.

When the subject matter is **materially misstated**, the practitioner expresses a **qualified** or **adverse** opinion, and the opinion is stated directly on the subject matter rather than on the assertion. A scope limitation can lead to a qualified opinion or a disclaimer.

**Example:** Kodiak Partners, a service organization, engages a CPA firm to examine its description of its system and the design and operating effectiveness of its controls, measured against the AICPA's trust services criteria. This is a SOC 2 engagement, an assertion-based examination, and the firm issues an opinion.

---

## Review Engagements

A **review** under AT-C 210 provides **limited assurance**. The practitioner performs mainly inquiry and analytical procedures and reports a conclusion in negative-assurance form. Reviews are useful when users want some comfort at a lower cost than an examination.

:::caution

The SSAEs do **not** permit a review of **prospective financial information**, **internal control**, or **compliance** with specified requirements. For those subject matters, the choices are an examination or an agreed-upon procedures engagement (and, for prospective statements, a compilation). The exam tests this restriction often.

:::

---

## Agreed-Upon Procedures Engagements

In an **agreed-upon procedures (AUP) engagement** under AT-C 215, the practitioner performs specific procedures and reports the **findings**. The **engaging party** agrees to the procedures and acknowledges that they are appropriate for its purpose. The practitioner gives no opinion or conclusion; users draw their own conclusions from the findings. Because the findings must be factual, procedures have to be specific (for example, "compare the invoice total to the amount recorded in the receivables ledger"), not vague ("review the receivables for reasonableness").

The AUP report includes:

- A title with the word "independent"
- Identification of the engaging party and the subject matter
- A statement that the engaging party has agreed to and acknowledged that the procedures are appropriate
- Each procedure performed and the related findings
- A statement that the practitioner was not engaged to perform, and did not perform, an examination or review, and that additional procedures might have brought other matters to light
- A statement that the practitioner is independent
- An alert restricting the report's use, when the practitioner decides one is needed (an AUP report is not automatically restricted)
- The signature, city and state, and date

**Example:** Panda Industries is applying for a loan. The bank wants to know whether Panda's receivables aging schedule foots and whether selected customer balances agree to invoices. Panda engages a CPA to perform exactly those procedures. The CPA reports the procedures and the findings (for example, "The schedule footed without exception; two of 25 balances differed from the invoices by a total of \$3,400"). The CPA does not say whether the receivables are fairly stated.

## Prospective Financial Statements

**Prospective financial statements** look forward. They come in two forms that differ in their assumptions and in who may use them:

| Type | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Forecast** | Expected financial position, results, and cash flows, based on the conditions the responsible party expects to exist and the course of action it expects to take | **General use**: may be distributed to any third party |
| **Projection** | Expected results given one or more **hypothetical assumptions** ("what if we build the new plant?") | **Limited use**: the responsible party and third parties negotiating directly with it |

A practitioner may **examine**, **compile**, or perform **agreed-upon procedures** on prospective financial statements, but may **not review** them. In an examination, the practitioner reports on whether the statements are presented in conformity with AICPA presentation guidelines and whether the assumptions provide a reasonable basis for the forecast (or, for a projection, given the hypothetical assumptions).

**Example:** Grizzly Inc. prepares a three-year forecast assuming 8% annual revenue growth under current market conditions and asks its CPA to examine it. The CPA evaluates whether the assumptions are reasonable and the presentation follows AICPA guidelines, then issues an examination opinion. The report also warns that actual results will usually differ from the forecast.

---

## Pro Forma Financial Information

**Pro forma financial information** shows the effects of a transaction, such as an acquisition or divestiture, on historical financial statements as if the transaction had occurred at an earlier date. Under AT-C 310, a practitioner may **examine** or **review** pro forma information. The historical statements it is built on must have been **audited or reviewed**, and the assurance on the pro forma information cannot exceed the assurance on the historical statements: an examination of pro forma information requires audited historical statements.

**Example:** Cub Entertainment acquires a smaller media company and prepares pro forma statements showing combined operations as if the acquisition had occurred at the beginning of the year. Because Cub's historical statements were reviewed, not audited, the CPA can review the pro forma information but cannot examine it.

---

## Compliance Attestation

Under **AT-C 315**, a practitioner may **examine** an entity's compliance with specified requirements (laws, regulations, contracts, or grants) or perform **agreed-upon procedures** on compliance or on internal control over compliance. A review is not available. The [Reporting on Compliance](./reporting-on-compliance.md) chapter covers these engagements and their reports in detail.

---

## Using the Work of Other Practitioners

Some attestation engagements involve more than one firm. **SSAE 23** aligned the attestation standards with the quality management standards and replaced the old term "other practitioner" with two terms. A **participating practitioner** performs work on the engagement as a member of the engagement team, under the engagement partner's direction and supervision. A **referred-to practitioner** reports on part of the subject matter and is not on the engagement team; the practitioner's report may refer to that work. SSAE 23 applies to engagements beginning on or after December 15, 2025.

## Summary

| Engagement | Assurance | Report Expression | Key Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Examination** | Reasonable | Opinion | Assertion-based (AT-C 205) or direct (AT-C 206) |
| **Review** | Limited | Conclusion (negative assurance) | Not allowed for prospective information, internal control, or compliance |
| **Agreed-upon procedures** | None | Procedures and findings | Engaging party acknowledges the procedures are appropriate; use may be restricted |
| **Forecast** | Examination, compilation, or AUP | Varies | General use; no review |
| **Projection** | Examination, compilation, or AUP | Varies | Limited use; no review |
| **Pro forma** | Examination or review | Varies | Historical statements must be audited (for an examination) or reviewed |
| **Compliance** | Examination or AUP | Varies | No review |
