# Tax-Exempt Organizations

Charities, schools, churches, and other organizations described in **IRC §501(c)(3)** pay no federal income tax on income from their exempt activities, and donors can deduct gifts to them. In return, the Code demands that they be organized and run for exempt purposes and not for the benefit of insiders. The TCP blueprint asks you to recall what it takes to qualify, what can cost an organization its exemption, and which kinds of income are taxed anyway as **unrelated business income**. This chapter follows that sequence and adds the excise taxes that can apply to an exempt organization or its insiders without costing it its exemption.

---

## Qualifying Under §501(c)(3)

### Organizational and Operational Tests

An organization must pass two tests. The first looks at its governing documents; the second looks at what it actually does.

| Test | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Organizational test** | The articles of incorporation, trust instrument, or articles of association must **limit the organization's purposes** to exempt purposes, must not empower it to engage (other than insubstantially) in activities that do not further those purposes, and must dedicate its assets to exempt purposes if it dissolves |
| **Operational test** | The organization must be **operated exclusively** for exempt purposes, which in practice means its primary activities further those purposes and no more than an insubstantial part of its activities does not |

### Exempt Purposes

Section 501(c)(3) lists the qualifying purposes: **religious, charitable, scientific, testing for public safety, literary, educational, fostering national or international amateur sports competition, and preventing cruelty to children or animals.** "Charitable" is broad. It includes relieving the poor, advancing education or religion, and lessening the burdens of government.

### Applying for Recognition

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Form 1023** | The standard application for recognition of §501(c)(3) status |
| **Form 1023-EZ** | A shorter online application for small organizations that meet the IRS eligibility worksheet |
| **Timing** | If filed within **27 months** after the organization is formed, recognition is retroactive to the date of formation |
| **Who need not apply** | Churches, and organizations whose gross receipts are normally \$5,000 or less |
| **Result** | An IRS determination letter recognizing the exemption |

Every §501(c)(3) organization is presumed to be a **private foundation** unless it shows that it is a public charity, typically by showing broad public support. The distinction matters because private foundations are subject to additional excise taxes and stricter rules.

## Keeping Exempt Status

### What Can Cost an Organization Its Exemption

| Problem | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Private inurement** | No part of the net earnings may benefit an insider, such as a founder, officer, or director, through excessive pay, bargain sales or rentals, or personal use of the organization's assets |
| **Private benefit** | The organization must serve public rather than private interests, even private interests of outsiders |
| **Substantial lobbying** | No substantial part of the organization's activities may be attempts to influence legislation; a public charity can instead elect the expenditure limits of §501(h) |
| **Political campaign activity** | Participating or intervening in any campaign for or against a candidate for public office is **absolutely prohibited** |
| **Substantial nonexempt activity** | More than an insubstantial amount of activity outside the exempt purposes, including unrelated business that becomes a primary activity, fails the operational test |
| **Failure to file** | Not filing the required annual return for **3 consecutive years** results in **automatic revocation** |

:::warning

The political campaign prohibition is the one rule with no substantiality threshold. Endorsing a candidate, contributing to a campaign, or publishing statements for or against a candidate can cost a §501(c)(3) organization its exemption even if it happens only once. Lobbying on legislation, by contrast, is allowed as long as it is not substantial.

:::

### Annual Filing Requirements

| Form | Who files |
|---|---|
| **Form 990** | Organizations with gross receipts of \$200,000 or more, or total assets of \$500,000 or more |
| **Form 990-EZ** | Organizations with gross receipts under \$200,000 and total assets under \$500,000 |
| **Form 990-N** (e-Postcard) | Organizations whose gross receipts are normally \$50,000 or less |
| **Form 990-PF** | Private foundations, regardless of size |
| **Form 990-T** | Any exempt organization with \$1,000 or more of gross unrelated business income |

Churches are not required to file the annual information return. An organization whose exemption is automatically revoked must apply again to be recognized.

## Excise Taxes That Apply Without Revoking Exemption

Revoking an exemption punishes the whole organization and the people it serves. For several abuses, the Code instead imposes excise taxes, either on the insiders who benefit or on the organization itself.

### Excess Benefit Transactions (§4958)

**Intermediate sanctions** apply when a public charity (or a §501(c)(4) social welfare organization) gives an economic benefit to a **disqualified person**, such as an officer, director, or other insider with substantial influence, that exceeds the value the organization receives in return.

| Tax | Rate |
|---|---|
| **Initial tax on the disqualified person** | **25%** of the excess benefit |
| **Additional tax if not corrected** within the taxable period | **200%** of the excess benefit |
| **Tax on an organization manager** who knowingly approved it | **10%** of the excess benefit, up to \$20,000 per transaction |

> **Example:** The executive director of an educational §501(c)(3) organization is paid \$500,000 when comparable positions pay \$300,000. The \$200,000 excess benefit triggers a 25% tax of **\$50,000** on the executive director. If the director does not correct it by repaying the excess, an additional 200% tax of **\$400,000** applies.

### Other Excise Taxes

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) changed two excise taxes for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. Both changes have been eligible for TCP testing since July 1, 2026.

| Tax | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Excess compensation (§4960)** | The organization pays a **21%** excise tax on pay over **\$1,000,000** to a **covered employee**, and on excess parachute payments. OBBBA expanded "covered employee" from the five highest-paid employees to **any** current employee, and any former employee who was an employee in a tax year beginning after 2016. |
| **Net investment income of private colleges (§4968)** | Applies only to private colleges and universities with at least **3,000** tuition-paying students, more than half of them in the United States. The rate depends on the endowment per student: **1.4%** for \$500,000 to \$750,000, **4%** for \$750,000 to \$2,000,000, and **8%** above \$2,000,000. |

> **Example:** Cub Arts Foundation pays its chief executive \$1,400,000. The foundation owes a §4960 excise tax of 21% × \$400,000 = **\$84,000**. Because "covered employee" now means any employee, the same tax would apply if the \$1,400,000 went to someone outside the five highest-paid group.

Private foundations face their own set of excise taxes, including a tax on net investment income and taxes on self-dealing between the foundation and its insiders.

## Unrelated Business Income

### What Makes Income Unrelated

An exempt organization pays tax on its **unrelated business taxable income (UBTI)** at the regular corporate rate of 21% (or trust rates, if it is organized as a trust). The purpose is fairness: an exempt organization should not have a tax advantage over a taxable business when it competes in a commercial activity. Income is unrelated business income only if all three elements are present:

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Trade or business** | An activity carried on to produce income from selling goods or performing services |
| **Regularly carried on** | With frequency and continuity comparable to similar commercial activities (an annual weekend fundraiser usually is not) |
| **Not substantially related** | The activity does not contribute importantly to the exempt purpose; using the profits for exempt purposes is not enough |

> **Example:** The Bear Museum, an educational §501(c)(3) organization, runs a gift shop. Sales of art books and reproductions of works in its collection further its educational purpose and are not unrelated business income. Regular sales of souvenir apparel and novelty items with no educational connection are unrelated business income.

### Types of Unrelated Business Income

The blueprint asks you to recall the kinds of income that are taxed. The most common are:

- **Income from a regularly carried on business** unrelated to the exempt purpose, such as selling advertising in the organization's magazine or running a parking garage open to the public.
- **Debt-financed income**: income from property bought with borrowed money, in proportion to the debt.
- **Rents from personal property**, and rents based on the tenant's net profits.
- **Interest, rents, royalties, and annuities from a controlled entity** (one the organization owns more than 50%), to the extent they reduce the controlled entity's taxable income.
- **All income from S corporation stock** the organization owns, including gain on its sale.
- An exempt partner's **share of a partnership's unrelated business income**.

### Income Excluded From UBTI

Even when the three-part test is met, most passive investment income is excluded, because it does not involve competing with taxable businesses:

| Excluded income | Note |
|---|---|
| **Dividends, interest, and annuities** | Unless debt-financed or from a controlled entity |
| **Royalties** | Unless from a controlled entity |
| **Rents from real property** | Rent from personal property leased with real property is also excluded if it is incidental (10% or less of the total rent) |
| **Gains from the sale of property** | Other than inventory or property held for sale to customers |
| **Research income** | Research for the government, and research by a college, university, or hospital |

Certain activities are also excluded entirely: a business in which **substantially all the work is done by volunteers**; a business run primarily for the **convenience** of members, students, patients, or employees (such as a university cafeteria); the sale of **donated merchandise** (such as a thrift shop); **qualified sponsorship payments**, which acknowledge a sponsor without advertising its products; and **bingo** games that are legal where they are played.

### Computing UBTI

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross unrelated business income | Revenue from each unrelated business |
| Less: directly connected deductions | Expenses directly attributable to that business |
| Less: specific deduction | **\$1,000** |
| = **UBTI** | Taxed at 21% for a corporation |

> **Example:** Grizzly Foundation, a §501(c)(3) organization, runs a public parking garage that is not related to its exempt purpose. The garage has \$150,000 of revenue and \$90,000 of directly connected expenses. UBTI is \$150,000 - \$90,000 - \$1,000 = **\$59,000**, and the tax is 21% × \$59,000 = **\$12,390**.

### Debt-Financed Property

When an exempt organization buys income-producing property with borrowed money, part of the income (and part of any gain on sale) becomes UBTI, even if it would otherwise be excluded as rent or a capital gain. The taxable share is the **debt-financed percentage**: average acquisition indebtedness for the year divided by the property's average adjusted basis. The same percentage of the property's deductions is allowed.

> **Example:** Panda Charitable Foundation buys an office building for \$1,000,000, financing \$600,000 with a mortgage, and rents it out for \$100,000 a year. For the first year, the debt-financed percentage is about \$600,000 / \$1,000,000 = 60%, so about 60% × \$100,000 = **\$60,000** of the rent is gross UBTI, even though real property rent is normally excluded. Sixty percent of the building's depreciation and interest expense is deductible against it.

### Each Business Computed Separately

Since 2018, an organization with more than one unrelated business must compute UBTI **separately for each one** (§512(a)(6)). A loss from one business cannot offset income from another, and a net operating loss from a business can be carried forward only against that same business.

## Summary

| Topic | Key rule |
|---|---|
| Qualifying | Organizational test (purposes limited, assets dedicated) and operational test (operated exclusively for exempt purposes) |
| Exempt purposes | Religious, charitable, scientific, public safety testing, literary, educational, amateur sports, preventing cruelty |
| Applying | Form 1023 or 1023-EZ; within 27 months for retroactive recognition; churches need not apply |
| Losing exemption | Private inurement, private benefit, substantial lobbying, any campaign intervention, substantial nonexempt activity, 3 years without filing |
| §4958 | 25% tax on the insider's excess benefit, 200% if uncorrected, 10% on approving managers |
| §4960 and §4968 | 21% tax on pay over \$1,000,000 to any employee; tiered 1.4%, 4%, or 8% tax on large private college endowments |
| UBTI test | Trade or business, regularly carried on, not substantially related |
| Taxable types | Unrelated business, debt-financed income, controlled-entity payments, S corporation stock, partnership share |
| Exclusions | Dividends, interest, royalties, real property rent, gains, research; volunteer, convenience, donated goods, sponsorships, bingo |
| Computation | Gross income - directly connected deductions - \$1,000 = UBTI, taxed at 21%; each business computed separately |
