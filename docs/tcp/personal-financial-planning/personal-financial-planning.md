# Personal Financial Planning

## Introduction

Personal financial planning is where TCP looks past the current year's return. A CPA reviewing a client's Form 1040 sees the retirement contributions, the investment income, the tuition payments, and the insurance premiums, and is often the first adviser to notice that a different choice would leave the client better off. The blueprint tests that role. You need to know the advantages and disadvantages of retirement plans, investments, education funding tools, and insurance, and to understand how ownership and beneficiary designations decide who receives an asset at death. You also need to prepare comparison schedules and compute after-tax returns. This chapter takes those topics in that order.

---

## Retirement Plans

Every retirement account answers the same three questions: are contributions made with pretax or after-tax dollars, how are earnings taxed while they stay in the account, and how are withdrawals taxed. Most of the planning follows from the answers.

### Traditional IRA

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Contribution limit** | Indexed annually; one combined limit covers traditional and Roth IRAs, with a catch-up at age 50 |
| **Deductibility** | Deductible unless the taxpayer (or spouse) is covered by an employer plan and MAGI exceeds an indexed phase-out range; nondeductible contributions create basis |
| **Earnings** | Tax-deferred |
| **Distributions** | Ordinary income, except the part that returns nondeductible basis |
| **Required minimum distributions (RMDs)** | Must begin by April 1 of the year after the owner reaches the applicable age (73, rising to 75 for people born in 1960 or later) |
| **Early withdrawal** | 10% additional tax before age 59½, with exceptions such as disability, qualified higher education expenses, and up to \$10,000 for a first home |

### Roth IRA

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Contribution limit** | Same combined limit, phased out at higher MAGI (indexed) |
| **Deductibility** | Not deductible; contributions are made with after-tax dollars |
| **Qualified distributions** | Tax-free if the owner's first Roth IRA was opened at least 5 years earlier and the owner is 59½ or older, disabled, or deceased, or uses up to \$10,000 for a first home |
| **Ordering of withdrawals** | Contributions come out first (always tax-free and penalty-free), then conversions, then earnings |
| **RMDs** | None during the owner's lifetime |

### 401(k) Plans

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Contribution limit** | Elective deferral limit is higher than the IRA limit and indexed, with a catch-up at age 50 |
| **Employer contributions** | Matching and profit-sharing contributions, often subject to a vesting schedule |
| **Traditional 401(k)** | Pretax deferrals; distributions are ordinary income |
| **Roth 401(k)** | After-tax deferrals; qualified distributions are tax-free; since 2024, no RMDs during the owner's lifetime, the same as a Roth IRA |
| **RMDs (traditional)** | Begin at the applicable age; an employee who is not a 5% owner can generally wait until retiring from that employer |
| **Loans** | A plan may allow loans up to the lesser of \$50,000 or half the vested balance; IRAs cannot make loans |

### Annuities

An **annuity** is a contract, usually with an insurance company, that exchanges a lump sum or a series of premiums for a stream of payments. A nonqualified annuity, bought with after-tax money outside a retirement plan, has no contribution limit and grows tax-deferred.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Withdrawals before annuitization** | Earnings come out first and are taxed as ordinary income |
| **Annuity payments** | Each payment is partly a tax-free return of the investment under the **exclusion ratio** (investment in the contract ÷ expected return) |
| **Early withdrawal** | 10% additional tax on the taxable part before age 59½ |
| **Death benefit** | Most contracts guarantee a minimum death benefit |
| **Costs** | Mortality and expense charges and surrender charges usually exceed mutual fund fees |

> **Example:** Lee, a retiree, paid \$100,000 for an annuity that pays \$800 a month for life. Lee's expected return under the IRS tables is \$160,000. The exclusion ratio is \$100,000 ÷ \$160,000 = 62.5%, so \$500 of each \$800 payment is tax-free and \$300 is ordinary income. Once Lee has recovered the full \$100,000, every later payment is fully taxable.

Buying an annuity inside an IRA or 401(k) rarely makes sense. The account already defers tax, so the annuity adds cost without adding a tax benefit.

### Employer Plans: Defined Contribution versus Defined Benefit

| Feature | Defined Contribution (e.g., 401(k)) | Defined Benefit (Pension) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who bears investment risk** | **Employee** | **Employer** |
| **Benefit at retirement** | Depends on contributions and investment performance | Set by formula (years of service × compensation) |
| **Portability** | Generally portable (rollover to an IRA) | Generally **not** portable |
| **Employer cost** | Predictable (the match or contribution percentage) | Variable and potentially large |
| **Vesting** | Employer contributions may vest over time | Benefits vest under the plan's terms |

### Preparing a Retirement Plan Comparison Schedule

The blueprint asks you to prepare a schedule that helps a client choose among plans. The core of most schedules is an after-tax comparison of pretax and Roth contributions. The rule is simple: if the tax rate at withdrawal will be lower than the rate today, the pretax account wins; if it will be higher, the Roth wins; if the rates are the same, the two come out equal.

> **Example:** Drew, a Sloth Security employee, can put \$10,000 of salary into either a traditional 401(k) or a Roth 401(k). Drew's marginal rate is 32% today and is expected to be 22% in retirement. Assume the investments double before withdrawal.
>
> | Item | Traditional 401(k) | Roth 401(k) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Salary used | \$10,000 | \$10,000 |
> | Tax paid now | \$0 | \$3,200 |
> | Amount invested | \$10,000 | \$6,800 |
> | Value at withdrawal (doubled) | \$20,000 | \$13,600 |
> | Tax at withdrawal | \$4,400 (22%) | \$0 |
> | **After-tax amount** | **\$15,600** | **\$13,600** |
>
> The traditional 401(k) wins because Drew deducts at 32% and pays tax at 22%. If Drew's rate in retirement were also 32%, both columns would end at \$13,600.

A complete schedule adds the other rows a client cares about: each plan's contribution limit, the employer match and vesting schedule, RMD rules, access to money before retirement (loans and early withdrawal exceptions), and investment choices and fees. Non-tax factors often tip the decision. A Roth account has no lifetime RMDs, gives tax diversification when future rates are uncertain, and shelters more money when the client already contributes the maximum, because the limit applies to after-tax dollars.

## Investment Options and Risk

The blueprint asks you to understand the risks of common investments and to compute their return net of tax.

### Types of Investment Risk

| Risk | Description |
|---|---|
| **Market risk** | The value of an investment falls with the overall market |
| **Interest rate risk** | Rising rates lower the value of fixed-income investments (bond prices move opposite to rates) |
| **Credit (default) risk** | The issuer fails to pay interest or principal |
| **Inflation (purchasing power) risk** | Returns do not keep pace with inflation |
| **Liquidity risk** | The investment cannot be sold quickly at a fair price |
| **Reinvestment risk** | Cash flows must be reinvested at lower rates |

### Comparing Investments

| Investment | Main Risks | Tax Treatment of Returns |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity securities** | Higher market risk, with higher expected long-term returns | Qualified dividends and long-term gains taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%; other dividends at ordinary rates; appreciation not taxed until sale |
| **Corporate bonds** | Interest rate and credit risk | Interest taxed as **ordinary income** |
| **Municipal bonds** | Interest rate risk; generally low credit risk for general obligation bonds | Interest generally **exempt from federal income tax** (and often from state tax in the issuing state); interest on private activity bonds is an AMT preference |
| **U.S. Treasury securities** | Minimal credit risk; interest rate risk rises with maturity | Interest taxed federally but exempt from state and local tax |

For equities, the timing of the tax matters as much as the rate. Appreciation is not taxed until the stock is sold, and appreciation still held at death escapes income tax entirely because the heirs' basis steps up to fair market value.

### Return on Investment Net of Tax

The after-tax return on a taxable investment is:

$$\text{After-Tax Return} = \text{Pre-Tax Return} \times (1 - \text{Marginal Tax Rate})$$

For a tax-exempt investment, the after-tax return equals the pretax return. To compare the two directly, convert the tax-exempt yield into a **tax-equivalent yield**:

$$\text{Tax-Equivalent Yield} = \frac{\text{Tax-Exempt Yield}}{1 - \text{Marginal Tax Rate}}$$

> **Example:** Jordan, the CFO of Ursa Security, is comparing a corporate bond yielding 5% with a municipal bond yielding 3.5%. Jordan's marginal rate is 37%.
>
> - Corporate bond after-tax return: 5% × (1 - 0.37) = **3.15%**
> - Municipal bond after-tax return: **3.50%** (tax-exempt)
> - Tax-equivalent yield of the municipal bond: 3.5% ÷ (1 - 0.37) = **5.56%**
>
> The municipal bond gives the higher after-tax return despite its lower stated yield.

High-income investors may also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax on taxable interest, dividends, and gains (MAGI above \$200,000, or \$250,000 for joint filers). It does not apply to tax-exempt interest, so it widens the municipal bond's advantage. If Jordan owes it, the corporate bond's after-tax return falls to 5% × (1 - 0.408) = 2.96%.

## Education Funding

The blueprint covers four ways to pay for postsecondary education: qualified tuition programs, student loans, grants, and scholarships. OBBBA also created a new account for children that families will compare with a 529 plan.

### Qualified Tuition Programs (529 Plans)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Contributions** | Not deductible for federal tax (many states allow a deduction). Each contribution is a completed gift that qualifies for the annual exclusion, and a donor can elect to spread a large gift over 5 years (5 × \$19,000 = \$95,000 for 2026). |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred, and tax-free if used for qualified expenses |
| **Qualified higher education expenses** | Tuition, fees, books, supplies, equipment, computers, and room and board for students enrolled at least half-time. Since July 2025, recognized postsecondary credential programs also qualify. |
| **K-12 expenses** | Up to \$20,000 a year starting in 2026 (was \$10,000). Since July 2025, these cover more than tuition: books, curriculum, online materials, tutoring, test fees, dual enrollment, and educational therapies. |
| **Nonqualified distributions** | The earnings portion is ordinary income plus a 10% additional tax. The additional tax is waived to the extent of tax-free scholarships, and on the beneficiary's death or disability. |
| **Flexibility** | Change the beneficiary to another family member; roll over to an ABLE account for a beneficiary with a disability (made permanent by OBBBA); or roll limited amounts into the beneficiary's Roth IRA once the account is 15 years old (up to \$35,000 over the beneficiary's lifetime, within the annual Roth limit) |

### Trump Accounts

OBBBA created a new savings account for children, the **Trump account** (§530A). It is a traditional IRA set up for a child under 18 and opened by an election on Form 4547. Contributions could begin on July 4, 2026.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Annual contribution limit** | \$5,000 per child (indexed after 2027); not deductible |
| **Contributions outside the limit** | Rollovers, qualified general contributions from governments or charities, and the \$1,000 pilot deposit from Treasury for each U.S.-citizen child with an SSN born in 2025 through 2028 |
| **Employer contributions** | Up to \$2,500 per employee a year excluded from the employee's income under §128 |
| **Investments** | Until the year the child turns 18, only unleveraged funds that track a broad U.S. stock index and charge fees of 0.1% or less |
| **Distributions** | None before the year the child turns 18; after that, the account generally follows the traditional IRA rules |
| **Basis** | Only after-tax contributions (such as a parent's) are basis; the pilot deposit, general contributions, and excluded employer contributions are not |

For paying for college, a 529 plan is usually the better tool, because qualified 529 withdrawals are entirely tax-free while Trump account withdrawals are taxable except for basis. A Trump account works more like an early start on retirement savings, with tax deferred for decades.

### Coordinating 529 Plans with Education Credits

The same expenses cannot support both a tax-free 529 distribution and an education credit. A common plan is to pay \$4,000 of tuition from other funds, which earns the full \$2,500 American opportunity tax credit, and pay the remaining costs from the 529.

| Feature | American Opportunity Tax Credit (AOTC) | Lifetime Learning Credit (LLC) |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximum credit** | \$2,500 per student (100% of the first \$2,000 of expenses and 25% of the next \$2,000) | \$2,000 per return (20% of up to \$10,000 of expenses) |
| **Eligible students** | First 4 years of postsecondary education, enrolled at least half-time | Any postsecondary course, including courses to improve job skills |
| **Refundable** | 40%, up to \$1,000 | No |
| **MAGI phase-out** | \$80,000 to \$90,000 (\$160,000 to \$180,000 MFJ), not indexed | Same |
| **Identification (starting 2026)** | Taxpayer's and student's SSNs and the institution's EIN on the return | Taxpayer's and student's SSNs on the return |

A missing SSN or EIN is treated as a math error, so the IRS can deny the credit without an audit.

### Student Loans

Interest on qualified student loans is deductible above the line, up to \$2,500 a year, and phased out at higher MAGI (indexed). The deduction is not available to married taxpayers filing separately or to someone claimed as a dependent.

Employers can pay up to \$5,250 a year of an employee's student loan principal or interest tax-free through an educational assistance program (§127). OBBBA made this permanent, with the \$5,250 indexed after 2026. Loans discharged because of the student's death or total and permanent disability are permanently excluded from income. The broader exclusion for other student loan discharges in 2021 through 2025 has expired, so most other forgiven student debt is taxable unless another exclusion applies, such as insolvency or public service loan forgiveness.

### Grants and Scholarships

A **qualified scholarship** or grant is tax-free to a degree candidate to the extent it pays tuition, required fees, books, supplies, and equipment. Amounts for room, board, and travel are taxable, and so are payments for teaching or research services the student must perform. A tax-free scholarship also reduces the expenses that count toward the education credits and tax-free 529 distributions.

:::tip[Planning Strategy]

For families who can start saving early, a 529 plan usually beats borrowing later: years of tax-free compounding are worth far more than a student loan interest deduction capped at \$2,500 a year. When the student receives a scholarship, the 529 money is not stranded, because withdrawals up to the scholarship amount avoid the 10% additional tax.

:::

## Insurance for Risk Mitigation

Insurance planning questions focus on what each type of policy is for and how it is taxed. You will not need actuarial calculations.

### Life Insurance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Replaces income and covers expenses for dependents after the insured's death |
| **Term life** | Coverage for a set period; lower premiums; no cash value |
| **Whole (permanent) life** | Lifetime coverage; higher premiums; builds cash value that grows tax-deferred |
| **Death benefit** | Generally received **income tax-free** by the beneficiary under §101(a) |
| **Cash value** | Withdrawals are tax-free up to the owner's basis (premiums paid); policy loans are not taxable unless the policy lapses with a loan outstanding |
| **Estate tax** | Proceeds are included in the insured's gross estate if the insured held any incidents of ownership or the estate is the beneficiary |

Who owns the policy matters. Transferring a policy to an irrevocable life insurance trust keeps the proceeds out of the insured's estate, provided the insured survives the transfer by three years. Business owners also need to watch their buy-sell agreements. In *Connelly v. United States* (2024), the Supreme Court held that when a corporation owns life insurance to fund the redemption of a deceased shareholder's stock, the proceeds increase the corporation's value for estate tax, and the obligation to redeem the shares does not offset them. A cross-purchase agreement, in which the owners insure each other, keeps the proceeds out of the corporation's value.

### Long-Term Care Insurance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Covers nursing home, assisted living, and home health care costs that health insurance and Medicare do not |
| **Premiums** | Qualified long-term care premiums count as medical expenses, up to age-based limits (indexed), subject to the 7.5% of AGI floor; self-employed individuals can deduct them above the line |
| **Benefits** | Generally tax-free up to a per diem limit (indexed) or the actual cost of care |

### Umbrella Liability Policies

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Adds liability coverage **above** the limits of homeowners, auto, and other underlying policies |
| **Coverage** | Catastrophic liability claims, such as lawsuits after an accident |
| **Tax treatment** | Premiums are personal expenses and **not deductible** |

## Legal Ownership of Assets and Beneficiary Designations

How an asset is titled decides three things at death: who receives it, whether it goes through probate, and how much of it is included in the gross estate (and therefore gets a new basis). For 2026, the basic exclusion amount is \$15,000,000 per person, fixed by OBBBA for 2026 and indexed after that, and portability lets a surviving spouse use the deceased spouse's unused exclusion. With exclusions that large, most clients owe no estate tax, so ownership planning usually turns on income tax basis, probate, and making sure assets reach the intended people. The estate and gift tax system itself is covered in [Gift Taxation](/tcp/gift-taxation).

### Forms of Ownership

| Ownership Type | Key Features | Included in the Decedent's Gross Estate |
|---|---|---|
| **Sole ownership** | One owner with full control; passes by will through probate | Full FMV |
| **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JTWROS)** | Two or more owners; the survivors automatically receive the decedent's share without probate | Spouses: 50%. Others: in proportion to the decedent's contribution to the purchase price |
| **Tenancy by the entirety** | A JTWROS for married couples, with protection from the creditors of one spouse | 50% |
| **Tenancy in common** | Two or more owners with no survivorship; each share passes by will or intestacy through probate | The decedent's share |
| **Community property** | In community property states, property acquired during the marriage is owned equally by the spouses | 50%, but both halves receive a basis step-up |

:::tip[Community Property Basis Step-Up]

Inherited property generally takes a basis equal to its FMV at the date of death. For spousal JTWROS property, only the decedent's half is included in the estate, so only that half gets the step-up. For community property, both halves get the step-up, even though only half is included. This is a significant income tax advantage when appreciated property is later sold.

:::

### Beneficiary Designations

Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death or transfer-on-death accounts **override** the will. If a client's will leaves everything to the children but the IRA beneficiary form still names a former spouse, the former spouse receives the IRA.

| Asset | Passes Under |
|---|---|
| Retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s) | The **beneficiary designation** on file with the plan or custodian |
| Life insurance | The **beneficiary designation** on the policy |
| Bank accounts | A **payable-on-death (POD)** designation |
| Brokerage accounts | A **transfer-on-death (TOD)** designation |
| Other real and personal property | The **will**, unless the property is jointly owned with survivorship rights |

Assets that pass by beneficiary designation, survivorship, or POD and TOD designations avoid probate, but they are still included in the gross estate. The choice of beneficiary also affects income tax. A surviving spouse can roll an inherited IRA into their own IRA, while most other beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years, paying tax on traditional IRA withdrawals as they go.

:::warning

A common planning mistake is failing to update beneficiary designations after marriage, divorce, or the birth of a child. A question may describe beneficiary designations that conflict with the client's stated wishes and ask you to spot the problem.

:::

## Summary

| Topic | Key Concept |
|---|---|
| Traditional IRA | Deductible contributions if eligible; ordinary income on distribution; RMDs from age 73 |
| Roth IRA | After-tax contributions; tax-free qualified distributions; no lifetime RMDs |
| 401(k) | Higher limits than IRAs; employer match; traditional and Roth options, with no lifetime RMDs for Roth 401(k)s |
| Annuities | Tax-deferred growth; earnings first on withdrawals; exclusion ratio on annuity payments |
| Traditional versus Roth | Pretax wins if the rate at withdrawal is lower; Roth wins if higher; equal if the same |
| Defined benefit versus defined contribution | DB: employer bears investment risk; DC: employee bears it |
| Investments | Compare after-tax returns; use tax-equivalent yield for municipal bonds; consider the 3.8% NIIT |
| 529 plans | Tax-free growth for qualified education expenses; \$20,000 K-12 limit from 2026; rollovers to ABLE and Roth accounts |
| Trump accounts | Child's traditional IRA; \$5,000 annual limit; no distributions before the year the child turns 18 |
| Education credits | AOTC up to \$2,500 per student; LLC up to \$2,000 per return; same expenses cannot also support a tax-free 529 withdrawal |
| Life insurance | Death benefit income tax-free; included in the estate if the insured owns the policy |
| Long-term care and umbrella insurance | LTC premiums may be medical expenses; umbrella premiums are not deductible |
| Asset ownership | Title decides who inherits, probate, estate inclusion, and basis step-up |
| Beneficiary designations | Override the will; review after major life events |
