# Business Analysis and Reporting (BAR)

**Business Analysis and Reporting** is one of the three discipline sections of the CPA exam, alongside Information Systems and Controls (ISC) and Tax Compliance and Planning (TCP). Every candidate takes the three core sections (AUD, FAR, and REG) and chooses one discipline. BAR is the natural choice if your career runs toward financial reporting, financial analysis, or technical accounting, because it goes past FAR in two directions: it asks you to **analyze** results rather than just record them, and it covers advanced topics such as business combinations, derivatives, and governmental reporting in more depth.

This section of the CPA Bear Book follows the 2027 BAR blueprint. Whether you are computing the net present value of a capital project for **Panda Industries**, judging when **Cub Entertainment** should recognize revenue on a multi-element contract, or reconciling a city's fund statements to its government-wide statements, the chapters ahead walk you through the ideas, the rules, and worked examples.

:::info[Exam Structure]

The BAR exam is **4 hours** long, with **50 multiple-choice questions** and **7 task-based simulations**. The two parts are weighted equally (50% each). The questions come in five testlets: two of multiple-choice questions, then three of simulations.

:::

## Content Areas and Weights

The 2027 blueprint, effective January 1, 2027, organizes BAR into three areas. The weights are unchanged from 2026.

| Content area | What it covers | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Business Analysis** | Financial statement analysis, non-GAAP and non-financial measures, cost accounting, budgeting and forecasting, capital structure, valuation models, risk management, and economic influences | 40-50% |
| **II. Technical Accounting and Reporting** | Selected FASB topics (intangibles, software, revenue, stock compensation, R&D, business combinations, consolidations, derivatives, leases), SEC reporting, and employee benefit plan statements | 35-45% |
| **III. State and Local Governments** | Financial reporting under GASB standards | 10-20% |

BAR leans toward higher-order skills. Analysis questions make up 30-40% of the exam, application 45-55%, and remembering and understanding 10-20%. Evaluation is not tested. The AICPA publishes the blueprints at aicpa-cima.com; check there for any later revision before you finalize a study plan.

:::note[Testing before January 1, 2027?]

This book still applies. The only 2027 change to BAR clarifies that the OPEB liability task covers plans administered through a trust and plans that are not, and both cases were already part of the standards. A few new FASB standards (ASUs 2025-03, 2025-04, 2025-07, and 2025-09) become testable on January 1, 2027; the chapters that cover them say so.

:::

For a section-by-section summary, see the [2027 blueprint guide](/blueprint/2027).

## What This Section Covers

### Area I: Business Analysis (40-50%)

This is the largest area and the one that makes BAR different from FAR. Instead of recording transactions, you interpret them.

- **Financial statement analysis**: comparing results with prior periods and budgets, explaining variances, interpreting ratios, and using data analytics outputs to explain results. If **Bear Co.** reports a falling current ratio while revenue grows, you should be able to say what that signals about working capital.
- **Non-financial and non-GAAP measures**: benchmarking, the balanced scorecard, and measures such as EBITDA, free cash flow, and customer retention.
- **Managerial and cost accounting**: fixed, variable, and mixed costs; absorption, variable, activity-based, process, and job order costing; variance analysis; and price, volume, and mix analysis of sales.
- **Budgeting, forecasting, and projection**: preparing data for analysis, building budgets on supportable assumptions, forecasting techniques, and planning tools such as sensitivity, what-if, and breakeven analysis.
- **Capital structure**: cost of capital, leverage, liquidity, loan covenants, and financing strategies. You might need to show how a new loan would affect **Polar Inc.**'s covenants.
- **Investment decision models**: present value and NPV, IRR, payback, economic value added, and how changes in assumptions (for example, in the Black-Scholes model) change a valuation. These tools tell you whether **Kodiak Partners** should accept a proposed investment.
- **Risk management**: the COSO Enterprise Risk Management framework, including its application to ESG risks, strategies for mitigating financial risks, working capital management, and SWOT analysis.
- **Economic and market influences**: supply, demand, and elasticity; inflation; opportunity cost; and how market conditions shape strategy and acquisition decisions.

### Area II: Technical Accounting and Reporting (35-45%)

These topics build on FAR but are tested at greater depth, often through contracts and agreements you must interpret.

- **Indefinite-lived intangible assets and goodwill**: initial measurement, impairment indicators, and impairment testing, including the private company alternative.
- **Internally developed software**: capitalization of software for internal use and software to be sold. The project-stage model for internal-use software remains the tested rule through 2027; ASU 2025-06 replaces it for testing starting January 1, 2028.
- **Revenue recognition**: applying the five-step model of ASC 606 to contracts, and using data analytics to find errors and unusual items in recorded revenue.
- **Stock compensation**: grant date, vesting conditions, valuation inputs and models, and journal entries for equity-classified and liability-classified awards.
- **Research and development costs**: identifying R&D and calculating the amount expensed.
- **Business combinations**: the acquisition method, goodwill or a bargain purchase gain, noncontrolling interests, contingent consideration, and measurement period adjustments.
- **Consolidated financial statements**: variable interest entities, noncontrolling interests, functional currency, and foreign currency translation adjustments.
- **Derivatives and hedge accounting**: identifying derivatives, hedge accounting criteria, presentation of gains and losses, and journal entries for interest rate swaps in fair value and cash flow hedges.
- **Leases**: lessor classification and accounting, seller-lessee accounting for sale and leaseback transactions, and interpreting lease agreements to check a lessee's accounting.
- **Public company reporting**: Regulation S-X and Regulation S-K, XBRL, and segment reporting under ASC 280.
- **Employee benefit plans**: the financial statements of defined benefit and defined contribution pension plans themselves (the statements of net assets available for benefits and of changes in net assets available for benefits, plus accumulated plan benefit information for defined benefit plans) and their note disclosures.

FAR may ask you to compute a lessee's lease liability. BAR is more likely to hand you the lease agreement and ask whether the lessor has a sales-type or operating lease, or whether the lessee recorded it correctly. Plan to review each topic with that kind of analysis in mind.

### Area III: State and Local Governments (10-20%)

This area covers governmental accounting and reporting under GASB standards. It carries the smallest weight, but the terminology, measurement focuses, and statements are unlike anything in business accounting, so allow extra time if the material is new to you.

- **The financial section of the annual comprehensive financial report**: government-wide statements, governmental, proprietary, and fiduciary fund statements, notes, MD&A, budgetary comparison reporting, other RSI, and the financial reporting entity.
- **Deriving the government-wide statements**: converting governmental fund amounts to governmental activities and preparing the required reconciliations.
- **Specific transactions**: net position and fund balance components, capital assets, long-term liabilities (including pensions, OPEB, and compensated absences), interfund activity, nonexchange revenues, expenditures and expenses, and budgetary accounting with encumbrances.

Several recent GASB statements are now tested, most notably **GASB 103** (financial reporting model improvements), which changed MD&A, budgetary comparisons, and the proprietary fund operating statement, and **GASB 101** (compensated absences). The chapters in this area teach the current rules.

## How to Approach BAR

1. **Start with Area I.** It carries the most weight and builds the analytical habits the rest of the exam rewards. Begin with financial statement analysis and cost accounting, then move to budgeting, capital structure, and valuation.
2. **Layer in Area II.** If you passed FAR recently, use that base, but focus on the parts that need judgment: revenue contracts, consolidation, and hedges.
3. **Finish with Area III.** Governmental accounting is self-contained. Learn the fund structure and measurement focuses first, then the transactions and reconciliations.
4. **Practice scenarios, not just formulas.** Analysis and application together make up most of the exam. Work problems where, for example, **Sloth Security** must decide whether to lease or buy equipment, or **Panda Industries** must decide whether an acquisition's goodwill is impaired.
5. **Build a formula sheet.** Ratios, WACC, NPV, and the variance formulas come up constantly in Area I.
6. **Connect the areas.** One simulation might ask you to record **Bear Co.**'s acquisition of a subsidiary (Area II), test the goodwill for impairment (Area II), and analyze the effect on its ratios (Area I).

:::tip[Common Pitfall]

Many candidates treat BAR as "more FAR." It is not. Memorizing rules is not enough when a simulation asks you to interpret a contract or explain a variance. Practice explaining why a number is what it is, not just computing it.

:::
