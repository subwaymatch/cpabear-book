# Economic and Market Influences on Business

No business operates in isolation. Demand for its products rises and falls, inflation changes what its inputs cost and what its debts are worth, and interest rates and exchange rates move the value of its investments and foreign sales. The BAR section expects you to **quantify** these effects (elasticity, real prices, rate and currency sensitivity, opportunity cost) and to **interpret** how market forces shape strategy, up to decisions to acquire or sell a business.

This chapter starts with supply, demand, and elasticity, then covers inflation, interest rate and currency risk, and opportunity cost. It closes with how market forces shape strategy and how to compare acquisition and divestiture opportunities.

---

## Supply and Demand

The **law of demand** says that, all else equal, buyers want less of a product as its price rises. The **law of supply** says that, all else equal, producers offer more as the price rises. The **equilibrium price** is where the two meet: the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded.

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Demand curve** | Downward-sloping relationship between price and quantity demanded |
| **Supply curve** | Upward-sloping relationship between price and quantity supplied |
| **Equilibrium** | Price at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded |
| **Surplus** | Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded (price is above equilibrium) |
| **Shortage** | Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied (price is below equilibrium) |

### Price Elasticity of Demand

Knowing that demand falls when price rises is not enough to set a price. A company needs to know **how much** it falls. Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures that sensitivity:

$$
\text{PED} = \frac{\%\text{ Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\%\text{ Change in Price}}
$$

PED is normally negative, so it is compared in absolute value:

| Elasticity Type | Absolute Value of PED | Meaning | Effect of a Price Increase on Total Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** | Greater than 1 | Buyers are very price-sensitive | Total revenue **decreases** |
| **Inelastic** | Less than 1 | Buyers are not very price-sensitive | Total revenue **increases** |
| **Unit elastic** | Equal to 1 | Quantity changes in proportion to price | Total revenue **unchanged** |

**Example (Bear Co.):** Bear Co. sells premium notebooks at \$20 each. When it raises the price to \$24 (a 20% increase), quantity demanded falls from 10,000 to 7,000 units (a 30% decrease).

$$
\text{PED} = \frac{-30\%}{20\%} = -1.5
$$

The absolute value, 1.5, is greater than 1, so demand is **elastic**. Total revenue falls from \$200,000 (10,000 × \$20) to \$168,000 (7,000 × \$24). The price increase hurt revenue. Companies whose customers have few alternatives, such as utilities or makers of essential medicines, face inelastic demand and have more pricing power.

### Cross-Price and Income Elasticity

Two related measures look at other influences on demand. **Cross-price elasticity** measures how demand for one good responds to a change in the price of another:

$$
\text{Cross-Price Elasticity} = \frac{\%\text{ Change in Quantity Demanded of Good A}}{\%\text{ Change in Price of Good B}}
$$

A **positive** value means the goods are **substitutes** (for example, competing software products): when B gets more expensive, buyers switch to A. A **negative** value means they are **complements** (for example, printers and ink cartridges): when B gets more expensive, buyers want less of both.

**Income elasticity** measures how demand responds to changes in buyers' income. A positive value marks a **normal good**, which people buy more of as they earn more. A negative value marks an **inferior good**, which people buy less of as their income rises.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. It is usually measured with one of three indices:

| Index | What It Measures |
|---|---|
| **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** | Price changes for a basket of consumer goods and services |
| **Producer Price Index (PPI)** | Price changes at the producer (wholesale) level |
| **GDP Deflator** | The broadest measure; covers all goods and services produced in the economy |

### Real and Nominal Values

A **nominal** amount is stated in current dollars. A **real** amount removes the effect of inflation so it reflects purchasing power. To find a product's real price, divide the nominal price by the price index (with the base year at 1.00). A product that cost \$100 in the base year and \$110 today, when the price index stands at 1.10, has the same real price as before.

For rates of return, the Fisher equation gives the relationship. The approximate version is easy to use:

$$
\text{Real Rate} \approx \text{Nominal Rate} - \text{Inflation Rate}
$$

The exact version is:

$$
(1 + \text{Nominal Rate}) = (1 + \text{Real Rate}) \times (1 + \text{Inflation Rate})
$$

**Example (Bear Co.):** Bear Co. holds a bond yielding 7% nominal, and inflation is 3%. The approximate real rate is 7% - 3% = 4%. The exact real rate is (1.07 ÷ 1.03) - 1 = 3.88%.

### Effects on Investments, Debt, and Future Expenses

| Area | Effect of Inflation |
|---|---|
| **Fixed-rate debt** | **Benefits borrowers**, who repay with dollars that buy less |
| **Fixed-income investments** | Erodes the real return of bondholders |
| **Future expenses** | Budgets must be adjusted upward; understating inflation leads to cost overruns |
| **Inventory (FIFO)** | Higher reported profit, because older, lower costs are matched against current revenue |
| **Inventory (LIFO)** | Current costs are matched against current revenue, so reported profit and taxes are lower |

**Example (Polar Inc.):** Polar Inc. has a \$500,000 fixed-rate loan at 5% with three years remaining. If inflation runs at 4% a year, the loan's real cost is only about 1% (5% - 4%), so inflation is shrinking Polar's debt burden in real terms.

To project a future expense, grow today's cost at the expected inflation rate. A \$200,000 annual maintenance contract that rises with 3% inflation will cost about \$200,000 × 1.03 × 1.03 × 1.03 = \$218,545 in three years.

**Deflation** (falling prices) has the opposite effect. It raises the real burden of debt, and it can feed on itself: buyers delay purchases while they expect lower prices, which reduces demand further.

## Interest Rate Risk

Interest rates touch almost every financial decision. When rates rise, borrowing costs more, the present value of future cash flows falls, and bond prices fall. When rates fall, the reverse happens.

Bond prices move **inversely** to interest rates, and **duration** measures how strongly. Duration is the weighted-average time to receive a bond's cash flows, and it serves as a rule of thumb for price sensitivity:

$$
\text{Approximate Price Change} = -\text{Duration} \times \Delta \text{Interest Rate} \times \text{Bond Price}
$$

**Example (Kodiak Partners):** Kodiak Partners holds a bond portfolio worth \$2,000,000 with an average duration of 6 years. If interest rates rise by 0.5%:

$$
\text{Price Change} \approx -6 \times 0.005 \times \$2{,}000{,}000 = -\$60{,}000
$$

The portfolio would lose about \$60,000 of value. The longer the duration, the greater the interest rate risk. An entity can reduce that risk by shifting to shorter-maturity bonds or by hedging with derivatives such as interest rate swaps.

## Currency Exchange Risk

An entity that buys, sells, or operates abroad is exposed to changes in exchange rates in three ways:

| Exposure Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Transaction** | Receivables and payables denominated in a foreign currency change in value before they are settled | Grizzly Industries invoices a customer in euros, and the euro weakens before collection |
| **Translation** | A foreign subsidiary's statements change in dollar terms when they are translated for consolidation | A subsidiary's yen balance sheet is worth fewer dollars after the yen falls |
| **Economic** | Exchange rates change the entity's competitive position and long-run cash flows | A strong dollar makes U.S. exports more expensive abroad |

### Hedging with Forward Contracts

A **forward contract** fixes an exchange rate today for an exchange on a future date, which removes transaction exposure.

**Example (Grizzly Industries):** Grizzly Industries expects to receive €500,000 from a European customer in 90 days. The spot rate is \$1.10 per euro, and management fears the euro will weaken. Grizzly enters a 90-day forward contract to sell euros at \$1.08.

- **Without the hedge,** if the euro falls to \$1.04, Grizzly receives 500,000 × \$1.04 = \$520,000.
- **With the hedge,** Grizzly receives 500,000 × \$1.08 = **\$540,000** however the euro moves.

The forward protects \$20,000 in this scenario. The trade-off is that if the euro had strengthened, Grizzly would have given up the gain. A hedge buys certainty, not profit.

For translation exposure, the accounting matters as well. When a foreign subsidiary's functional currency is its local currency, its statements are translated into dollars under ASC 830 using the **current rate method**: assets and liabilities at the current (balance sheet) rate, revenues and expenses at the rates when they occurred (a weighted-average rate in practice), and equity at historical rates. The resulting **translation adjustment** goes to other comprehensive income, not net income. The chapter on [Consolidated Financial Statements](../technical-accounting-combinations-and-reporting/consolidated-financial-statements.md) covers translation and remeasurement in full.

## Opportunity Cost

**Opportunity cost** is the benefit given up by choosing one alternative over the next best one. It never appears in the accounting records, but every decision has one, and ignoring it leads to poor choices.

$$
\text{Opportunity Cost} = \text{Benefit of the Best Alternative Not Chosen}
$$

**Example (Cub Entertainment):** Cub Entertainment has \$1,000,000 to invest and two choices:

| | Project A | Project B |
|---|---|---|
| **Expected annual return** | 12% (\$120,000) | 9% (\$90,000) |
| **Risk level** | Moderate | Low |

If Cub chooses Project A, its opportunity cost is the \$90,000 it could have earned from Project B. If it chooses B, the opportunity cost is A's \$120,000. Project A earns \$30,000 a year more than its opportunity cost, so it is the better choice if Cub is willing to accept its higher risk.

The same idea runs through many exam questions: keeping an asset versus selling it (the forgone sale proceeds), making a component versus buying it (the other uses of the factory space), and accepting a special order when the plant is at capacity (the contribution margin from the regular sales displaced). In each case, compare the chosen option with the **best** alternative, not the worst.

## Market Influences on Business Strategy

Market forces shape where a company buys its inputs, what it sells, where it sells, and how lean it must run. The blueprint names four kinds of response.

**Sourcing production inputs.** Relying on a single supplier leaves a company exposed to disruptions such as natural disasters or geopolitical events. Spreading purchases across suppliers and regions reduces that concentration risk, usually at a higher cost. Acquiring a supplier (backward vertical integration) secures supply but ties up capital.

**Innovating to develop or diversify products.** Companies innovate to stay ahead of competitors, and diversification spreads risk across products or markets. **Related diversification** moves into adjacent products, such as Sloth Security adding cybersecurity consulting to its physical security business. **Unrelated diversification** enters a different industry, such as a food company buying a technology firm.

**Seeking new markets.** Expanding into new regions or customer segments can drive growth, but it brings new regulations, cultural differences, and currency exposure.

**Productivity and cost-cutting initiatives.** When competition or rising costs squeeze margins, companies turn to automation, lean manufacturing to eliminate waste, outsourcing of non-core functions, and economies of scale from higher volume.

### Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter's framework explains why some industries are more profitable than others. Profitability depends on five forces:

| Force | Key Question |
|---|---|
| **Threat of new entrants** | How easily can new competitors enter? |
| **Bargaining power of suppliers** | Can suppliers dictate terms? |
| **Bargaining power of buyers** | Can customers force prices down? |
| **Threat of substitutes** | Are alternative products available? |
| **Competitive rivalry** | How intense is competition among existing firms? |

Industries with high barriers to entry and weak buyer power, such as pharmaceuticals, tend to earn higher margins than industries with low barriers and intense rivalry, such as general retail.

## Acquisitions and Divestitures

Companies acquire other businesses to capture **synergies** (cost savings or added revenue from combining operations), gain **market share** and distribution channels, **diversify** away from a single product or market, or obtain **capabilities** such as technology, talent, or intellectual property.

### Valuing an Acquisition Target

Two valuation approaches are common. **Comparable multiples** value the target by applying the ratios at which similar companies trade:

$$
\text{Enterprise Value} = \text{EBITDA} \times \text{Industry Multiple}
$$

**Discounted cash flow (DCF)** estimates intrinsic value as the present value of projected free cash flows, plus a terminal value for the years beyond the forecast:

$$
\text{Value} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{FCF}_t}{(1 + r)^t} + \frac{\text{Terminal Value}}{(1 + r)^n}
$$

**Enterprise value** is the value of the whole business to all capital providers. To find the value of the **equity**, subtract the target's debt and add its cash.

**Example (Sloth Security):** Sloth Security is considering buying a smaller competitor with EBITDA of \$4,000,000. Comparable companies trade at 8 times EBITDA. The target has \$5,000,000 of debt and \$1,000,000 of cash.

$$
\text{Enterprise Value} = \$4{,}000{,}000 \times 8 = \$32{,}000{,}000
$$

$$
\text{Equity Value} = \$32{,}000{,}000 - \$5{,}000{,}000 + \$1{,}000{,}000 = \$28{,}000{,}000
$$

### Divestitures

A **divestiture** (selling a business unit) makes sense when the unit no longer fits the strategy, is underperforming and absorbing resources, must be separated for regulatory reasons, or is worth more to the company as cash that can be redeployed at a higher return.

| Factor | Acquisition | Divestiture |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Grow capabilities or market presence | Refocus on core operations |
| **Cash flow** | Requires an outflow (purchase price) | Generates an inflow (sale proceeds) |
| **Risk** | Integration risk, overpayment risk | Lost revenue, employee disruption |
| **Valuation focus** | Is the target worth the price? | Is the company getting fair value for the unit? |

:::caution

Acquisition **synergies are often overestimated**. When comparing opportunities, check whether the price paid exceeds the target's standalone value plus a realistic estimate of synergies. The difference is the premium, and the synergies must earn it back.

:::

## Quantifying Economic and Market Risks

The measures in this chapter can be collected by type of risk:

| Risk Area | Measure | Formula or Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest rate risk** | Duration | Weighted-average time to receive a bond's cash flows; approximate % price change per 1% rate change |
| **Currency risk** | Net foreign currency exposure | Foreign currency assets minus foreign currency liabilities |
| **Inflation risk** | Real rate of return | Nominal return minus inflation (approximately) |
| **Price risk** | Price elasticity; beta | Sensitivity of demand to price; sensitivity of a stock's return to the market |
| **Liquidity risk** | Current ratio | Current assets ÷ Current liabilities |
| **Credit risk** | Debt-to-equity; times interest earned | Total debt ÷ Total equity; EBIT ÷ Interest expense |
