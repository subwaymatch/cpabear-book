# Proprietary Funds Financial Statements

Some government activities run like businesses. A water utility bills customers for what they use, a parking garage charges by the hour, and a central motor pool bills other departments for vehicles. Governments account for these activities in **proprietary funds**, which use the same **economic resources measurement focus** and **accrual basis** as a commercial enterprise. Proprietary fund statements therefore report capital assets, depreciation, and long-term debt directly, with no conversion needed.

The BAR blueprint expects you to recall the basic concepts of proprietary funds and to prepare all three of their statements from trial balance data: the statement of net position, the statement of revenues, expenses, and changes in fund net position, and the statement of cash flows. This chapter explains the two kinds of proprietary funds, then works through each statement using one utility. Pay close attention to the operating statement: **GASB 103** (testable since July 1, 2025) changed its format and the definitions of operating and nonoperating items.

---

## Enterprise Funds and Internal Service Funds

The two proprietary fund types differ in who the customer is.

| | Enterprise funds | Internal service funds |
|---|---|---|
| **Customers** | The public (external users) | Other departments, funds, or governments, on a cost-reimbursement basis |
| **Examples** | Water and sewer utility, airport, transit system, golf course | Motor pool, IT services, central printing, self-insurance |
| **Columns in the fund statements** | Each major enterprise fund in its own column; nonmajor funds combined | All internal service funds combined in one column |
| **Government-wide reporting** | Business-type activities | Usually folded into governmental activities |

An **enterprise fund** may be used for any activity that charges fees to external users, and it **must** be used when any one of these is true:

1. The activity is financed with debt secured **solely** by a pledge of the net revenues from its own fees and charges.
2. Laws or regulations require that the activity's costs, including capital costs such as depreciation or debt service, be recovered with fees and charges rather than taxes.
3. The government's pricing policies set fees and charges to recover the activity's costs, including capital costs.

:::tip[Exam Tip]

Watch the word "solely" in the first test. If revenue bonds are also backed by the government's full faith and credit, they are not payable solely from the activity's revenues, so that criterion is not met (although one of the other two may still be).

:::

An **internal service fund** is never required. Management chooses to use one when it wants to accumulate the cost of a shared service and bill it back to the departments that use it. Because most internal service funds serve governmental departments, their assets and liabilities are normally reported in the governmental activities column of the government-wide statements. If an internal service fund mainly serves enterprise funds, it goes to business-type activities instead. The adjustments this requires are covered in [Deriving Government-Wide Financial Statements](../governmental-transactions-and-items/deriving-government-wide-financial-statements.md).

Enterprise funds are subject to the **major fund** test. A fund is major if its assets plus deferred outflows, liabilities plus deferred inflows, revenues, or expenses are at least 10 percent of the enterprise fund total for that element **and** at least 5 percent of the combined governmental and enterprise fund total for the same element. A government may also present any fund it considers particularly important as major. Internal service funds are never major funds.

## Measurement Focus and the Three Statements

Because proprietary funds use the economic resources focus and accrual basis, they record **expenses**, not expenditures. A proprietary fund records depreciation, capitalizes the equipment it buys, and reports the bonds it issues as liabilities. If you see "expenditures" on a proprietary fund statement, something is wrong.

Proprietary funds present three statements:

1. **Statement of net position**: assets, deferred outflows of resources, liabilities, deferred inflows of resources, and net position at year-end.
2. **Statement of revenues, expenses, and changes in fund net position**: operating results, subsidies, and other nonoperating items for the year.
3. **Statement of cash flows**: cash flows in four categories, using the direct method.

The rest of this chapter prepares all three for the **Bear City Water Utility Fund**.

---

## Statement of Net Position

The statement of net position may be classified (current and noncurrent assets and liabilities) or presented in order of liquidity. Most governments use the classified format. Net position is the residual:

$$
\text{Net Position} = \text{Assets} + \text{Deferred Outflows} - \text{Liabilities} - \text{Deferred Inflows}
$$

It is split into the same three components used government-wide: **net investment in capital assets**, **restricted**, and **unrestricted**. Those calculations, including the treatment of unspent bond proceeds, are explained in [Net Position and Components](../governmental-transactions-and-items/net-position-and-components.md). Designations by the governing board are not restrictions, so they stay in unrestricted.

## Statement of Revenues, Expenses, and Changes in Fund Net Position

This statement separates the fund's day-to-day business from everything else. Before GASB 103, each government wrote its own policy for what counted as "operating," so similar utilities classified items differently. GASB 103 replaced those policies with a definition.

### Operating and Nonoperating Items Under GASB 103

GASB 103 defines **nonoperating** revenues and expenses as a closed list of five categories:

1. **Subsidies** received and provided
2. **Contributions to permanent and term endowments**
3. Revenues and expenses related to **financing** (for example, interest expense)
4. Resources from the **disposal of capital assets and inventory** (gains and losses on sale)
5. **Investment income and expenses**

**Operating** revenues and expenses are everything else. Charges for services, salaries, supplies, utilities, repairs, and depreciation are all operating.

There is one exception. An item that would normally be nonoperating is operating if it is part of the fund's **principal ongoing operations**. Interest earned by a fund that makes home loans to first-time buyers is operating revenue for that fund, because lending is its business.

**Subsidies** get special attention. A subsidy is (a) resources received from another party or fund that does not get goods or services in return, and that keep the fund's fees lower than they would otherwise be; (b) resources the fund provides to another party or fund that does not provide goods or services in return, and that the fund recovers through its pricing; or (c) **all other transfers**. Subsidies for operations are reported as **noncapital subsidies**, followed by a new required subtotal: **operating income (loss) and noncapital subsidies**. Capital contributions (such as capital grants and developer contributions) and transfers restricted for capital assets are reported with the other nonoperating revenues and expenses.

### The Required Format

GASB 103 prescribes this order:

| Caption |
|---|
| Operating revenues (detailed), then total operating revenues |
| Operating expenses (detailed), then total operating expenses |
| **Operating income (loss)** |
| Noncapital subsidies (detailed), then total noncapital subsidies |
| **Operating income (loss) and noncapital subsidies** |
| Other nonoperating revenues and expenses (detailed), then their total |
| **Income (loss) before unusual or infrequent items** |
| Unusual or infrequent items (detailed) |
| **Increase (decrease) in fund net position** |
| Fund net position, beginning and end of year |

**Unusual or infrequent items** replaced the old special and extraordinary items. They are transactions or events that are unusual in nature **or** infrequent in occurrence, such as flood damage, and each one is shown separately as the last flow before the change in net position. The notes say which program or function it relates to and whether management controlled it.

:::warning[Superseded Format]

Older materials show "income before contributions, transfers, and special and extraordinary items," with capital contributions and transfers listed below nonoperating items. That is the superseded GASB 34 format. For 2027, transfers are noncapital subsidies (or, if restricted for capital, other nonoperating items), capital contributions are other nonoperating revenues, and special and extraordinary items no longer exist.

:::

### Example

During 20X5 the water utility transferred \$150,000 to the General Fund (a payment in lieu of taxes) and received \$600,000 of capital grants for a new main.

| &emsp;**Total operating expenses** | **7,160,000** |
| **Operating income** | **1,380,000** |
| **Noncapital subsidies:** | |

Two classifications are worth a second look. **Interest expense** on the revenue bonds is nonoperating because it is a financing expense, even though the bonds paid for the utility's core plant. **Connection fees** that only recover the cost of hooking up a new customer are operating revenue; fees that also help pay for system capacity (often called system development or capital recovery fees) are capital contributions.

## Statement of Cash Flows

Proprietary funds are the only funds that present a statement of cash flows. It differs from a FASB statement of cash flows in two ways: the **direct method** is required, and cash flows are sorted into **four** categories instead of three.

| Category | What goes here |
|---|---|
| **Operating activities** | Cash received from customers and from other funds for services; cash paid to employees and suppliers |
| **Noncapital financing activities** | Operating grants and subsidies received; transfers to and from other funds (other than for capital); borrowing and repayment of debt not related to capital assets, and the interest on it |
| **Capital and related financing activities** | Buying, building, or selling capital assets; capital grants and contributions received; transfers for capital purposes; proceeds of capital debt and the principal and interest paid on it |
| **Investing activities** | Buying and selling investments; interest and dividends received |

The four categories move several items to different places than FASB uses. Most of the exam questions on this statement come from these differences.

| Item | GASB (proprietary fund) | FASB |
|---|---|---|
| Interest and dividends received | Investing | Operating |
| Interest paid on capital-related debt | Capital and related financing | Operating |
| Interest paid on other debt | Noncapital financing | Operating |
| Purchase of capital assets | Capital and related financing | Investing |
| Operating grants received | Noncapital financing | Generally operating |

### Reconciliation of Operating Income to Operating Cash Flows

Even though the body uses the direct method, GASB also requires a reconciliation of **operating income** to net cash from operating activities. In effect you prepare both methods: direct in the statement, indirect in the schedule. Note that the reconciliation starts from operating income, not from the change in net position.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Operating income | \$1,380,000 |
| Adjustments: | |
| &emsp;Depreciation | 1,400,000 |
| &emsp;Increase in accounts receivable | (220,000) |
| &emsp;Decrease in inventories | 30,000 |
| &emsp;Increase in accounts payable | 40,000 |
| &emsp;Increase in accrued salaries | 40,000 |
| **Net cash provided by operating activities** | **\$2,670,000** |

The direct-method lines come from the same changes. Customers paid \$8,540,000 of revenue less the \$220,000 increase in receivables, or \$8,320,000. Employees were paid \$2,800,000 of personnel cost less the \$40,000 increase in accrued salaries, or \$2,760,000. Suppliers were paid the \$2,960,000 of purchased water, utilities, supplies, and repairs, less the \$30,000 drawn from inventory and the \$40,000 increase in payables, or \$2,890,000.

## Journal Entries

Proprietary fund entries look like business entries. A few typical ones for Bear City:

The water utility bills customers \$680,000 for December usage.

```journal
Dec 31
Dr. Accounts Receivable[a] 680,000
Cr. Charges for Services 680,000
```

The utility records a year of depreciation on its plant and equipment.

```journal
Dec 31
Dr. Depreciation Expense 1,400,000
Cr. Accumulated Depreciation[ca] 1,400,000
```

The utility issues \$1,000,000 of revenue bonds at par to build a pumping station. The liability stays in the fund.

```journal
Dr. Cash[a] 1,000,000
Cr. Revenue Bonds Payable[l] 1,000,000
```

Bear City's motor pool (an internal service fund) bills the police department, which is paid from the General Fund, \$45,000 for vehicle maintenance.

```journal
Dr. Due from General Fund[a] 45,000
Cr. Charges for Services 45,000
```

## Summary

| Topic | Proprietary funds | Governmental funds |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement focus and basis | Economic resources, accrual | Current financial resources, modified accrual |
| Capital assets and long-term debt | Reported in the fund | Not reported in the fund |
| Outflows | Expenses, including depreciation | Expenditures |
| Operating statement | Operating, noncapital subsidies, other nonoperating (GASB 103) | Revenues, expenditures, other financing sources and uses |
| Statement of cash flows | Required; direct method; four categories | Not presented |
| Bottom line | Net position | Fund balance |

## Practice Checkpoint

1. What three criteria make an enterprise fund mandatory?
2. Under GASB 103, what are the five categories of nonoperating revenues and expenses?
3. Where does a transfer out of an enterprise fund to the General Fund appear on the enterprise fund's operating statement?
4. Where is interest received classified in a proprietary fund statement of cash flows? Where is it classified under FASB?
5. Where is an internal service fund that mainly serves governmental departments reported in the government-wide statements?

:::info[Answers]

1. Debt secured solely by the activity's net revenues; laws or regulations requiring cost recovery through fees; or pricing policies designed to recover costs, including capital costs.
2. Subsidies; contributions to permanent and term endowments; financing revenues and expenses; disposals of capital assets and inventory; and investment income and expenses.
3. In noncapital subsidies, above the subtotal "operating income (loss) and noncapital subsidies," because all transfers not restricted for capital are subsidies.
4. Investing activities under GASB; operating activities under FASB.
5. In the governmental activities column.

:::
