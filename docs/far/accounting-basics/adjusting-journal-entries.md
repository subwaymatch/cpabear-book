# Adjusting Journal Entries

**Adjusting journal entries** are period-end entries that bring the accounts onto the accrual basis. They make sure revenues and expenses are recognized in the period they belong to.

:::info[Three rules]

1. Adjusting entries are recorded **before** the financial statements are issued.
2. Adjusting entries **never** include cash.
3. Each adjusting entry includes **one income statement account** and **one balance sheet account**.

:::

## The Four Core Types

Every adjustment is either an accrual or a deferral. **Accruals** record revenue or expense before cash changes hands. **Deferrals** adjust amounts for which cash changed hands first.

### Accrued Revenue

Revenue has been earned, but cash has not been received and the customer has not yet been billed.

**Example:** Kodiak Partners earns \$8,000 of consulting revenue in December and bills the client in January. On December 31, it records:

```journal
Dr. Accounts receivable          8,000
    Cr. Consulting revenue           8,000
```

### Accrued Expense

An expense has been incurred, but cash has not yet been paid.

**Example:** Panda Industries owes employees \$12,000 for the last week of the year and will pay them in January. On December 31, it records:

```journal
Dr. Wages expense               12,000
    Cr. Wages payable               12,000
```

### Deferred Revenue

Cash has already been received, but the revenue has not yet been earned. The receipt creates a liability, and the adjustment moves the earned portion to revenue.

**Example:** On October 1, Cub Entertainment collects \$36,000 for a 12-month subscription.

Original receipt:

```journal
Dr. Cash                        36,000
    Cr. Unearned revenue            36,000
```

Year-end adjustment for the three months earned (\$36,000 × 3/12):

```journal
Dr. Unearned revenue             9,000
    Cr. Subscription revenue         9,000
```

### Deferred Expense

Cash has already been paid, but part of the asset is still unexpired at year-end. The payment creates an asset, and the adjustment moves the expired portion to expense.

**Example:** On July 1, Bear Co. pays \$24,000 for one year of insurance coverage.

Original payment:

```journal
Dr. Prepaid insurance           24,000
    Cr. Cash                        24,000
```

Year-end adjustment for the six months expired (\$24,000 × 6/12):

```journal
Dr. Insurance expense           12,000
    Cr. Prepaid insurance           12,000
```

## Visual Summary

| Situation at year-end | Balance sheet effect | Income statement effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earned but not yet collected | Asset increases (receivable) | Revenue increases |
| Incurred but not yet paid | Liability increases (payable) | Expense increases |
| Collected before earned | Liability decreases (unearned revenue) | Revenue increases |
| Paid before incurred | Asset decreases (prepaid) | Expense increases |

## Why Adjusting Entries Matter

A missing adjusting entry misstates both the balance sheet and the income statement.

Return to Bear Co., which paid \$24,000 for annual insurance on July 1. If Bear Co. makes no adjusting entry on December 31, prepaid insurance is overstated by \$12,000 and insurance expense is understated by \$12,000. Pretax income is therefore overstated by \$12,000.

:::tip

If cash changed hands before year-end (or will change hands after it) and no cash moves at year-end, the year-end entry is usually an adjusting entry.

:::

## Correcting Entries Are Different

A correcting entry fixes an error. An adjusting entry updates timing. Correcting entries may involve cash; adjusting entries do not.

:::note[Checklist]

- Identify whether cash came first or performance came first.
- Decide whether the missing account is an asset or a liability.
- Remember that adjusting entries always touch one balance sheet account and one income statement account.

:::
