# Adjusting Journal Entries

During the year, most entries are triggered by cash: a customer pays, a supplier is paid, payroll goes out. At period-end, though, some revenues have been earned and some expenses incurred without any cash moving yet, and some cash that already moved relates partly to the next period. **Adjusting journal entries** fix that timing so the statements are on the accrual basis, with revenues and expenses in the period they belong to. This chapter covers the four types of adjustment, shows what goes wrong when one is missed, and distinguishes adjustments from corrections of errors.

Three features make adjusting entries easy to spot. They are recorded at period-end, before the financial statements are issued. They do not involve cash. And each one touches one income statement account and one balance sheet account.

## The Four Types of Adjustment

Every adjustment is either an accrual or a deferral. **Accruals** record revenue or expense before cash changes hands. **Deferrals** adjust amounts for which cash changed hands first.

### Accrued Revenue

Revenue has been earned, but cash has not been received and the customer may not yet have been billed. Kodiak Partners earns \$8,000 of consulting revenue in December and bills the client in January. On December 31, it records:

```journal
Dr. Accounts receivable          8,000
    Cr. Consulting revenue           8,000
```

### Accrued Expense

An expense has been incurred, but cash has not yet been paid. Panda Industries owes employees \$12,000 for the last week of the year and will pay them in January. On December 31, it records:

```journal
Dr. Wages expense               12,000
    Cr. Wages payable               12,000
```

### Deferred Revenue

Cash has already been received, but the revenue has not yet been earned. The receipt creates a liability, and the adjustment moves the earned portion to revenue. On October 1, Cub Entertainment collects \$36,000 for a 12-month subscription:

```journal
Oct 1
Dr. Cash                        36,000
    Cr. Unearned revenue            36,000
```

By December 31, three months have been earned (\$36,000 × 3/12):

```journal
Dec 31
Dr. Unearned revenue             9,000
    Cr. Subscription revenue         9,000
```

### Deferred Expense

Cash has already been paid, but part of what was bought is still unused at year-end. The payment creates an asset, and the adjustment moves the used portion to expense. On July 1, Bear Co. pays \$24,000 for one year of insurance coverage:

```journal
Jul 1
Dr. Prepaid insurance           24,000
    Cr. Cash                        24,000
```

By December 31, six months of coverage have expired (\$24,000 × 6/12):

```journal
Dec 31
Dr. Insurance expense           12,000
    Cr. Prepaid insurance           12,000
```

### Summary of Effects

| Situation at year-end | Balance sheet effect of the adjustment | Income statement effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earned but not yet collected | Asset increases (receivable) | Revenue increases |
| Incurred but not yet paid | Liability increases (payable) | Expense increases |
| Collected before earned | Liability decreases (unearned revenue) | Revenue increases |
| Paid before incurred | Asset decreases (prepaid) | Expense increases |

## Why Adjusting Entries Matter

Because each adjustment touches both statements, a missing adjusting entry misstates both. Return to Bear Co., which paid \$24,000 for annual insurance on July 1. If Bear Co. makes no adjusting entry on December 31, prepaid insurance is overstated by \$12,000 and insurance expense is understated by \$12,000, so pretax income is overstated by \$12,000.

When you work an adjustment problem, start by asking whether cash or performance came first. If performance came first, you are accruing a receivable or a payable. If cash came first, you are reducing an unearned revenue liability or a prepaid asset.

## Correcting Entries Are Different

An adjusting entry updates timing. A **correcting entry** fixes an error, such as a payment posted to the wrong account. Correcting entries may involve cash and may touch two balance sheet accounts or two income statement accounts; adjusting entries do not. Errors discovered after the statements are issued are corrected under the error correction rules covered in the accounting changes chapter.
