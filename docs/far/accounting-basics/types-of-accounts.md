# Types of Accounts

Every transaction a business makes, from selling a product to paying a utility bill, is recorded in accounts. To keep that data organized, accountants classify every account into one of ten types: asset, contra-asset, liability, contra-liability, equity, contra-equity, revenue, expense, gain, and loss. For the Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR) section of the CPA Exam, you need to know which type each line of a journal entry belongs to, because the type tells you the account's normal balance and which financial statement it appears on. This chapter walks through the balance sheet types first, then the income statement types, and ends with a table of normal balances.

---

## Balance Sheet Accounts

### Assets

**Assets** are resources a company controls that will bring it future economic benefits. (The conceptual framework's formal definition is "a present right of an entity to an economic benefit.") Common examples are **Cash**, **Accounts Receivable** (amounts customers owe the company), **Inventory** (goods held for sale), and **Equipment** (machinery, computers, and vehicles).

A cash sale increases an asset (Cash) and a revenue account:

```journal
Dr. Cash[a] 1,000
    Cr. Sales Revenue 1,000
```

### Contra-Assets

A **contra-asset** account has a credit balance, the opposite of an asset, and reduces the carrying amount of a related asset. **Accumulated Depreciation** holds the total depreciation recorded to date on an asset such as Equipment. The **Allowance for Credit Losses** (often called the allowance for doubtful accounts) holds the estimated amount of Accounts Receivable that the company does not expect to collect. Keeping these amounts in separate accounts lets the balance sheet show both the original amount and the reduction.

### Liabilities

**Liabilities** are a company's obligations to transfer resources to others. Examples include **Accounts Payable** (amounts owed to suppliers), **Notes Payable** (a formal written promise to pay, such as a bank loan), and **Wages Payable** (pay owed to employees for work they have already performed).

### Contra-Liabilities

A **contra-liability** account is less common. It has a debit balance and reduces the carrying amount of a related liability. The usual example is **Discount on Bonds Payable**, which arises when a company issues bonds for less than their face value. A discount on a note payable works the same way.

### Equity

**Equity** is the owners' residual claim on the company's assets after deducting its liabilities, often called the company's book value. In a corporation, **Common Stock** records the par (or stated) value of shares issued, **Additional Paid-in Capital** records amounts received above par, and **Retained Earnings** holds the cumulative net income kept in the business rather than paid out as dividends.

### Contra-Equity

A **contra-equity** account has a debit balance and reduces total equity. **Treasury Stock** (the company's own shares that it has reacquired and not retired) is the corporate example. In a sole proprietorship or partnership, **Owner's Withdrawals** (or Drawings) record cash or other assets an owner takes out for personal use.

## Income Statement Accounts

### Revenues

**Revenue** comes from a company's normal, central operations, such as selling goods or performing services. Examples include **Sales Revenue** and **Service Revenue** (consulting or repair fees, for example). **Interest Income** is revenue for a bank; most other companies report it as other income, outside operations.

### Expenses

An **expense** is a cost of carrying out those central operations. Examples include **Salaries Expense**, **Rent Expense**, **Utilities Expense**, and **Cost of Goods Sold** (the cost of the inventory that was sold).

### Gains and Losses

**Gains** and **losses** are increases and decreases in equity from peripheral or incidental transactions, events outside the company's primary business. The classic example is selling a used asset. If a company sells a delivery van with a book value of \$6,000 for \$8,000, it records a \$2,000 gain. If it sells the same van for \$5,000, it records a \$1,000 loss. Other examples include amounts received or paid to settle a lawsuit and impairment losses (write-downs of assets whose carrying amounts are no longer recoverable).

---

## Normal Balances at a Glance

Each account type has a **normal balance**: the side (debit or credit) that increases it.

| Account type | Normal balance | Financial statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset | Debit | Balance sheet |
| Contra-asset | Credit | Balance sheet (reduces assets) |
| Liability | Credit | Balance sheet |
| Contra-liability | Debit | Balance sheet (reduces liabilities) |
| Equity | Credit | Balance sheet |
| Contra-equity | Debit | Balance sheet (reduces equity) |
| Revenue | Credit | Income statement |
| Expense | Debit | Income statement |
| Gain | Credit | Income statement |
| Loss | Debit | Income statement |

:::tip[Remember DEALER]

**D**ividends (and drawings), **E**xpenses, and **A**ssets normally have debit balances. **L**iabilities, **E**quity, and **R**evenues normally have credit balances. Losses behave like expenses, and gains behave like revenues.

:::

This classification is the foundation for the rest of FAR. Once you know the type of every account in an entry, you know how each one increases and decreases and where it appears in the financial statements. That lets you reason through journal entries instead of memorizing them.
