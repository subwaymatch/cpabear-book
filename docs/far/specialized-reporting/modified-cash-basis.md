# Modified Cash Basis and Other Special Purpose Frameworks

Not every set of financial statements follows U.S. GAAP. A small business that answers to one bank and its owners may find full GAAP more expensive than it is worth, and a company whose lender only wants to see the numbers from its tax return can simply report on that basis. **Special purpose frameworks** are bases of accounting other than GAAP, described in AU-C Section 800 of the auditing standards: the **cash basis**, the **modified cash basis**, the **income tax basis**, **regulatory** bases, and **contractual** bases. They are often called by their older name, **other comprehensive bases of accounting (OCBOA)**. FAR tests four skills here: choosing the right statement titles, preparing cash and modified cash basis statements, converting cash basis amounts to the accrual basis, and preparing income tax basis statements. The chapter takes them in that order.

## Presentation Rules and Statement Titles

Special purpose framework statements must not look like GAAP statements. Their titles make the basis clear, and the notes explain it.

| Topic | Guideline |
| --- | --- |
| Statement titles | Use titles that distinguish the statements from GAAP statements |
| Required statements | Present the equivalent of a balance sheet and an income statement |
| Equity changes | Explain changes in owners' equity accounts, in a separate statement or in the notes |
| Statement of cash flows | Not required |
| Disclosures | Describe the basis of accounting and how it differs from GAAP (the effects need not be quantified); for items similar to GAAP items, give similar disclosures |

Unmodified titles such as "balance sheet," "income statement," and "statement of cash flows" suggest GAAP, so they are avoided. Typical titles are:

| Framework | Balance Sheet Equivalent | Income Statement Equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Cash basis | Statement of assets and liabilities arising from cash transactions | Statement of cash receipts and disbursements |
| Modified cash basis | Statement of assets, liabilities, and equity (modified cash basis) | Statement of revenues and expenses (modified cash basis), or statement of revenues collected and expenses paid |
| Income tax basis | Statement of assets, liabilities, and equity (income tax basis) | Statement of revenues and expenses (income tax basis) |
| Regulatory basis (for example, an insurance company) | Statement of admitted assets, liabilities, and surplus (statutory basis) | Statement of income (statutory basis) |

## Cash Basis and Modified Cash Basis

Under the **pure cash basis**, revenue is recorded when cash is received and expenses when cash is paid. The statements are simple and objective, but they can badly distort performance. A company that buys a \$50,000 truck reports a \$50,000 expense that year and none in the four years it uses the truck.

The **modified cash basis** fixes the worst of those distortions. It starts with cash-basis accounting and adds selected accrual-basis treatments. Each modification must have **substantial support**, which generally means it applies the GAAP treatment to that item. The most common modifications are:

- **Capitalizing long-lived assets** and depreciating them, instead of expensing the purchase
- **Recording borrowings as liabilities**, so that loan proceeds are not revenue and principal payments are not expenses
- **Recording inventory** as an asset and expensing it as cost of goods sold
- **Accruing income taxes**, and sometimes interest or payroll
- **Recognizing significant prepaids and deferred revenue**

Entities do not all choose the same modifications. The framework is more flexible than GAAP, but each entity must apply its choices consistently and describe them in the notes.

### Example: Preparing Modified Cash Basis Statements

Kodiak Partners, a consulting firm, prepares modified cash basis statements. It began the year with \$20,000 of cash and \$20,000 of partners' capital. During the year:

- Cash collected from clients was \$240,000.
- Cash paid for salaries and rent was \$150,000.
- On January 1, the firm bought equipment for \$30,000 cash. Under its modified cash basis, it capitalizes the equipment and depreciates it straight-line over 5 years (\$6,000 per year).
- On July 1, it borrowed \$50,000 on a long-term note, recorded as a liability. No interest has been paid, and the firm does not accrue interest.
- The partners made no withdrawals.

**Kodiak Partners: Statement of Revenues and Expenses (Modified Cash Basis)**

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenues collected | \$240,000 |
| Salaries and rent paid | (150,000) |
| Depreciation | (6,000) |
| **Excess of revenues over expenses** | **\$84,000** |

**Kodiak Partners: Statement of Assets, Liabilities, and Partners' Capital (Modified Cash Basis)**

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash (\$20,000 + \$240,000 - \$150,000 - \$30,000 + \$50,000) | \$130,000 |
| Equipment, net of \$6,000 accumulated depreciation | 24,000 |
| **Total assets** | **\$154,000** |
| Note payable | \$50,000 |
| Partners' capital (\$20,000 + \$84,000) | 104,000 |
| **Total liabilities and partners' capital** | **\$154,000** |

Under the pure cash basis, the \$30,000 equipment purchase would have been an expense, and the loan would not appear at all. The modifications make the statements more useful without requiring full accrual accounting.

## Converting Cash Basis to Accrual Basis

Exam questions often give cash receipts and payments plus beginning and ending balances, and ask for accrual-basis revenue or expense. The logic follows the T-accounts. Revenue earned this year is the cash collected, adjusted for the change in what customers owe and for the change in what was collected in advance. Expenses incurred are the cash paid, adjusted for the change in what is still owed and for the change in what was paid in advance.

$$
\text{Accrual Revenue} = \text{Cash Collected} + \text{Ending A/R} - \text{Beginning A/R} + \text{Beginning Unearned Revenue} - \text{Ending Unearned Revenue}
$$

$$
\text{Accrual Expense} = \text{Cash Paid} + \text{Ending Accrued Liabilities} - \text{Beginning Accrued Liabilities} + \text{Beginning Prepaids} - \text{Ending Prepaids}
$$

The signs are easy to rebuild. An increase in receivables means revenue was earned but not yet collected, so it is added. An increase in unearned revenue means cash was collected but not yet earned, so it is subtracted. Expenses work the same way with payables and prepaids.

Continuing the Kodiak Partners example, assume these balances:

| Account | Beginning | Ending |
|---|---:|---:|
| Accounts receivable | \$15,000 | \$25,000 |
| Unearned consulting fees | \$8,000 | \$5,000 |
| Salaries payable | \$4,000 | \$6,000 |
| Prepaid rent | \$3,000 | \$2,000 |

$$
\text{Accrual Revenue} = \$240{,}000 + \$25{,}000 - \$15{,}000 + \$8{,}000 - \$5{,}000 = \$253{,}000
$$

$$
\text{Accrual Salaries and Rent} = \$150{,}000 + \$6{,}000 - \$4{,}000 + \$3{,}000 - \$2{,}000 = \$153{,}000
$$

On the accrual basis, Kodiak would also accrue interest on the note. If the note carries 6% interest, six months of interest (\$50,000 × 6% × 6/12 = \$1,500) would be added to expenses, with a matching interest payable.

## Income Tax Basis

**Income tax basis** statements use the same methods the entity uses to file its federal income tax return, either the **cash method** or the **accrual method** of tax accounting. Owners and lenders of closely held businesses often prefer them because they are cheap to prepare and tie directly to the return.

Several items differ from GAAP, and the differences are the usual exam questions:

| Item | GAAP | Income Tax Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation | Over the asset's useful life | Tax depreciation methods and lives (for example, MACRS or bonus depreciation) |
| Credit losses | Allowance based on expected credit losses | Deducted when specific accounts are written off |
| Warranty costs | Accrued when the product is sold | Deducted when paid |
| Deferred income taxes | Recognized for temporary differences | **None**; there are no book-tax differences to defer |
| Nontaxable revenue and nondeductible expenses (municipal bond interest, fines) | Ordinary revenue and expense | Still reported, so the statements reflect all transactions, often shown separately or disclosed |

Panda Industries files its tax return on the accrual method and prepares income tax basis statements. Its GAAP pretax income would be \$300,000. GAAP depreciation is \$40,000, while tax depreciation is \$70,000. Its GAAP credit loss expense is \$12,000, while accounts actually written off total \$5,000.

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| GAAP pretax income | \$300,000 |
| Additional tax depreciation (\$70,000 - \$40,000) | (30,000) |
| Credit loss expense not deductible until write-off (\$12,000 - \$5,000) | 7,000 |
| **Excess of revenues over expenses before income taxes (income tax basis)** | **\$277,000** |

The income statement equivalent would be titled "Statement of Revenues and Expenses (Income Tax Basis)," and the notes would describe the income tax basis and how it differs from GAAP.
