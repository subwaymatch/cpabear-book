# Modified Cash Basis and Other Special Purpose Frameworks

Special purpose frameworks are bases of accounting other than generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). They are often called by their older name, **other comprehensive bases of accounting (OCBOA)**. Common examples are the cash basis, the modified cash basis, the income tax basis, and regulatory bases.

The key point for FAR: these frameworks are **not U.S. GAAP**, but they still produce a coherent set of financial statements.

## Big Picture

A modified cash basis starts with cash-basis accounting and adds selected accrual-basis features. Smaller private businesses use it when they want more informative reporting than the pure cash basis without the cost of full GAAP.

:::info[Core exam point]

When a question asks about modified cash basis presentation, focus on **statement titles**, **minimum required statements**, and **which accrual-type adjustments are commonly added**.

:::

## General OCBOA Presentation Guidelines

| Topic | General guideline |
| --- | --- |
| Statement titles | Use titles that distinguish the statements from GAAP statements |
| Required statements | Present the equivalent of a balance sheet and an income statement |
| Equity changes | Explain changes in owners' equity accounts |
| Statement of cash flows | Not required |
| Disclosures | Describe the basis of accounting and how it differs from GAAP (the effects need not be quantified); for items similar to GAAP items, give similar disclosures |

### Title examples

- Statement of assets, liabilities, and equity (income tax basis)
- Statement of revenue collected and expenses paid (modified cash basis)

The title must make clear that the statements are **not** prepared in conformity with U.S. GAAP. Unmodified titles such as "balance sheet" or "income statement" suggest GAAP, so avoid them.

## Common Modified Cash Basis Adjustments

A modification should have substantial support, which generally means it applies the GAAP treatment to that item. The most common modifications follow.

### 1. Capitalize long-lived assets

Instead of expensing a major equipment purchase immediately, the entity capitalizes the equipment and depreciates it.

```journal
Dr. Equipment                     50,000
    Cr. Cash                          50,000
```

At year-end, record depreciation:

```journal
Dr. Depreciation expense           5,000
    Cr. Accumulated depreciation       5,000
```

### 2. Record long-term debt

The loan is recorded as a liability. Later principal payments reduce the liability instead of being expensed.

```journal
Dr. Cash                         100,000
    Cr. Notes payable               100,000
```

### 3. Record selected accruals

Some entities add limited accruals, especially for interest, payroll, or income taxes.

```journal
Dr. Interest expense              2,000
    Cr. Interest payable             2,000
```

### 4. Maintain inventory and cost of goods sold

Record inventory purchases as an asset:

```journal
Dr. Inventory                    15,000
    Cr. Cash                         15,000
```

At period-end, expense the inventory sold:

```journal
Dr. Cost of goods sold           10,000
    Cr. Inventory                    10,000
```

### 5. Recognize prepaids and deferred revenue when significant

```journal
Dr. Prepaid insurance             3,000
    Cr. Cash                          3,000
```

```journal
Dr. Cash                          5,000
    Cr. Unearned revenue             5,000
```

## Example: Bear Co. on a Modified Cash Basis

Bear Co. prepares modified cash basis statements for its owners. During the year it:

- buys equipment for \$50,000 and depreciates it
- borrows \$100,000 on a long-term note
- accrues \$2,000 of unpaid interest
- records inventory purchases and cost of goods sold

These modifications make the statements more useful than pure cash-basis statements. They show the long-lived assets, the debt, and the inventory actually consumed during the year.

## How Modified Cash Basis Differs from GAAP

| Issue | Modified cash basis | GAAP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue timing | Often close to cash collection | Accrual basis under Accounting Standards Codification (ASC) 606 |
| Expense timing | Often close to cash payment, with selected adjustments | Full accrual and matching concepts |
| Statement of cash flows | Usually not required | Required |
| Disclosure depth | More limited | Full GAAP disclosures |

:::warning

Do not assume every modified cash basis entity records the same set of adjustments. The framework is more flexible than GAAP, but the presentation still needs to be internally consistent and clearly labeled.

:::

## FAR Takeaways

:::note[Checklist]

- Title OCBOA statements differently from GAAP statements.
- Present the equivalent of a balance sheet and an income statement.
- A statement of cash flows is not required.
- Disclosures must still explain the basis of accounting and help users understand significant items.

:::
