# Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position

The **balance sheet** (also called the **statement of financial position**) reports an entity's **assets**, **liabilities**, and **stockholders' equity** at a specific **point in time**. It is the only primary financial statement that shows a snapshot rather than activity over a period, and it answers a simple question: what does the company own, what does it owe, and what is left for the owners right now?

$$
\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Stockholders' Equity}
$$

Lenders and investors read the balance sheet mainly to judge liquidity and solvency, so the most important skill it tests is **classification**: is an item current or noncurrent, and where in the statement does it belong? FAR simulations often give you a trial balance and ask you to prepare a classified balance sheet or to find the errors in one. This chapter walks through the sections of a classified balance sheet in order (current assets, noncurrent assets, current and noncurrent liabilities, and equity), then covers working capital, formats, and disclosures, and ends with a practice problem.

---

## Classified and Unclassified Balance Sheets

A **classified** balance sheet separates assets and liabilities into **current** and **noncurrent** groups. Most commercial and industrial companies use it, and it is the format the exam tests almost exclusively. An **unclassified** balance sheet lists assets and liabilities without that split, usually in order of liquidity. Banks and other financial institutions often use it, because for them the current/noncurrent line is less meaningful.

The dividing line is **one year from the balance sheet date or the operating cycle, whichever is longer**. The **operating cycle** is the time it takes to turn cash into inventory, inventory into receivables, and receivables back into cash. For most companies it is shorter than a year, so the one-year test applies. A company with a long cycle, such as a winery aging its product, uses the longer cycle.

## Assets

An asset is "a present right of an entity to an economic benefit" (Concepts Statement No. 8, Chapter 4).

### Current Assets

Current assets are expected to be **converted to cash, sold, or consumed within one year** (or the operating cycle, if longer). They are presented in order of **liquidity**:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Cash and cash equivalents** | Bank balances, money market funds, Treasury bills with an original maturity of three months or less |
| **Short-term investments** | Trading securities, available-for-sale (AFS) debt securities expected to be sold within one year |
| **Accounts receivable** | Trade receivables, net of the allowance for credit losses |
| **Inventories** | Raw materials, work in process, finished goods |
| **Prepaid expenses** | Prepaid insurance, prepaid rent |
| **Other current assets** | Current portion of notes receivable, income tax receivable |

Bear Co.'s current assets section looks like this:

| Bear Co. Current Assets | Dec. 31 |
|---|---:|
| Cash and cash equivalents | \$85,000 |
| Short-term investments | 40,000 |
| Accounts receivable, net of \$12,000 allowance | 188,000 |
| Inventories | 220,000 |
| Prepaid expenses | 15,000 |
| **Total current assets** | **\$548,000** |

Cash that is restricted for a long-term purpose is **not** a current asset. A **sinking fund** set aside to retire bonds in five years is a noncurrent investment, even though it holds cash.

### Noncurrent Assets

**Investments and funds** include equity method investments, held-to-maturity debt securities, AFS debt securities not expected to be sold within one year, sinking funds, and the cash surrender value of life insurance. Whether an AFS security is current depends on management's intent to sell it within a year, not on its maturity date.

**Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E)** is reported at cost less accumulated depreciation. Land is not depreciated. Companies usually present PP&E by major class, with the accumulated depreciation shown on the face of the statement or in the notes:

| Item           |            Cost |    Accum. Depr. |             Net |
| -------------- | --------------: | --------------: | --------------: |
| Land           |       \$200,000 |             N/A |       \$200,000 |
| Buildings      |         800,000 |       (240,000) |         560,000 |
| Equipment      |         350,000 |       (105,000) |         245,000 |
| **Total PP&E** | **\$1,350,000** | **(\$345,000)** | **\$1,005,000** |

**Intangible assets** lack physical substance but provide future benefits. Finite-lived intangibles, such as patents, copyrights, and customer lists, are amortized and reported net of accumulated amortization. Indefinite-lived intangibles, such as a trademark the company expects to keep renewing, are not amortized but are tested for impairment at least annually. **Goodwill** is reported separately. Public business entities do not amortize it; private companies and not-for-profit entities may elect to amortize it over 10 years or less.

**Other noncurrent assets** include deferred tax assets (which are always classified as noncurrent), long-term prepayments, and operating lease right-of-use assets.

## Liabilities

A liability is "a present obligation of an entity to transfer an economic benefit" (Concepts Statement No. 8, Chapter 4).

### Current Liabilities

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled within **one year** (or the operating cycle, if longer), usually with current assets:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Accounts payable** | Trade payables to suppliers |
| **Accrued liabilities** | Wages payable, interest payable, taxes payable |
| **Unearned revenue** | Customer deposits, gift card liabilities |
| **Short-term notes payable** | Bank lines of credit, commercial paper |
| **Current portion of long-term debt** | Principal payments due within one year |
| **Dividends payable** | Declared but unpaid cash dividends |

A declared but unpaid cash dividend is a current liability, because the declaration creates the obligation. (A declared stock dividend is not a liability; it stays in equity.)

Two classification rules for debt come up often.

**Short-term debt expected to be refinanced.** A short-term obligation is classified as **current** unless the entity intends to refinance it on a long-term basis **and** demonstrates the ability to do so before the financial statements are issued (or available to be issued). Under ASC 470-10, it demonstrates that ability either by actually refinancing the obligation on a long-term basis after the balance sheet date, or by entering into a qualifying financing agreement that clearly permits long-term refinancing.

**Debt covenant violations.** Long-term debt becomes **current** if the borrower has violated a covenant at the balance sheet date and the violation makes the debt callable by the lender. It stays noncurrent only if the lender waives the right to demand repayment for more than one year from the balance sheet date, or if the agreement gives a grace period and it is probable that the violation will be cured within it.

### Noncurrent Liabilities

Noncurrent liabilities are obligations not due within the next year (or operating cycle, if longer):

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Long-term notes and bonds payable** | Bonds payable (net of discount or premium and debt issuance costs), mortgage notes |
| **Lease liabilities** | Noncurrent portion of finance and operating lease liabilities |
| **Deferred tax liabilities** | Temporary differences that create future taxable amounts (always noncurrent) |
| **Pension and postretirement obligations** | Net defined benefit liability |

The portion of long-term debt or a lease liability that is due within the next year is reclassified to current liabilities.

## Stockholders' Equity

Stockholders' equity is the residual interest in assets after deducting liabilities. The equity section typically includes:

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Preferred stock** | Par value of preferred shares issued |
| **Common stock** | Par value of shares issued |
| **Additional paid-in capital (APIC)** | Amounts received above par value on stock issuances |
| **Retained earnings** | Cumulative net income less cumulative dividends |
| **Accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)** | Cumulative other comprehensive income (OCI) items: pension adjustments, unrealized gains and losses on AFS debt securities and cash flow hedges, foreign currency translation, and instrument-specific credit risk |
| **Treasury stock** | Cost of shares reacquired by the entity, deducted from equity |
| **Noncontrolling interest** | Equity in consolidated subsidiaries not owned by the parent, shown separately from the parent's equity |

| Grizzly Inc. Stockholders' Equity | Dec. 31 |
|---|---:|
| Preferred stock, \$100 par, 1,000 shares issued | \$100,000 |
| Common stock, \$1 par, 50,000 shares issued | 50,000 |
| Additional paid-in capital | 600,000 |
| Retained earnings | 425,000 |
| Accumulated other comprehensive income | 18,000 |
| Less: Treasury stock (2,000 shares at cost) | (30,000) |
| **Total stockholders' equity** | **\$1,163,000** |

Treasury stock is subtracted, not added, and the number of shares authorized, issued, and outstanding is disclosed for each class of stock.

## Working Capital and the Current Ratio

**Working capital** is current assets minus current liabilities, and the **current ratio** expresses the same relationship as a ratio. Both measure short-term liquidity, which is why classification errors on the balance sheet matter: moving a liability from noncurrent to current lowers both measures and can push a borrower out of compliance with a loan covenant.

$$
\text{Working capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities} \qquad \text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
$$

Bear Co. has current assets of \$548,000 and current liabilities of \$310,000. Its working capital is \$238,000 (\$548,000 - \$310,000), and its current ratio is about 1.77 (\$548,000 ÷ \$310,000). A current ratio above 1.0 means current assets exceed current liabilities. The financial ratio analysis chapter covers the other liquidity ratios.

## Format and Disclosures

In the **account format**, assets appear on the left and liabilities and equity on the right, mirroring the accounting equation. In the **report format**, the most common in practice, assets come first, followed by liabilities and then equity, from top to bottom.

Some details are shown **parenthetically** on the face of the balance sheet, such as the allowance for credit losses, accumulated depreciation, the par value of stock, and the number of shares authorized, issued, and outstanding. Much more appears in the notes: accounting policies (for inventory, depreciation, and revenue), contingencies and guarantees, debt and lease maturities, fair value information, related party transactions, and subsequent events. The notes chapter covers these.

## Practice Problem

Sloth Security reports the following balances at December 31:

| Account | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | \$60,000 |
| Accounts receivable | 150,000 |
| Allowance for credit losses | (8,000) |
| Inventory | 200,000 |
| Prepaid insurance | 12,000 |
| Land | 180,000 |
| Building | 500,000 |
| Accumulated depreciation (building) | (100,000) |
| Equipment | 220,000 |
| Accumulated depreciation (equipment) | (55,000) |
| Goodwill | 90,000 |
| Accounts payable | 85,000 |
| Wages payable | 22,000 |
| Bonds payable (due in 5 years) | 400,000 |
| Common stock (\$1 par) | 40,000 |
| APIC | 360,000 |
| Retained earnings | 380,000 |
| AOCI | 12,000 |
| Treasury stock | (50,000) |

**Required:** (a) Calculate total current assets. (b) Calculate working capital. (c) Calculate total stockholders' equity.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a) Total current assets:**

\$60,000 + \$150,000 - \$8,000 + \$200,000 + \$12,000 = **\$414,000**

**(b) Working capital:**

Current liabilities = \$85,000 + \$22,000 = \$107,000

Working capital = \$414,000 - \$107,000 = **\$307,000**

**(c) Total stockholders' equity:**

\$40,000 + \$360,000 + \$380,000 + \$12,000 - \$50,000 = **\$742,000**

**Check that the balance sheet balances:**

- Total assets = \$414,000 current assets + \$180,000 land + \$400,000 building (net) + \$165,000 equipment (net) + \$90,000 goodwill = **\$1,249,000**
- Total liabilities and equity = \$107,000 current liabilities + \$400,000 bonds payable + \$742,000 equity = **\$1,249,000**

If the bonds were instead due in eight months, they would move to current liabilities, and working capital would become a deficit of \$93,000 (\$414,000 - \$507,000).

</details>
