# Financial Reporting Foundations

This chapter connects the FAR overview to the detailed conceptual framework. Before you memorize rules, understand three things: **what financial reporting is trying to communicate**, **which statements make up a complete set**, and **why exam questions focus on classification and presentation**.

:::info[Why this matters]

Many Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR) questions are not really about math. They test whether you can identify the right statement, the right element, and the right place to report an item under U.S. generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP).

:::

## The Goal of General-Purpose Financial Reporting

General-purpose financial reporting helps investors, lenders, and other creditors decide whether to provide resources to an entity. The statements should help these users evaluate:

- liquidity and solvency
- profitability and cash generation
- management's stewardship of the entity's resources
- the timing and uncertainty of future cash flows

For example, Bear Co.'s bank may focus on current assets and current liabilities before renewing a line of credit. Kodiak Partners might focus on earnings trends and debt service capacity before making an investment.

## A Complete Set of Financial Statements

A complete set of GAAP financial statements includes the five statements below, plus the notes:

| Statement | Main question answered |
| --- | --- |
| Statement of financial position (balance sheet) | What does the entity own and owe at the reporting date? |
| Income statement | How much did the entity earn (net income) during the period? |
| Statement of comprehensive income | Which nonowner changes in equity bypassed net income? |
| Statement of cash flows | Where did cash come from, and where did it go? |
| Statement of changes in stockholders' equity | How did each equity account change during the period? |

### How Candidates Use Them

- Use the **balance sheet** for classification questions.
- Use the **income statement** for revenue, expense, gain, and loss presentation.
- Use the **statement of comprehensive income** for other comprehensive income (OCI) and accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI) topics.
- Use the **statement of cash flows** to separate operating, investing, and financing effects.
- Use the **statement of changes in stockholders' equity** for stock issuances, dividends, AOCI rollforwards, and treasury stock.

## The Financial Statements Work Together

The statements are connected, so a single transaction often appears in more than one of them.

**Example:** Sloth Security collects \$24,000 in advance for six months of security monitoring services.

```journal
Dr. Cash                         24,000
    Cr. Unearned revenue             24,000
```

That entry affects:

- the **balance sheet** immediately, through cash and a liability
- the **income statement** later, as the revenue is earned (\$4,000 per month)
- the **statement of cash flows** immediately, as an operating cash inflow

:::tip[Study habit]

When you practice FAR, ask two questions for every transaction: **What is the journal entry?** and **Which statements change right now?**

:::

## Core Presentation Ideas Tested in FAR

### 1. Classification Matters

The CPA Exam often tests where an item belongs rather than whether it exists at all. For example:

- a short-term investment may be a **cash equivalent** or a **marketable security**, depending on its maturity
- a liability may be **current** or **noncurrent**, depending on when it will be settled
- a gain may belong in **net income** or **OCI**, depending on the topic

### 2. Accrual Accounting Drives the Statements

Under accrual accounting, entities recognize economic events when they occur, not when cash changes hands. That is why deferred revenue, accrued expenses, prepaid items, and receivables appear so often in FAR.

### 3. Disclosure Is Part of Reporting

The notes are part of the financial statements, and some information appears only there. For example, if Polar Co. has a significant concentration with one customer or uses the last-in, first-out (LIFO) method for inventory, that information may appear in the notes rather than on the face of the statements.

## Common Sources of FAR Mistakes

:::warning[Common pitfalls]

- Confusing **net income** with **comprehensive income**
- Forgetting that some items belong in the **notes** rather than on the face of the statements
- Mixing up **owner transactions** with **nonowner changes in equity**
- Treating every cash receipt as revenue or every cash payment as an expense

:::

## A Short Illustration

Panda Industries reports the following for the year:

- net income of \$180,000
- an unrealized gain on available-for-sale (AFS) debt securities of \$12,000, net of tax
- a cash dividend declaration of \$40,000

Each item affects equity in a different way:

| Item | Statement effect |
| --- | --- |
| Net income | Increases retained earnings through the income statement |
| Unrealized gain on AFS debt securities | Increases OCI and AOCI, not net income |
| Cash dividend | Reduces retained earnings as a distribution to owners |

Comprehensive income for the year is \$192,000 (\$180,000 + \$12,000). The dividend is not part of comprehensive income because it is a transaction with owners. The conceptual framework provides the logic behind this kind of classification.
