# Financial Reporting Foundations

Before you memorize a single rule, it helps to know what financial statements are for and how they fit together. Many Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR) questions are not really about math. They test whether you can pick the right statement, the right element, and the right place to report an item under U.S. generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). This chapter covers the goal of general-purpose reporting, the statements that make up a complete set, how one transaction flows through several of them, and the presentation ideas that come up again and again on the exam.

## The Goal of General-Purpose Financial Reporting

General-purpose financial reporting helps investors, lenders, and other creditors decide whether to provide resources to an entity. To make that decision, users want to judge the entity's liquidity and solvency, its profitability and ability to generate cash, the timing and uncertainty of its future cash flows, and how well management has looked after the entity's resources.

Different users weigh these questions differently. Bear Co.'s bank may focus on current assets and current liabilities before renewing a line of credit. Kodiak Partners, thinking about an equity investment, might care more about earnings trends and the company's capacity to service its debt.

## A Complete Set of Financial Statements

A complete set of GAAP financial statements includes the five statements below, plus the notes:

| Statement | Main question answered |
| --- | --- |
| Statement of financial position (balance sheet) | What does the entity own and owe at the reporting date? |
| Income statement | How much did the entity earn (net income) during the period? |
| Statement of comprehensive income | Which nonowner changes in equity bypassed net income? |
| Statement of cash flows | Where did cash come from, and where did it go? |
| Statement of changes in stockholders' equity | How did each equity account change during the period? |

Each statement answers a different kind of exam question. Classification questions (current or noncurrent, asset or expense) usually come back to the balance sheet. Questions about revenues, expenses, gains, and losses belong to the income statement, while other comprehensive income (OCI) and accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI) belong to the statement of comprehensive income. The statement of cash flows sorts activity into operating, investing, and financing, and the statement of changes in stockholders' equity tracks stock issuances, dividends, treasury stock, and the AOCI rollforward.

## The Financial Statements Work Together

Because the statements are connected, a single transaction often appears in more than one of them. Suppose Sloth Security collects \$24,000 in advance for six months of security monitoring:

```journal
Dr. Cash                         24,000
    Cr. Unearned revenue             24,000
```

The balance sheet changes immediately: cash goes up, and so does a liability for services Sloth still owes. The statement of cash flows also shows the \$24,000 right away, as an operating inflow. The income statement is affected only later, as Sloth earns the revenue at \$4,000 per month.

A good study habit follows from this. For every transaction you practice, ask two questions: what is the journal entry, and which statements change right now?

## Core Presentation Ideas Tested in FAR

**Classification matters.** The exam often tests where an item belongs rather than whether it exists. A short-term investment may be a cash equivalent or a marketable security, depending on its maturity. A liability may be current or noncurrent, depending on when it will be settled. A gain may belong in net income or in OCI, depending on the topic.

**Accrual accounting drives the statements.** Under accrual accounting, an entity recognizes economic events when they occur, not when cash changes hands. That is why deferred revenue, accrued expenses, prepaid items, and receivables appear so often in FAR. It is also why a common mistake is treating every cash receipt as revenue and every cash payment as an expense.

**Disclosure is part of reporting.** The notes are part of the financial statements, and some information appears only there. If Polar Co. depends heavily on one customer, or uses the last-in, first-out (LIFO) method for inventory, a reader learns that from the notes rather than from the face of the statements.

## A Short Illustration

Two other common mistakes are confusing net income with comprehensive income and mixing up transactions with owners with other changes in equity. One example shows the difference. Panda Industries reports the following for the year:

- net income of \$180,000
- an unrealized gain on available-for-sale (AFS) debt securities of \$12,000, net of tax
- a cash dividend declaration of \$40,000

Each item affects equity in a different way:

| Item | Statement effect |
| --- | --- |
| Net income | Increases retained earnings through the income statement |
| Unrealized gain on AFS debt securities | Increases OCI and AOCI, not net income |
| Cash dividend | Reduces retained earnings as a distribution to owners |

Comprehensive income for the year is \$192,000 (\$180,000 + \$12,000). The dividend is not part of comprehensive income because it is a transaction with owners.
